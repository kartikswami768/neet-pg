# 07 — Burns: Assessment, Resuscitation and Management

> [!info] Primary sources
> [WOR—Surgery: Trauma and Burns pp. 173–178]; [Doctut Merged—BURNS, fluid resuscitation, topical agents, chemical/electrical burns]; [Robbins—Environmental Injury, Tissue Repair, Shock]; [First Aid 2025—Skin and Environmental Injuries]; [Combined Bootcamp—Shock and Connective Tissue]. Numeric formulae represent **the specific uploaded teaching sources** and are initial estimates, not fixed clinical fluid orders.

## 1. Etiology and injury mechanism

- **Thermal:** scald, flame, contact; common cause of superficial/partial and deep burns.
- **Chemical:** alkalis, acids, specific agents such as **hydrofluoric acid**.
- **Electrical:** current traverses tissue, often causing deeper injury than surface suggests.
- **Inhalation:** thermal injury above glottis, irritant-mediated lower-airway injury and systemic toxic gas exposure.
- **Radiation/friction/cold injuries:** separate mechanisms; severe cold injury includes frostbite (discussed below).

### Pathophysiology of a major burn
1. Thermal/chemical injury denatures proteins, disrupts membranes and damages microcirculation.
2. Endothelial injury/inflammatory mediator release → **capillary permeability ↑** → intravascular plasma loss + interstitial edema.
3. **Hypovolemia, hemoconcentration and reduced perfusion** can produce burn shock.
4. Catecholamines, cortisol and cytokines drive **hypermetabolism, insulin resistance and protein catabolism**.
5. Loss of skin barrier plus devitalized tissue predisposes to infection/sepsis.

### Jackson zones of burn injury

| Zone | Location/meaning | Viability |
|---|---|---|
| **Coagulation** | Central maximal thermal injury/protein denaturation | **Irreversible necrosis** |
| **Stasis** | Surrounds coagulation, reduced perfusion | **Potentially salvageable**; hypotension, edema and infection can convert to necrosis |
| **Hyperemia** | Peripheral inflammation/vasodilation | Usually recoverable |

**Exam principle:** early effective perfusion and avoidance of secondary infection/pressure support preservation of the **zone of stasis**.

## 2. Assessment — extent × depth × site × physiology

### Primary survey in burns
- **A: Airway.** Consider rapid progressive edema in facial/neck burns, inhalational injury and enclosed-space fires.
- **B: Breathing.** Check oxygenation/ventilation, carbon monoxide exposure, circumferential chest eschar.
- **C: Circulation.** Evaluate shock, IV/IO access, estimated burn size, bleeding/other trauma and urine output.
- **D: Disability.** GCS/glucose, hypoxia/toxin-related altered mentation and associated injuries.
- **E: Exposure.** Remove hot/wet/contaminated items and constricting jewelry; inspect entire body but **avoid hypothermia**.

### Red flags for inhalation injury
- **Closed-room fire**, facial/neck burn, soot in mouth/sputum, hoarseness, stridor, respiratory distress, oral burns and altered consciousness.
- **Singed nasal hair is a clue but not sufficient alone** to diagnose significant lower-airway injury.
- Airway edema can progress: urgent expert airway assessment and **early intubation when clinically indicated** may prevent a later impossible airway.

## 3. Depth classification

| Depth | Tissue involved | Appearance | Sensation/blanch | Healing and scar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Superficial (first degree)** | Epidermis only | Red, dry; no true blisters | **Painful**, blanches | Heals in days, typically without scar |
| **Superficial partial thickness** | Epidermis + superficial dermis | Moist pink/red, blistering | **Very painful**, brisk blanch | Usually epithelializes with little scarring if uncomplicated |
| **Deep partial thickness** | Deep dermis | Pale/mottled, less moist | Reduced pinprick sensation, poor blanch | Slow healing, hypertrophic scar/contracture risk; may need excision/grafting |
| **Full thickness (third degree)** | Entire epidermis and dermis | Dry leathery, white/brown/black **eschar** | Often **insensate** in burned center, absent blanch | Requires replacement of skin, commonly excision and grafting |
| **Fourth degree** | Extends into fat/muscle/tendon/bone | Charred, extensive deep damage | Insensate tissue | Complex reconstruction, possible amputation |

- The older notes sometimes place subcutaneous tissue exclusively within “third degree”; **the modern useful distinction is complete dermal destruction in full-thickness burns**, with fourth degree extending to deeper structures.
- **First-degree/superficial epidermal burns are excluded from TBSA calculations used for burn-resuscitation formulae.**
- **Blister ≠ always superficial:** deep partial thickness can blister but show diminished sensation/blanching.
- Pain is not a reliable marker of overall severity: a painless full-thickness center may be surrounded by painful partial-thickness burns.

## 4. Estimating total body surface area (TBSA)

### Wallace Rule of Nines — adult

| Body region | % of total surface |
|---|---:|
| Head and neck | **9%** |
| Each upper limb | **9%** |
| Anterior trunk | **18%** |
| Posterior trunk | **18%** |
| Each lower limb | **18%** |
| Perineum/genitals | **1%** |
| **Total** | **100%** |

### Other methods
- **Lund–Browder chart:** **most accurate practical chart for children**; adjusts proportionally larger head and smaller lower limbs with age.
- **Patient palmar surface including fingers:** approximates **~1% TBSA** in conventional bedside estimates; useful for scattered small burns.
- Use drawings or a burn chart to record **superficial partial, deep partial and full-thickness regions separately**; re-evaluate burn depth as tissue declares itself over time.

### Worked examples
1. Adult with full anterior trunk and entire right arm partial-thickness burn: **18 + 9 = 27% TBSA**.
2. Small discrete lesions covering approximately three patient handprints: **about 3%** (rough estimate).

## 5. Initial local and systemic management

1. **Stop burning:** remove heat exposure, electrical source or chemical agent without endangering rescuers.
2. **Cool thermal burns promptly using cool running water**, not ice; large surface-area cooling must not induce systemic hypothermia.
3. **Remove constrictions** (rings, watches, clothing if not firmly adherent) before edema develops.
4. **ABCDE**, supplemental oxygen/airway support when indicated, vascular access, blood sampling for severe injury.
5. **Estimate TBSA and depth**; examine associated trauma, circumferential injury and inhalation features.
6. Provide effective analgesia, protective clean dressings and **tetanus prophylaxis assessment**.
7. Significant burns: fluid resuscitation, accurate monitoring including urine output, thermoregulation and transfer to a specialist burns unit when indicated.

**Blisters:** management varies with size, site, contamination and dressing choice. Do not automatically rupture all blisters; ruptured/contaminated blisters may need debridement under controlled care.

## 6. Fluid resuscitation: formulas and endpoints

### Formulae in the uploaded surgical sources

| Regimen | Initial crystalloid estimate for first 24 h | Comments |
|---|---|---|
| **Classic Parkland** | `4 mL × kg × %TBSA` | Historical teaching formula; may overestimate initial needs in some adults |
| **Modified Brooke/adult source table** | `2 mL LR × kg × %TBSA` | WOR/Doctut cite for adults/older children, **≥14 y** in source |
| **Pediatric source table** | `3 mL LR × kg × %TBSA` | Add appropriate **maintenance glucose-containing fluid** for young children |
| **Electrical injury source table** | `4 mL LR × kg × %TBSA` initially | More important to titrate to pigment injury/perfusion and urine output |

**Remember:** this is the **burn resuscitation component**, not an exact fixed target. Fluid requirement depends on age, injury mechanism, delay to presentation, inhalation injury, urine output and perfusion.

### Timing
- Classical division: **half during first 8 hours from the time of injury**; remaining half during the next 16 hours.
- If the patient presents 3 hours after burn, the first-half allocation applies to the **remaining 5 hours**, not a new 8-hour window.

### Worked example
**70-kg adult, 30% TBSA partial/full-thickness burn**:

| Formula | Initial first-24-h LR estimate | First 8 h from burn | Next 16 h |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Classic Parkland (4 mL) | **8,400 mL** | 4,200 mL | 4,200 mL |
| Modified Brooke (2 mL) | **4,200 mL** | 2,100 mL | 2,100 mL |

These are **starting estimates**, not competing “correct total volumes” for the same patient. Real infusion is titrated to response and adjusted for prior fluid administration.

### Resuscitation endpoints
- **Adult urine output:** classically **~0.5 mL/kg/h**.
- **Children:** classically **~1 mL/kg/h**.
- **Electrical/rhabdomyolysis:** higher targets are discussed in the sources; adjust to degree of pigment injury and specialist protocol, not to urine color alone.
- Also monitor BP, mental status, peripheral perfusion, lactate/base deficit, creatinine, sodium, fluid balance, respiratory status and edema.
- Both under-resuscitation and over-resuscitation are hazardous: excessive fluid → extremity/abdominal compartment syndromes and pulmonary edema (“fluid creep”).

### Colloids
- WOR discusses albumin/colloids after early capillary leak and references **Muir–Barclay**; use as historical background rather than routine first-line prescription.

## 7. Inhalation and toxic gas injury

### Upper/lower airway injury
- **Upper airway:** thermal swelling can obstruct oropharynx/larynx, especially after enclosed-space exposure.
- **Lower airway:** chemical injury by smoke products leads to bronchospasm, inflammation, casts and impaired gas exchange.
- **Circumferential chest eschar:** externally limits chest wall excursion and ventilation.

### Carbon monoxide poisoning
- CO binds hemoglobin with high affinity, **reducing oxygen delivery** and shifting oxyhemoglobin dissociation; tissue hypoxia can occur despite deceptively adequate PaO₂.
- **Standard pulse oximetry may appear falsely normal**, so consider **co-oximetry/carboxyhemoglobin** in significant smoke exposure.
- Treatment includes high-concentration oxygen and specialist assessment for selected advanced therapy.

### Cyanide toxicity
- Enclosed-space combustion can expose victims to cyanide, inhibiting mitochondrial **cytochrome c oxidase** → failure of oxidative phosphorylation → severe lactic acidosis and cardiovascular collapse.
- Consider with major smoke exposure, altered consciousness/hemodynamic collapse and high lactate; specialist antidotal therapy may be needed.

## 8. Burn wound treatment and dressings

| Depth / condition | General approach |
|---|---|
| Superficial | Cooling, analgesia, simple topical protection; no routine grafting |
| Superficial partial | Clean nonadherent dressings; moisture balance; assess infection and healing |
| Deep partial | Specialist assessment for excision/grafting depending on healing potential and functional location |
| Full thickness | **Early excision + skin graft** when suitable; selected complex reconstruction |
| Infected/necrotic wound | Debridement, drainage and targeted treatment as indicated |

### Topical agents appearing in sources

| Agent | Key feature / caveat |
|---|---|
| **Silver sulfadiazine** | Broad antimicrobial activity; often a classic exam answer, but not automatically the optimal dressing for uncomplicated superficial burns; may delay epithelialization |
| **Silver nitrate** | Topical antimicrobial; staining and electrolyte disturbances can occur |
| **Mafenide acetate** | **Penetrates eschar**, painful application; systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibition may cause **metabolic acidosis** |
| **Cerium nitrate** | Specialist burn treatment discussed in notes; not universal first-choice agent |
| **Paraffin/nonadherent gauze, collagen, hydrocolloid** | Protect superficial/partial-thickness wounds and support moist healing in selected situations |

### Infection prevention
- Aseptic handling, appropriate dressings, early excision of nonviable tissue when indicated, optimal nutrition and perfusion.
- **Routine systemic antibiotic prophylaxis for all uncomplicated burns is not indicated as a general principle**; treat clinically established infection and follow local burn protocols.
- **Pseudomonas** is a notable pathogen in older burn-unit teaching, but severe burn sepsis can involve multiple bacteria/fungi.

## 9. Escharotomy vs fasciotomy

| Feature | Escharotomy | Fasciotomy |
|---|---|---|
| What is incised? | **Inelastic burned skin/eschar**, through to subcutaneous layer as needed | **Deep fascia** around muscular compartment |
| Indication | Constricting circumferential deep burn impairing distal perfusion or chest expansion | **True compartment syndrome** from elevated intracompartment pressure, including electrical/crush-associated muscle edema |
| Goal | Release external skin constriction | Decompress muscle compartment |
| Key distinction | Does **not routinely require dividing deep fascia** | Fascia must be released |

**Important source correction:** WOR describes escharotomy as “deep fascia is cut,” but that is **the defining step of fasciotomy**, not an ordinary escharotomy. Maintain the distinction for exams and clinical concepts.

### Recognizing constriction/compartment compromise
- Progressive pain/paresthesias (if viable nerves), tense swelling, reduced distal perfusion, Doppler/pulse abnormalities and impaired breathing from chest restriction.
- A palpable pulse does **not** exclude early compartment syndrome.

## 10. Chemical, electrical and cold injuries

### Chemical burns
- **Alkali** usually causes deeper **liquefactive necrosis**; acids tend to produce coagulative injury, although individual agents vary.
- Protect rescuers, remove contaminated clothing, **brush off dry powder before irrigation**, then use copious irrigation where appropriate.
- **Do not attempt acid–alkali neutralization on skin** because of heat generation/delayed care.
- Special substances and eye exposures require specific protocols and urgent specialist assistance.

### Hydrofluoric acid (HF)
- Fluoride binds **calcium and magnesium** → **hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, dysrhythmias**, with possible hyperkalemia.
- Tissue damage can be severe despite small skin lesion or delayed pain.
- Source-specific therapy: **calcium gluconate** locally or through specialized administration depending on severity/site; ECG and electrolytes are important in significant exposures.

### Electrical injury
- **Surface burn can underestimate internal muscle/vascular/nerve damage.**
- Risk: deep tissue necrosis, **rhabdomyolysis/myoglobinuria**, AKI, arrhythmias, compartment syndrome, entry/exit wounds.
- Evaluate mechanism/voltage, ECG when indicated, kidney function, CK, electrolytes, urine pigment and affected limb perfusion.
- Deep necrosis may require surgical debridement/fasciotomy, grafts or reconstruction.

### Lightning injury
- Very high-voltage, extremely brief electrical discharge; cardiopulmonary arrest/neurologic injury may dominate despite limited surface burn.

### Frostbite (cold burn, separate mechanism)
- Freezing → ice crystals, vascular thrombosis and reperfusion injury; commonly affects digits/ears/nose.
- Protect from refreezing; controlled rewarming and analgesia; assess tissue viability later—**do not rush irreversible amputation decisions before demarcation unless infection/other emergencies demand it**.
- **Trench foot** is a prolonged nonfreezing immersion injury, not identical to frostbite.

## 11. Nutrition and long-term complications

### Metabolic needs
- Major burns create marked **hypermetabolism and protein catabolism**; muscle loss/nitrogen loss can be pronounced (Doctut and WOR discuss peaks during days 5–10).
- Early adequate **enteral nutrition if the gut works** protects physiology and supports wound healing.
- Protein and total energy targets are **individualized**; historical equations such as Curreri/Davies are teaching concepts rather than perfect calculators for every burn patient.

### Complications over time

| Phase | Major threats |
|---|---|
| Immediate | Asphyxia, airway obstruction, associated trauma, toxic inhalation, cardiac arrest |
| Early hours–days | Burn shock, electrolyte derangement, AKI, compartment syndromes, respiratory failure |
| Later days–weeks | Wound infection/sepsis, pneumonia, organ dysfunction, malnutrition |
| Long term | Hypertrophic scar, keloid, contracture, neuropathy, deformity, psychological and functional sequelae |

### Rehabilitation and reconstruction
- Early physiotherapy, pressure/off-loading and splinting as indicated; optimize nutrition.
- Scar management may include silicone/pressure therapy, intralesional treatments and selective surgery.
- Contractures limiting joint function may require release and **skin graft/flap** reconstruction.

## 12. Referral to specialist burn center
- Burns involving **face, hands, feet, perineum/genitalia or major joints**, significant deep burns or functional risk.
- **Inhalation injury**, chemical/electrical burns, major pediatric burns, associated trauma or significant comorbidity.
- WOR uses partial-thickness burns **>10% TBSA** as an important threshold; actual referral criteria vary by age, resources and specialist guidance.

### Chapter 07 — rapid revision
- **Jackson:** coagulation (dead), stasis (salvageable), hyperemia (recoverable).
- **Superficial partial = painful, pink, blanching; full thickness = dry eschar, insensate, nonblanching.**
- **Lund–Browder** best reflects changing child body proportions.
- **Wallace adults:** head 9; arms 9 each; legs 18 each; front/back trunk 18 each; perineum 1.
- **Classic Parkland 4 × kg × %TBSA; modified adult Brooke 2 × kg × %TBSA.** Split first 8 h **from injury**, next 16 h; titrate to urine output and physiology.
- **CO poisoning may have deceptively normal SpO₂.**
- **HF burn → hypocalcemia; mafenide → metabolic acidosis.**
- **Escharotomy = incise eschar; fasciotomy = divide deep fascia.**
