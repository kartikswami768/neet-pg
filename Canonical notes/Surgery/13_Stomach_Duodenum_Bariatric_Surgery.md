# 13. Stomach, Duodenum and Bariatric Surgery

**Part:** III — Gastrointestinal Surgery  
**Tags:** #Surgery #GIT #Stomach #Duodenum #Bariatric #NEETPG #INICET

## 13.1 Relevant Surgical Anatomy

### Vascular supply of the stomach

| Region | Principal artery |
|---|---|
| Lesser curvature | Left and right gastric |
| Greater curvature | Left and right gastroepiploic |
| Fundus | Short gastric arteries |
| Posterior fundus (variable) | Posterior gastric artery |

### Vagus nerve

- Anterior vagal trunk originates predominantly from the left vagus.
- Posterior vagal trunk originates predominantly from the right vagus.
- Nerves of Latarjet supply the stomach and terminate near the pylorus as the **crow's-foot branches**.
- Hepatic and celiac branches are relevant during vagotomy.
- The criminal nerve of Grassi is an aberrant posterior vagal branch historically associated with recurrent ulcers after incomplete vagotomy.

### Duodenal relations

- Posterior aspect of the first part: gastroduodenal artery.
- Anterior aspect of the first part: intraperitoneal surface.
- Third part: crossed anteriorly by superior mesenteric vessels.

**Clinical correlation:** posterior duodenal ulcer → gastroduodenal arterial bleeding; anterior duodenal ulcer → free perforation.

## 13.2 Congenital Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis

### Pathophysiology

Hypertrophy of the circular muscle of the pylorus produces gastric outlet obstruction.

### Presentation

- Classically firstborn male infant.
- Presents after an initially normal neonatal period, commonly around 2–6 weeks.
- Progressive, projectile, **nonbilious vomiting**.
- Hungry after vomiting.
- Visible gastric peristalsis moving left to right.
- Palpable olive-shaped pyloric mass.

### Biochemical consequences

Loss of gastric HCl → hypochloremia and metabolic alkalosis.

Repeated fluid losses → volume contraction and potassium depletion.

**Classic result:** hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.

Paradoxical aciduria may occur as severe volume and potassium depletion drive renal hydrogen-ion excretion.

### Investigations

| Investigation | Finding |
|---|---|
| Ultrasound | Thickened, elongated pyloric muscle |
| Upper GI contrast study | Narrowed pyloric channel, string/double-track signs |
| Blood electrolytes | Hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis |

The revision source gives commonly taught ultrasound measurements of muscle thickness greater than 4 mm and channel length greater than 16 mm; age and local imaging criteria can affect interpretation.

### Management

1. Correct dehydration and electrolyte/acid–base abnormalities.
2. Establish adequate urine output.
3. Perform **Ramstedt pyloromyotomy**.
4. Resume feeds according to postoperative condition.

**Ramstedt pyloromyotomy:** longitudinal splitting of hypertrophied pyloric muscle without breaching the mucosa.

**Exam pearl:** the immediate priority is correction of metabolic abnormalities, not taking an unstable infant directly to the operating theatre.

## 13.3 Peptic Ulcer Disease

### Definition

A chronic ulcer extending through the muscularis mucosae into deeper gastric or duodenal tissue.

### Pathophysiology

Aggressive factors:
- Hydrochloric acid and pepsin.
- *Helicobacter pylori*.
- NSAID-related prostaglandin deficiency.

Protective factors:
- Mucus-bicarbonate barrier.
- Mucosal blood flow.
- Prostaglandins.
- Epithelial regeneration.

Ulcers arise when injury exceeds mucosal defenses.

### Gastric versus duodenal ulcer

| Feature | Gastric | Duodenal |
|---|---|---|
| Typical location | Lesser curvature/antral region | First part of duodenum |
| Fundamental abnormality | Impaired mucosal defenses | Often increased acid exposure |
| Pain with food | Classically worsens | Classically improves |
| Malignancy | Must be excluded by appropriate biopsy | Primary duodenal ulcers rarely malignant |
| Posterior bleeding vessel | Often left gastric branches | Gastroduodenal artery |
| Anterior perforation | Possible | Classic free perforation |

### *H. pylori*

- Curved gram-negative organism.
- Urease positive.
- CagA and VacA contribute to mucosal damage.
- Associated with chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, gastric adenocarcinoma and gastric MALT lymphoma.

Relevant tests:
- Urea breath test.
- Stool antigen.
- Endoscopic biopsy-based testing.

After eradication therapy, confirmation is based on suitable active-infection testing rather than antibody serology.

### Gastric ulcer: modified Johnson classification

| Type | Location/association |
|---|---|
| I | Lesser curvature near incisura; most common classic type |
| II | Gastric ulcer associated with duodenal ulcer |
| III | Prepyloric ulcer |
| IV | High lesser-curvature/proximal gastric ulcer |
| V | NSAID-associated ulcer that may occur anywhere |

### Special ulcers

| Named ulcer | Association |
|---|---|
| Cushing ulcer | Intracranial injury or raised ICP |
| Curling ulcer | Severe burns and mucosal ischemia |
| Cameron lesion | Linear erosions/ulcers associated with large hiatal hernia |

## 13.4 Complications of Peptic Ulcer Disease

### A. Hemorrhage

Most frequent major complication in the revision sources.

**Posterior duodenal ulcer**
- Erodes gastroduodenal artery.
- May cause severe upper GI bleeding.

**Gastric ulcer**
- Bleeding may involve gastric arterial branches.

### Forrest classification

| Class | Endoscopic finding | Risk category |
|---|---|---|
| Ia | Active spurting bleeding | High |
| Ib | Active oozing bleeding | High |
| IIa | Nonbleeding visible vessel | High |
| IIb | Adherent clot | Intermediate/variable |
| IIc | Flat pigmented spot | Low |
| III | Clean ulcer base | Low |

### Bleeding-ulcer management

1. Airway and hemodynamic assessment.
2. IV access, laboratory assessment and appropriate resuscitation.
3. PPI therapy in the appropriate setting.
4. Early upper GI endoscopy after stabilization.
5. Endoscopic hemostasis:
   - Clips.
   - Coagulation.
   - Injection therapy combined with another hemostatic modality when required.
6. Recurrent/uncontrolled bleeding:
   - Repeat endoscopic intervention where appropriate.
   - Angiographic embolization.
   - Surgical hemostasis if necessary.

### B. Perforation

**Classic:** anterior duodenal ulcer.

Clinical features:
- Sudden severe epigastric pain.
- Generalized tenderness and guarding.
- Board-like rigidity.
- Tachycardia, fever or shock.
- Absent bowel sounds with paralytic ileus.

Imaging:
- Free intraperitoneal air beneath diaphragm.
- Rigler sign on supine radiograph.
- CT is useful in suspected perforation.

#### Valentino syndrome

Perforated duodenal contents track toward the right iliac fossa and mimic acute appendicitis.

### Perforated-ulcer surgical treatment

- Resuscitation and broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy.
- Laparoscopy or laparotomy for source control when indicated.
- Primary closure with omental reinforcement in suitable ulcers.

**Graham/modified Graham omental patch**
- Omentum reinforces the site of ulcer perforation.
- Widely taught for uncomplicated perforated duodenal ulcer.

Large perforations, severe contamination, malignant gastric ulcers and unstable physiology may require alternative procedures.

### C. Gastric outlet obstruction

Causes include:
- Pyloric/duodenal scarring from chronic peptic ulcer disease.
- Gastric carcinoma.
- Extrinsic compression or other structural obstruction.

Clinical features:
- Persistent nonbilious vomiting.
- Retained food from preceding meals.
- Early satiety.
- Weight loss and dehydration.
- Succussion splash and visible gastric peristalsis.

Biochemical findings:
- Hypochloremia.
- Hypokalemia.
- Metabolic alkalosis.

Management:
- NG decompression as needed.
- IV fluid and electrolyte replacement.
- Upper GI endoscopy and tissue diagnosis when indicated.
- Definitive treatment directed at cause, including dilation or surgery in selected benign cases.

## 13.5 Surgical Treatment of Peptic Ulcer Disease

Surgical acid-reduction operations are historically important and remain exam-relevant.

### Types of vagotomy

| Operation | Nerves divided | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Truncal vagotomy | Main vagal trunks | Extensive acid reduction; alters gastric motility and other vagal functions |
| Selective vagotomy | Gastric branches while preserving major extragastric branches | Reduces acid with less extragastric denervation |
| Highly selective vagotomy | Parietal-cell branches with preservation of antral/pyloric innervation | No routine drainage procedure required |

### Why a drainage procedure after truncal vagotomy?

Denervation can impair pyloric relaxation and gastric emptying.

Possible accompanying procedures:
- Pyloroplasty.
- Gastrojejunostomy.

### Heineke–Mikulicz pyloroplasty

- Longitudinal pyloric incision.
- Transverse closure.
- Widens the pyloric outlet.

### Complications of vagotomy

- Delayed gastric emptying/pylorospasm.
- Postvagotomy diarrhea.
- Gallstones after truncal denervation.
- Recurrent ulceration after incomplete denervation.
- Postoperative nutritional and motility disturbances.

## 13.6 Gastric Reconstruction

### Comparison

| Procedure | Anastomosis |
|---|---|
| Billroth I | Gastroduodenostomy |
| Billroth II | Gastrojejunostomy |
| Roux-en-Y gastrojejunostomy | Gastric remnant joined to Roux limb with downstream jejunojejunostomy |
| Roux-en-Y esophagojejunostomy | Esophagus joined to Roux limb after total gastrectomy |

### Post-gastrectomy syndromes

#### A. Early dumping syndrome

- Occurs soon after meals.
- Hyperosmolar chyme rapidly enters small bowel.
- Fluid shifts into bowel lumen.
- Symptoms: abdominal cramps, diarrhea, palpitations, sweating, dizziness.

#### B. Late dumping syndrome

- Postprandial exaggerated insulin response.
- Reactive hypoglycemia.
- Symptoms: sweating, tremor, weakness, palpitations and hunger.

| Feature | Early dumping | Late dumping |
|---|---|---|
| Main mechanism | Osmotic fluid shift | Reactive hypoglycemia |
| Dominant symptoms | GI and vasomotor | Hypoglycemic |
| Dietary approach | Small meals, reduced rapidly absorbed carbohydrate | Similar preventive dietary strategy |

Management:
- Small, frequent meals.
- Reduce concentrated/simple sugars.
- Adjust fluid timing around meals.
- Selected resistant cases: octreotide.

#### C. Afferent loop syndrome

- Obstruction of the afferent biliopancreatic limb after reconstruction.
- Causes abdominal pain, distension and bilious vomiting.
- Severe obstruction risks pancreatitis, cholangitis or perforation.
- Requires assessment for endoscopic or surgical correction.

#### D. Efferent loop obstruction

- Impedes gastric emptying.
- Vomiting may contain food and bile.
- Management depends on cause and severity.

#### E. Internal hernia

- **Petersen hernia:** through mesenteric space posterior to the Roux limb.
- Mesocolic defects may cause herniation in retrocolic reconstructions.
- Can produce closed-loop obstruction and ischemia.

#### F. Nutritional consequences

- Iron deficiency.
- Vitamin B12 deficiency.
- Calcium/vitamin D deficiency.
- Protein-energy malnutrition in selected patients.

## 13.7 Gastric Adenocarcinoma

### Risk factors

- *H. pylori*-associated chronic gastritis.
- Intestinal metaplasia.
- Dietary salt/preserved foods.
- Tobacco exposure.
- Certain gastric polyps and premalignant conditions.
- Prior gastric surgery.
- Familial/genetic predisposition.

### Lauren classification

| Feature | Intestinal type | Diffuse type |
|---|---|---|
| Histology | Gland-forming malignant epithelium | Discohesive cells, often signet-ring |
| Molecular association | Multistep intestinal-type pathway | E-cadherin/CDH1 alterations |
| Background | Atrophic gastritis and metaplasia | May occur without classic metaplastic sequence |
| Morphology | Mass or ulcerating lesion | Diffuse infiltrative thickening |
| Classical appearance | Gland-forming tumor | Linitis plastica |

### Linitis plastica

- Diffuse infiltration of the gastric wall.
- Fibrotic, rigid, poorly distensible stomach.
- Described as **leather-bottle stomach**.

### Early versus advanced gastric cancer

- **Early gastric cancer:** limited to mucosa or submucosa, regardless of lymph node involvement.
- **Advanced gastric cancer:** invades muscularis propria or deeper.
- Early lesions can be assessed with endoscopic classifications and selected lesions treated by endoscopic resection.

### Borrmann classification: advanced gastric carcinoma

- I: Polypoid/fungating mass.
- II: Ulcerated with raised, well-defined margins.
- III: Ulceroinfiltrative.
- IV: Diffusely infiltrative.

### Classical metastatic manifestations

| Sign | Description |
|---|---|
| Virchow node / Troisier sign | Left supraclavicular metastatic lymphadenopathy |
| Sister Mary Joseph nodule | Umbilical metastasis |
| Krukenberg tumor | Ovarian metastasis, classically signet-ring adenocarcinoma |
| Blumer shelf | Metastatic deposits in rectovesical/rectouterine pouch |
| Irish node | Left axillary metastatic node |
| Leser–Trélat sign | Sudden eruption of multiple seborrheic keratoses associated with malignancy |
| Tripe palms | Velvety hyperkeratosis of palms associated with internal malignancy |

### Investigations

1. Upper GI endoscopy with multiple biopsies.
2. Contrast-enhanced CT for staging.
3. EUS for selected local staging.
4. PET-CT in selected cases rather than as a universal replacement for CT.
5. Staging laparoscopy in appropriate potentially resectable advanced disease to identify occult peritoneal spread.

### Surgical treatment

**Distal gastric cancer**
- Subtotal/distal gastrectomy when adequate clearance achievable.
- Reconstruction according to surgical anatomy.

**Proximal/diffuse gastric cancer**
- Total gastrectomy may be required.
- Roux-en-Y esophagojejunostomy.

### Lymphadenectomy

- **D1:** perigastric lymphatic dissection.
- **D2:** extended regional lymphadenectomy along major gastric arterial drainage territories.
- At least **16 examined lymph nodes** is a commonly taught minimum for adequate pathological staging.

Modern multimodal treatment also incorporates systemic therapy according to resectability and oncological stage.

### Surgical complications

- Anastomotic leak.
- Hemorrhage.
- Intra-abdominal abscess.
- Pancreatic injury.
- Delayed gastric emptying.
- Dumping syndrome.
- Malnutrition.
- Vitamin B12 deficiency after total gastrectomy.

## 13.8 Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GIST)

### Origin

Mesenchymal neoplasms associated with the **interstitial cells of Cajal**.

### Common site

Stomach is the most frequent location.

### Molecular and immunohistochemical characteristics

| Marker | Significance |
|---|---|
| CD117 / KIT | Frequently positive |
| DOG1 | Useful diagnostic marker |
| CD34 | Frequently expressed |
| KIT and PDGFRA mutations | Important molecular drivers in many tumors |
| SDH deficiency | Associated with certain syndromic GISTs |

### Clinical presentation

- Upper GI bleeding.
- Abdominal pain.
- Submucosal mass.
- Anemia.
- Occasionally incidental detection.

### Investigations

- Contrast-enhanced CT.
- Endoscopy/EUS.
- Histology and IHC when tissue diagnosis is required.
- Risk assessment based on size, mitotic activity and site.

### Fletcher risk concept

Tumor size and mitotic count help estimate progression risk. Location and rupture further affect risk in more detailed schemes.

### Management

- Resect localized operable tumors with intact pseudocapsule and negative margins.
- Avoid tumor rupture.
- Routine extensive lymphadenectomy is generally not a defining component of GIST surgery.
- **Imatinib:** targeted treatment for appropriate mutation-sensitive tumors.
- Subsequent targeted therapies are selected according to mutation and resistance profile.

### Carney triad

- Gastric GIST.
- Pulmonary chondroma.
- Extra-adrenal paraganglioma.

Important distinction: Carney triad and Carney–Stratakis syndrome are related but not identical entities.

## 13.9 Gastric Volvulus

### Definition

Rotation of stomach causing obstruction and potentially vascular compromise.

### Types

| Type | Axis |
|---|---|
| Organoaxial | Along longitudinal line between cardia and pylorus |
| Mesenteroaxial | Around transverse axis |

Organoaxial volvulus is classically associated with a diaphragmatic/paraesophageal hernia.

### Borchardt triad

1. Severe epigastric pain/distension.
2. Unproductive retching.
3. Failure to pass a nasogastric tube.

### Management

- Resuscitation and decompression.
- Urgent assessment for ischemia.
- Detorsion/reduction.
- Repair the associated anatomical defect.
- Gastropexy in appropriate cases.

## 13.10 Bezoars

- **Trichobezoar:** collection of hair in stomach.
- **Phytobezoar:** collection of vegetable fibers.
- Can cause gastric outlet obstruction or intestinal obstruction.
- Rapunzel syndrome describes extension of a large trichobezoar beyond the stomach into the small bowel.
- Endoscopic removal is possible for selected bezoars; larger trichobezoars may need surgery.
- Address predisposing behavioral or gastrointestinal conditions.

## 13.11 Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery

### Indications in revision sources

- BMI at least 40 kg/m².
- BMI at least 35 kg/m² with obesity-associated complications.
- Lower BMI thresholds may be used in appropriate Asian populations and metabolic surgery pathways.

These are classical revision thresholds; eligibility criteria vary with the guideline and patient characteristics.

### Preoperative evaluation

- Obesity history and previous treatment.
- Metabolic comorbidities.
- Nutritional status and deficiencies.
- Psychiatric/behavioral evaluation.
- Eating patterns and adherence capacity.
- Anesthetic and thromboembolic risk.
- Evaluation for reflux and relevant upper GI pathology.

### OS-MRS risk factors

Obesity Surgery Mortality Risk Score includes:
- Hypertension.
- Age older than 45 years.
- Male sex.
- BMI greater than 50 kg/m².
- Pulmonary embolism risk factors.

### Procedures

| Procedure | Mechanism/features |
|---|---|
| Sleeve gastrectomy | Resection along greater curvature; restrictive and hormonal effects |
| Roux-en-Y gastric bypass | Restriction, altered nutrient flow and hormonal effects |
| Adjustable gastric band | Adjustable restrictive device |
| Intragastric balloon | Endoscopic space-occupying device |
| Biliopancreatic diversion/duodenal switch | Major intestinal rearrangement with greater malabsorptive effect |
| SADI-S | Single-anastomosis duodeno-ileal bypass with sleeve |

### Sleeve gastrectomy

- Greater curvature stomach resected.
- Narrow gastric tube remains.
- Potential problems:
  - Staple-line bleeding.
  - Leak near gastroesophageal junction.
  - Gastroesophageal reflux.
  - Stenosis or twisting.
  - Nutritional deficiencies.
  - Weight regain.

### Roux-en-Y gastric bypass

- Small gastric pouch.
- Gastrojejunostomy to Roux limb.
- Jejunojejunostomy reconstructs bowel continuity.

Complications:
- Anastomotic leak.
- Marginal ulcers.
- Internal hernia.
- Dumping syndrome.
- Micronutrient deficiency.
- Small-bowel obstruction.

### Duodenal switch / biliopancreatic diversion

- Greater nutrient malabsorption.
- Extensive weight reduction potential.
- Significant risk of protein and micronutrient deficiency.
- Demands structured long-term nutritional follow-up.

### Deficiencies after bariatric surgery

- Iron.
- Vitamin B12.
- Folate.
- Calcium and vitamin D.
- Thiamine.
- Fat-soluble vitamins in malabsorptive procedures.
- Protein deficiency in severe cases.

## 13.12 Upper Gastrointestinal Hemorrhage

### Causes

**Nonvariceal**
- Peptic ulcer.
- Erosive gastritis.
- Mallory–Weiss tear.
- Gastric malignancy.
- Angiodysplasia/GAVE.

**Variceal**
- Portal-hypertension-associated esophageal or gastric varices.

### Initial approach

1. Assess airway, breathing and circulation.
2. Identify hemodynamic instability.
3. Establish IV access and resuscitate as required.
4. Check blood counts, coagulation and crossmatch.
5. Perform upper GI endoscopy after appropriate stabilization.
6. Treat the bleeding source.

### Risk assessment

- Glasgow–Blatchford score: predicts need for intervention.
- Rockall score: assesses rebleeding/mortality risk.
- Forrest classification: describes bleeding-ulcer stigmata.

### Nonvariceal bleeding

- Endoscopic clips, thermal therapies and combination hemostasis.
- PPI treatment as appropriate.
- Interventional radiology or surgery for uncontrolled/recurrent bleeding.

### Variceal bleeding

- Resuscitation.
- Vasoactive medication such as terlipressin or octreotide.
- Endoscopic variceal ligation.
- Appropriate antibiotics in the setting of cirrhosis-associated bleeding.
- Rescue measures may include balloon tamponade or TIPS in selected situations.

**Portal hypertension, TIPS and portosystemic shunts are cross-linked to Part IV rather than duplicated in full here.**
