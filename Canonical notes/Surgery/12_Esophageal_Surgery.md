# 12. Esophageal Surgery

**Part:** III — Gastrointestinal Surgery  
**Tags:** #Surgery #GIT #Esophagus #NEETPG #INICET

## 12.1 Surgical Anatomy

### Basic anatomy

- Esophagus is a muscular tube extending from the **pharyngoesophageal junction (C6)** to the gastroesophageal junction.
- Pierces the diaphragm at **T10**.
- Upper third: predominantly striated muscle.
- Middle third: mixed striated and smooth muscle.
- Lower third: predominantly smooth muscle.
- Unlike most of the GI tract, the thoracic esophagus has **no serosal covering**:
  - Poor containment of perforations.
  - Greater likelihood of mediastinitis.
  - Important for tumor spread.

### Three major constrictions

| Site | Distance from upper incisors | Significance |
|---|---:|---|
| Cricopharyngeal junction | 15 cm | Narrowest esophageal constriction; common foreign-body impaction |
| Aortic arch/left main bronchus | 25 cm | External compression |
| Diaphragmatic hiatus | 40 cm | Gastroesophageal junction region |

### Surgical vascular anatomy

- Cervical esophagus: inferior thyroid artery branches.
- Thoracic esophagus: aortic and bronchial branches.
- Abdominal esophagus: left gastric and inferior phrenic branches.
- Venous drainage: azygos system, thyroid veins, left gastric vein.
- Lymphatic drainage is longitudinal, allowing spread beyond the primary tumor region.

### Nerve supply

- Parasympathetic: vagus nerve.
- Intramural neural control: myenteric (Auerbach) plexus.
- Inhibitory neurons release **nitric oxide and VIP**, allowing LES relaxation.

## 12.2 Approach to Dysphagia

Dysphagia must be classified before investigating.

| Feature | Likely etiology |
|---|---|
| Difficulty initiating swallowing, choking | Oropharyngeal dysphagia |
| Solids first, then liquids | Progressive mechanical obstruction |
| Solids and liquids from onset | Motility disorder |
| Progressive dysphagia with weight loss | Esophageal malignancy |
| Intermittent solid-food dysphagia | Schatzki ring or eosinophilic esophagitis |
| Regurgitation of undigested food | Achalasia or Zenker diverticulum |
| Dysphagia with odynophagia | Inflammatory or infectious esophagitis |

**Investigative approach**

1. Determine oropharyngeal versus esophageal dysphagia.
2. Identify alarm features: weight loss, anemia, bleeding, progressive symptoms.
3. Upper GI endoscopy for suspected structural or mucosal disease.
4. Barium swallow for relevant anatomical and motility patterns.
5. High-resolution manometry when a motility disorder is suspected and structural obstruction has been excluded.

## 12.3 Esophageal Foreign Bodies

### Common impaction sites

- Cricopharyngeal narrowing.
- Aortic/bronchial constriction.
- Distal esophageal narrowing.

### Coin versus airway foreign body

| X-ray projection | Esophageal coin | Tracheal coin |
|---|---|---|
| AP view | Circular face commonly visible | Edge commonly visible |
| Lateral view | Edge commonly visible | Circular face commonly visible |

### Button battery

- May show the **double-ring or halo sign**.
- Particularly dangerous because of local electrical and caustic injury.
- Esophageal button batteries require urgent endoscopic removal.
- Observation alone is inappropriate for an impacted esophageal battery.

### Other foreign bodies

- Sharp objects: increased perforation risk.
- Food bolus: commonly associated with stricture, ring, or eosinophilic esophagitis.
- Multiple magnets: risk of pressure necrosis and fistulation when separated by bowel walls.

Management depends on location, object type, symptoms and complications. The simple “observe if beyond C6” rule in older notes should not be applied indiscriminately.

## 12.4 Corrosive Esophageal Injury

### Mechanism

**Alkali**
- Causes liquefactive necrosis.
- Saponification and protein dissolution facilitate deep tissue penetration.
- Commonly produces severe esophageal injury.

**Acid**
- Typically causes coagulative necrosis.
- More commonly associated with gastric injury, although severe esophageal damage can occur.

### Zargar endoscopic classification

| Grade | Findings |
|---|---|
| 0 | Normal |
| I | Edema and erythema |
| IIa | Superficial ulceration, erosions or exudates |
| IIb | Deep or circumferential ulceration |
| IIIa | Focal necrosis |
| IIIb | Extensive necrosis |
| IV | Perforation in expanded classifications |

### Clinical complications

**Early**
- Airway edema.
- Esophageal perforation.
- Mediastinitis.
- Gastric necrosis.
- Sepsis.

**Late**
- Esophageal stricture.
- Gastric outlet obstruction.
- Nutritional impairment.
- Increased long-term risk of esophageal carcinoma.

### Management principles

- Assess and protect airway.
- NPO, fluid resuscitation and analgesia.
- Avoid induced vomiting and chemical neutralization.
- Do not blindly insert a nasogastric tube.
- Early specialist evaluation and appropriately selected endoscopy/CT.
- No routine role for prophylactic corticosteroids.
- Antibiotics are indicated when infection, perforation or other relevant complications warrant them, not merely because corrosive ingestion occurred.

**Stricture treatment:** endoscopic dilatation; selected refractory cases require reconstruction or esophagectomy.

## 12.5 Tracheoesophageal Fistula and Esophageal Atresia

### Types

| Type | Anatomical defect |
|---|---|
| A | Esophageal atresia without fistula |
| B | Proximal TEF with distal esophageal atresia |
| C | Proximal esophageal atresia with distal TEF — **most common** |
| D | Proximal and distal TEF |
| E | Isolated H-type TEF without atresia |

### Clinical presentation

- Excessive salivation and drooling.
- Choking or respiratory distress during feeding.
- Failure to pass a nasogastric/orogastric tube.
- Coiled tube in the upper esophageal pouch.
- Abdominal distension when a distal TEF permits air into the stomach.

### Associated anomalies: VACTERL

- **V:** Vertebral defects.
- **A:** Anorectal malformations.
- **C:** Cardiac anomalies.
- **TE:** Tracheoesophageal fistula/atresia.
- **R:** Renal anomalies.
- **L:** Limb anomalies.

### Evaluation and treatment

- Radiography demonstrates a coiled tube in proximal atresia.
- Bronchoscopy/esophagoscopy assists in defining selected fistulas.
- Waterston classification is based on birth weight and associated pneumonia/anomalies.
- Maintain aspiration precautions and decompress the upper pouch.
- Definitive management usually involves fistula division and esophageal anastomosis.
- Long-gap atresia may require staged reconstruction.

## 12.6 Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

### Pathophysiology

GERD reflects failure of the antireflux barrier and/or impaired esophageal clearance.

Main mechanisms:
1. Transient LES relaxations.
2. Reduced resting LES pressure.
3. Shortened intra-abdominal esophagus.
4. Hiatal hernia with separation of LES and diaphragmatic crura.
5. Impaired clearance and delayed gastric emptying.

### Antireflux barrier

- Intrinsic LES.
- Intra-abdominal esophageal segment.
- Right diaphragmatic crus.
- Angle of His.
- Gastric mucosal folds.

### Clinical features

**Typical:** heartburn, sour regurgitation, water brash.

**Extraesophageal:** chronic cough, laryngitis, hoarseness, asthma-like symptoms and dental erosions.

### Investigations

| Investigation | Role |
|---|---|
| Upper GI endoscopy | Detects esophagitis, stricture, Barrett changes |
| Ambulatory esophageal pH monitoring | Objective measurement of pathological acid exposure |
| Impedance-pH monitoring | Evaluates acid and non-acid reflux |
| Esophageal manometry | Assesses motility, especially before antireflux surgery |
| Barium swallow | Evaluates structural anatomy and selected hiatal hernias |

### Surgical indications

- Objectively confirmed GERD with persistent symptoms despite appropriate therapy.
- Relevant complications.
- Significant reflux with a surgically relevant hiatal hernia.
- Selected patients preferring a definitive procedural approach after appropriate evaluation.

### Fundoplication

**Principle:** wrap the gastric fundus around the distal esophagus to reinforce the antireflux barrier.

| Procedure | Wrap |
|---|---|
| Nissen | 360° complete |
| Dor | Anterior partial wrap, approximately 180° |
| Toupet | Posterior partial wrap, approximately 270° |
| Belsey Mark IV | Partial transthoracic fundoplication |

#### Nissen fundoplication

- Most commonly discussed complete fundoplication.
- A floppy wrap aims to prevent excessive postoperative obstruction.
- Potential complications:
  - Gas-bloat syndrome.
  - Dysphagia.
  - Inability to belch or vomit normally.
  - Wrap migration or disruption.
  - Recurrent reflux.

#### Other procedures

- **Collis gastroplasty:** lengthening procedure for selected shortened esophagus cases.
- **Magnetic sphincter augmentation (LINX):** selected patients with appropriate anatomy and confirmed reflux.

## 12.7 Barrett Esophagus

### Definition

Specialized intestinal metaplasia of distal esophageal epithelium associated with chronic reflux.

### Pathogenesis

Chronic acid exposure → injury to squamous epithelium → adaptive metaplasia → possible dysplasia → esophageal adenocarcinoma.

### Histology

- Columnar mucosa with intestinal differentiation.
- **Goblet cells** are the characteristic histological finding in the source definition.

### Classification

- Short segment: less than 3 cm.
- Long segment: at least 3 cm.
- Prague classification records:
  - **C:** circumferential extent.
  - **M:** maximum extent.

### Seattle biopsy protocol

- Four-quadrant biopsies at prescribed intervals.
- Additional targeted biopsy of visible abnormalities.
- Intervals depend on dysplasia status and the surveillance protocol.

### Dysplasia and management

| Finding | General management |
|---|---|
| Nondysplastic Barrett | Surveillance endoscopy according to risk |
| Confirmed low-grade dysplasia | Endoscopic eradication therapy or carefully selected surveillance |
| High-grade dysplasia | Endoscopic eradication therapy; resect visible lesions |
| Selected superficial malignancy | Endoscopic resection, with further treatment based on histology |

- Endoscopic mucosal resection is useful for visible lesions.
- Radiofrequency ablation destroys residual flat dysplastic Barrett mucosa.

## 12.8 Achalasia Cardia

### Definition

Esophageal motility disorder characterized by:
1. Failure of LES relaxation.
2. Absent normal esophageal peristalsis.
3. Functional obstruction at the gastroesophageal junction.

### Pathophysiology

Destruction of inhibitory ganglion cells in the **myenteric plexus**.

Reduced NO/VIP-mediated inhibition leads to persistent LES tone and failure of relaxation.

### Etiology

- Primary idiopathic achalasia.
- Chagas disease.
- Pseudoachalasia due to malignant GE-junction infiltration.
- Triple-A/Allgrove syndrome:
  - Alacrimia.
  - Achalasia.
  - ACTH-resistant adrenal insufficiency.

### Clinical features

- Dysphagia to solids and liquids.
- Regurgitation of retained, undigested food.
- Weight loss.
- Retrosternal pain.
- Nocturnal aspiration or cough.

### Investigations

- **High-resolution manometry:** reference diagnostic test.
- Barium swallow: **bird-beak tapering** with proximal dilation.
- Endoscopy: excludes pseudoachalasia and other mechanical obstruction.

### Chicago classification

| Type | Key manometric feature |
|---|---|
| I — Classic | Failed peristalsis without significant panesophageal pressurization |
| II | Failed peristalsis with panesophageal pressurization |
| III — Spastic | Failed normal peristalsis with spastic/premature contractions |

### Eckardt score

Assesses:
- Dysphagia.
- Regurgitation.
- Chest pain.
- Weight loss.

Used to quantify symptom burden and treatment response.

### Treatment

| Procedure | Mechanism | Important feature |
|---|---|---|
| Pneumatic dilation | Balloon-mediated disruption of LES muscle | Particularly effective in selected type I/II cases |
| Laparoscopic Heller myotomy | Division of LES muscle | Frequently combined with partial fundoplication |
| POEM | Endoscopic submucosal tunnel and myotomy | Particularly useful in type III/spastic disease |
| Botulinum toxin | Temporarily blocks cholinergic stimulation | Higher recurrence; useful when definitive procedures unsuitable |

**Heller myotomy:** extends across the distal esophagus and onto the gastric cardia.

**Why add Dor/Toupet fundoplication?** Myotomy relieves the obstruction but weakens the reflux barrier.

**Important:** GERD is a major complication following myotomy, particularly POEM without an antireflux wrap.

## 12.9 Distal Esophageal Spasm

- Intermittent dysphagia and angina-like chest pain.
- Premature distal esophageal contractions.
- LES relaxation is generally preserved.
- Barium swallow: **corkscrew or rosary-bead appearance**.
- Manometry differentiates it from achalasia.
- Management discussed in the sources includes smooth-muscle relaxants and selected endoscopic myotomy for refractory symptoms.

### Comparison

| Condition | LES relaxation | Peristalsis | Characteristic clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Achalasia | Impaired | Absent | Bird-beak |
| Distal esophageal spasm | Generally preserved | Premature/spastic | Corkscrew |
| Hypercontractile esophagus | Generally preserved | Excessively forceful | High contractile integral |
| Scleroderma esophagus | Hypotensive LES | Weak/absent distally | Severe GERD |

## 12.10 Esophageal Diverticula

### Zenker diverticulum

- Pharyngoesophageal pulsion diverticulum.
- Originates through **Killian dehiscence** between the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus.
- False diverticulum.
- Characteristic features:
  - Regurgitation of undigested food.
  - Halitosis.
  - Dysphagia.
  - Aspiration pneumonia.
- Investigation: barium swallow.
- Management: endoscopic septotomy/diverticulotomy or surgical diverticulectomy with cricopharyngeal myotomy.

### Traction diverticulum

- Typically mid-esophageal.
- Often associated with mediastinal inflammatory fibrosis.
- True diverticulum containing the full wall.

### Epiphrenic diverticulum

- Distal esophageal pulsion diverticulum.
- Frequently associated with an underlying motility disorder.
- Treatment must address the underlying functional obstruction.

## 12.11 Hiatal Hernia

| Type | Anatomy | Key issue |
|---|---|---|
| I — Sliding | GE junction migrates into thorax | GERD |
| II — Paraesophageal | Fundus herniates; GE junction remains near normal | Volvulus/obstruction risk |
| III — Mixed | Sliding and paraesophageal components | Reflux and mechanical risk |
| IV | Additional abdominal organs may herniate | Complex mechanical complications |

- Type I is the most common.
- Type II–IV have greater relevance for mechanical obstruction, volvulus and ischemia.
- Fundoplication and hiatal repair may be combined in selected patients.

## 12.12 Esophageal Perforation

### Causes

- Instrumentation/endoscopy.
- Forceful vomiting.
- Foreign bodies.
- Corrosive injury.
- Penetrating trauma.

### Boerhaave syndrome

**Definition:** spontaneous transmural esophageal rupture, classically following forceful vomiting.

- Usually distal esophagus.
- Often left posterolateral wall.
- Gastric/esophageal contents enter the mediastinum.
- Can cause mediastinitis, sepsis and pleural contamination.

**Mackler triad**
1. Vomiting or retching.
2. Chest pain.
3. Subcutaneous emphysema.

**Hamman sign:** mediastinal crunch synchronous with the heartbeat.

### Boerhaave versus Mallory–Weiss

| Feature | Boerhaave | Mallory–Weiss |
|---|---|---|
| Injury | Transmural rupture | Mucosal tear |
| Predominant presentation | Severe chest pain, mediastinitis | Hematemesis |
| Mediastinal contamination | May occur | No |
| Management | Urgent specialist evaluation, source control | Usually endoscopic/conservative |

### Investigations

- Chest X-ray: pneumomediastinum, pleural effusion.
- CT with suitable contrast assessment in stable patients.
- Water-soluble contrast swallow in selected circumstances.
- Endoscopy when appropriate after specialist assessment.

### Treatment principles

- NPO, IV fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics when perforation/mediastinal contamination is present.
- Drain contaminated spaces.
- Close or seal the perforation when appropriate.
- Provide enteral or parenteral nutritional support.
- Selected contained leaks may be treated nonoperatively.
- Larger or uncontrolled leaks may require operative repair, drainage or endoscopic stenting.

## 12.13 Other Benign Esophageal Conditions

### Schatzki ring

- Distal esophageal mucosal ring.
- Intermittent solid-food dysphagia.
- Symptomatic cases may undergo endoscopic dilation.

### Eosinophilic esophagitis

- Immune/antigen-mediated disease.
- Commonly presents with food impaction and dysphagia.
- Endoscopy: concentric rings, furrows or strictures.
- Biopsy: **at least 15 eosinophils/high-power field**.
- Treatment in the sources: PPI, swallowed topical steroids and appropriate dietary therapy.

### Infectious esophagitis

| Organism | Typical setting | Endoscopic pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Candida | Immunocompromised | White plaques |
| CMV | Advanced immunosuppression | Large/deep ulcers |
| HSV | Immunosuppression | Small punched-out ulcers |

### Esophageal leiomyoma

- Common benign mesenchymal esophageal tumor.
- Frequently asymptomatic.
- Barium swallow may reveal a smooth filling defect.
- Selected symptomatic lesions undergo enucleation or endoscopic resection.

## 12.14 Esophageal Carcinoma

### Squamous cell carcinoma versus adenocarcinoma

| Feature | SCC | Adenocarcinoma |
|---|---|---|
| Typical location | Middle esophagus | Distal esophagus/GE junction |
| Associations | Tobacco, alcohol, achalasia, corrosive strictures, tylosis | Chronic GERD, Barrett esophagus, obesity |
| Histology | Squamous differentiation, keratinization | Gland formation/mucin |
| Epidemiology | Prominent globally | Common in Western populations |

### Presentation

- Progressive dysphagia, usually solids before liquids.
- Weight loss and anorexia.
- Odynophagia.
- Hoarseness from recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement.
- Cough with swallowing from tracheoesophageal fistula.

### Investigation and staging

1. **Upper GI endoscopy with biopsy:** establishes tissue diagnosis.
2. CT chest and abdomen: assesses local extension and distant spread.
3. EUS: assesses depth of invasion and regional nodes where appropriate.
4. PET-CT: detects occult metastatic disease in selected staging situations.

### Selected T-stage anatomy

| Stage | Invasion |
|---|---|
| T1a | Lamina propria or muscularis mucosae |
| T1b | Submucosa |
| T2 | Muscularis propria |
| T3 | Adventitia |
| T4a | Selected potentially resectable adjacent structures |
| T4b | Unresectable adjacent structures such as aorta or trachea |

### Siewert classification of GE-junction tumors

- Type I: distal esophageal adenocarcinoma.
- Type II: true junctional tumor.
- Type III: subcardial gastric tumor.

This helps determine the anatomical approach and extent of resection.

### Esophagectomy procedures

| Operation | Basic approach |
|---|---|
| Ivor Lewis | Abdominal and right thoracic approach; intrathoracic anastomosis |
| McKeown | Abdominal, thoracic and cervical approach; cervical anastomosis |
| Transhiatal esophagectomy | Abdominal and cervical approach without formal thoracotomy |

### Reconstruction

- **Gastric conduit:** most common replacement.
- Main vascular supply: right gastroepiploic artery, with contribution from preserved vessels.
- Colon or jejunum may be used when gastric replacement is unsuitable.
- Posterior mediastinal route is anatomically shortest.

### Important postoperative complications

- Anastomotic leak.
- Conduit ischemia or necrosis.
- Pulmonary complications.
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury.
- Anastomotic stricture.
- Chylothorax.
- Delayed gastric emptying.

### Palliation

- Self-expanding metal stents (SEMS) for selected malignant dysphagia or fistulas.
- Nutritional support.
- Oncological treatment according to stage and clinical suitability.
