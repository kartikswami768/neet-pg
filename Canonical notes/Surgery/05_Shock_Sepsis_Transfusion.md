# 05 — Shock, Sepsis, Hemorrhage and Blood Transfusion

> [!info] Main sources
> [WOR—General Surgery pp. 17–22]; [Combined Bootcamp—Cardiology: Shock, PDF pp. 428–436]; [Robbins Pocket Companion—Hemodynamic Disorders/Shock, PDF pp. 155–183]; [First Aid 2025—Cardiovascular/Pathology and Pharmacology]; [Doctut—Shock and Blood Transfusion]. **Sepsis and transfusion terminology differs between sources; exam-source vs clinical protocols are distinguished below.**

## 1. Shock: definition and physiology

**Shock = acute circulatory failure leading to inadequate tissue oxygen delivery/utilization and cellular dysfunction.** Hypotension is common but can be a **late** or initially absent sign.

- Oxygen delivery: `DO₂ = cardiac output × arterial oxygen content`; arterial oxygen content depends mainly on hemoglobin and arterial oxygen saturation.
- **Cardiac output (CO) = heart rate × stroke volume.**
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≈ (SBP + 2 × DBP)/3** at usual heart rates.
- Low perfusion → anaerobic glycolysis → **lactate accumulation and metabolic acidosis**, reduced renal/brain/heart function and eventually multiorgan dysfunction.
- Compensatory sympathetic response: tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction, RAAS/ADH activation; in distributive shock, vascular tone is pathologically reduced.

### Classification and hemodynamics

| Type | Core mechanism | CO / filling pressures / SVR | Characteristic clinical clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolemic** | Loss of circulating blood/fluid → reduced preload | CO ↓, CVP/PCWP ↓, SVR ↑ | **Cold extremities, tachycardia, flat neck veins** |
| **Cardiogenic** | Myocardial pump failure (MI, severe arrhythmia, failure) | CO ↓, CVP often ↑, PCWP ↑ in LV failure, SVR ↑ | Cold, raised JVP, pulmonary edema/ischemia |
| **Obstructive** | Impeded filling or outflow (tamponade, PE, tension pneumothorax) | CO ↓, CVP usually ↑, SVR ↑ | Raised JVP with distinctive obstructive cause |
| **Septic—early warm** | Inflammatory vasodilation, leak and maldistributed flow | SVR ↓, CO often ↑ initially | **Warm peripheries**, bounding pulse, wide pulse pressure |
| **Septic—late/cold** | Myocardial dysfunction/hypovolemia, advanced illness | CO may ↓ | Cold extremities and organ failure |
| **Anaphylactic** | Mast cell mediator release → vasodilation and permeability ↑ | SVR ↓, preload ↓ | **Hypotension + urticaria/bronchospasm/airway swelling** (skin findings may be absent) |
| **Neurogenic** | Loss of sympathetic vascular tone after spinal cord/neuraxial injury | SVR ↓; HR may be **low** | **Hypotension + inappropriate bradycardia**, warm dry skin |

### Neurogenic vs spinal shock
- **Neurogenic shock:** a **hemodynamic** form of distributive shock due to sympathetic interruption.
- **Spinal shock:** transient **neurologic loss of reflexes and motor/sensory activity** below acute spinal cord injury.
- They are **not synonyms** and may coexist.

### General approach to an unstable patient
1. Recognize altered consciousness, cold skin, tachypnea, low perfusion, abnormal pulse/BP and reduced urine output.
2. **ABCDE**, oxygenation/ventilation and airway support as needed.
3. Establish IV/IO access and obtain bedside glucose, ECG, hemoglobin, electrolytes, lactate, blood gas, blood cultures if infection suspected, type and crossmatch where bleeding possible.
4. Rapidly determine likely physiology: **bleeding, pump failure, obstruction or distributive process**.
5. Give disease-specific resuscitation (fluid, blood, vasopressor/inotrope, decompression, hemorrhage control, antibiotics/source control).
6. Reassess perfusion, lactate trend, mental status, capillary refill, breathing, lungs, urine output and source response. **Treat the cause, not a single number.**

## 2. Hemorrhage and hypovolemic shock

### Mechanism
**Blood loss → reduced venous return → low preload → reduced stroke volume → decreased CO and oxygen delivery.** Reflex tachycardia and vasoconstriction may maintain SBP initially; a normal initial BP **does not exclude severe blood loss**.

### Visible vs concealed loss
- Visible: external wound, GI bleeding, operative field, surgical drains.
- Concealed: **chest, abdomen, retroperitoneum/pelvis, long-bone fractures**, and occasionally large soft-tissue spaces.
- **One on the floor, four more** is a classic teaching reminder to search external losses plus chest, abdomen, pelvis and long bones. Exact volumes depend on injury.

### Hemorrhage by timing
| Type | When | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | At time of injury/operation | Direct vessel damage |
| **Reactionary** | Usually first 24 h | Dislodged clot, slipped ligature, normalization of BP |
| **Secondary** | Days later | Infection/erosion and vessel-wall breakdown |

### Hemorrhagic shock classes (classic ATLS teaching framework)

| Class | Estimated loss of adult blood volume | Typical findings | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | <15% | Minimal pulse/BP change | Compensated |
| **II** | 15–30% | Tachycardia, narrowing pulse pressure, anxiety | Progressive loss |
| **III** | 30–40% | Marked tachycardia/tachypnea, low urine output, hypotension/confusion | Serious compromise |
| **IV** | >40% | Profound hypotension, altered consciousness, very poor perfusion | Life-threatening |

- A person’s absolute volume lost depends on **their circulating blood volume**, not a fixed 500-mL increment. WOR/Doctut tables use differing numbers; the **percentage-based classes are the defensible core**.
- Beta-blockade, pregnancy, athletic baseline and elderly physiology can blunt classic tachycardia.

### Resuscitation: key concepts
- **Stop bleeding immediately:** pressure, tourniquet when appropriate, hemostatic dressing, pelvic binder, surgery/interventional radiology.
- Obtain **two large-bore peripheral IVs** (e.g., 14–18 G when feasible) or rapid IO access; do not delay resuscitation for a central line.
- Use warmed fluids/blood; prevent **hypothermia**; minimize excessive crystalloids in significant hemorrhage.
- **Damage-control resuscitation (DCR):** rapid hemorrhage control, balanced blood components, hemorrhage/coagulopathy treatment, temperature and calcium management, avoid unnecessary dilution.
- **Permissive hypotension:** a temporizing strategy in selected uncontrolled hemorrhage **without** TBI/critical contraindications; not suitable when brain/spinal cord perfusion must be protected.
- **Tranexamic acid (TXA):** WOR describes **1 g IV over 10 min then 1 g over 8 h** for selected trauma patients at risk of major hemorrhage; timing is critical in source trial material. Do not substitute the contradictory Doctut 2+2 g text as a standard trauma regimen.

### Fluid-response categories
- **Rapid responder:** perfusion improves and remains improved after resuscitation; continue search for injury.
- **Transient responder:** improves then deteriorates → likely ongoing loss.
- **Nonresponder:** little response → massive ongoing bleeding, inadequate intervention or another shock mechanism; urgent definitive control.

### Endpoints of resuscitation
- Improvement in mental state, skin perfusion/capillary refill, pulse/BP and **urine output** (often goal ≥0.5 mL/kg/h in adults).
- **Serial lactate/base deficit** and trends in hemoglobin, coagulation/fibrinogen, ionized calcium and temperature.
- **Shock index = HR / SBP**; source convention **>0.9** suggests increased risk, but interpretation is context-sensitive.
- **Modified shock index = HR / MAP**; a high value flags compromised hemodynamics, not a stand-alone diagnostic criterion.
- **CVP is not a reliable stand-alone measure of fluid responsiveness**; treat filling-pressure tables as physiologic patterns, not fluid-order algorithms.

## 3. Sepsis and septic shock

### Pathophysiology: connect Robbins to bedside
1. Microbial PAMPs/toxins and injured-tissue mediators activate innate immunity (e.g., pattern-recognition receptors).
2. Excessive cytokines (**TNF, IL-1, IL-6**) and endothelial activation → **vasodilation, capillary leak, microvascular maldistribution and coagulation activation**.
3. Endothelial dysfunction and leukocyte/platelet activation may promote microthrombi and **DIC**.
4. Tissue extraction/utilization of oxygen and mitochondrial function become impaired; lactate may rise.
5. Myocardial depression, AKI, ARDS, encephalopathy and MODS may develop.

### Definition caveat: older SIRS vs contemporary terminology
- WOR and Doctut include the older exam definition: **SIRS ≥2 criteria + infection = sepsis**; septic shock described as infection with fluid-refractory hypotension.
- SIRS criteria: temperature >38°C or <36°C; HR >90/min; RR >20/min or PaCO₂ <32 mmHg; WBC >12,000 or <4,000/µL or >10% immature forms.
- **Modern conceptual usage:** sepsis means **life-threatening organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated host response to infection**; it is not synonymous with any infection plus SIRS. This reflects the distinction needed to interpret the older source chart; specific current protocol details are not independently verified here.
- **qSOFA screen:** respiratory rate ≥22/min, SBP ≤100 mmHg, altered mentation; ≥2 raises concern for poor outcome, **but cannot alone rule out sepsis**.

### Recognize the emergency
- Suspected infection with hypotension, tachypnea, altered mental status, mottling/poor perfusion, oliguria or rising lactate.
- Consider common surgical sources: intra-abdominal perforation/abscess, anastomotic leak, biliary sepsis, necrotizing infection, infected line or urinary obstruction.

### Sepsis management sequence
1. **Early recognition and ABC assessment.**
2. Measure lactate and evaluate perfusion; obtain microbiological samples **without significantly delaying antibiotics**.
3. **Prompt IV broad-spectrum antibiotics** guided by likely source and local antibiogram; tailor when evidence arrives.
4. **IV crystalloid resuscitation** for shock/hypoperfusion with repeated assessment for fluid overload/response.
5. **Norepinephrine** is the typical first-line vasopressor for persistent vasodilatory hypotension; **MAP ~65 mmHg** is the common initial target in the sources, individualized to patient.
6. **Source control**: drain pus, remove infected device, decompress obstructed infected system, operate for perforation/necrotic tissue when indicated.
7. Monitor organ support (oxygen/ventilation, renal function, glucose, thromboprophylaxis/nutrition when safe).

### Sepsis Six (revision mnemonic)
- **Give:** oxygen when needed, IV fluids when indicated, IV antibiotics.
- **Take/measure:** blood cultures, serum lactate, accurate urine output.
- Historical early goal-directed therapy charts emphasize CVP/ScvO₂ as universal targets; **do not treat these invasive pressure/saturation numbers as mandatory endpoints in every patient**.

### DIC in shock and sepsis
- Systemic activation of coagulation → **microvascular fibrin deposition** + **consumption of platelets/factors** → simultaneous thrombosis/organ injury and bleeding.
- Lab pattern often includes falling platelets, prolonged PT/aPTT, high fibrin-degradation markers (D-dimer), and falling fibrinogen (may vary early).
- Manage precipitating cause; blood component replacement guided by clinical bleeding/procedural needs rather than laboratory abnormality alone.

## 4. Blood groups, components and transfusion logic

### Blood products

| Product | Primary purpose | Major exam note |
|---|---|---|
| **Packed RBCs** | Restore oxygen-carrying capacity | Not a substitute for correcting iron deficiency in stable nonbleeding patients |
| **Platelet concentrate** | Treat/prevent clinically important thrombocytopenic bleeding | Assess platelet function, count and procedure |
| **Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)** | Replace multiple coagulation factors | Not a generic volume expander |
| **Cryoprecipitate** | Fibrinogen-rich product (also VIII, XIII and vWF) | Low fibrinogen in major hemorrhage/DIC |
| **Whole blood** | RBC, plasma and platelet components in appropriate systems | May be used in some trauma protocols |
| **Autologous blood / cell salvage** | Reduce exposure to donor blood in selected operations | Depends on contamination, equipment, protocol |

- **Before transfusion:** confirm identity, component, indication and compatibility; document informed consent where possible, baseline vitals, and observe for adverse response.
- **Crossmatch** for RBC transfusion; emergency uncrossmatched units require controlled protocol and proper follow-up.
- **RBC threshold:** WOR offers simplistic <6, 6–8, >8 g/dL statements. A transfusion decision depends on **active bleeding, shock, symptoms, cardiac disease and physiological context**; a rigid single hemoglobin cutoff is insufficient.

### Massive transfusion
- Classical definition: **>10 units RBC over 24 h**, **>4 units RBC in 1 h** with ongoing need, or replacing approximately one blood volume (definitions differ).
- **Balanced resuscitation:** commonly described using RBC:plasma:platelet strategies around **1:1:1** as a simplified teaching ratio; use local massive-transfusion protocol and laboratory-guided correction.
- **Complications:** hypothermia, dilutional/consumptive coagulopathy, hypocalcemia (**citrate binds Ca²⁺**), hypomagnesemia, potassium shifts, acid–base disturbances, pulmonary complications and volume overload.

## 5. Transfusion reactions — high-yield distinctions

| Reaction | Mechanism | Clinical pattern | First response concept |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acute hemolytic** | Most often ABO incompatibility; complement activation | Fever, chills, back/flank pain, hypotension, dark urine, DIC/AKI | **Stop transfusion**, support ABC/circulation, notify blood bank and investigate |
| **Febrile nonhemolytic** | Cytokines / recipient response to donor leukocytes | Fever/rigor without hemolysis | Exclude serious reaction first; leukoreduction reduces risk |
| **Allergic (mild)** | Reaction to donor plasma proteins | Pruritus/urticaria | Stop/assess; symptom treatment after serious causes excluded |
| **Anaphylactic** | Severe hypersensitivity, occasionally IgA-related | Bronchospasm, airway edema, severe hypotension | Stop transfusion, **IM epinephrine** and resuscitation |
| **TRALI** | Inflammatory noncardiogenic capillary leak, sometimes donor HLA/HNA antibodies | Acute hypoxemia and bilateral infiltrates generally within **6 h** | Stop; oxygen/respiratory support |
| **TACO** | Transfusion-associated **circulatory overload** | Dyspnea, pulmonary edema, raised filling pressures/volume signs | Stop/slow; upright/support breathing, diuresis where appropriate |
| **Septic transfusion reaction** | Bacterial contamination | Abrupt fever, rigor, severe shock | Stop, cultures, antibiotics/support |
| **Delayed hemolytic** | Anamnestic alloantibody response | Days later falling Hb, jaundice | Investigate antibody, manage anemia |

### TRALI vs TACO

| Feature | TRALI | TACO |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Inflammatory lung injury | Hydrostatic edema/volume overload |
| JVP/volume signs | Not typically elevated from overload | Often elevated |
| BNP and fluid balance | Not reliable alone | May support overload |
| CXR | Bilateral infiltrates | Bilateral edema ± cardiomegaly/effusions |
| Main treatment | Respiratory support | Respiratory support + judicious diuresis |

**Exam caution:** WOR describes a normal CXR for TACO; that is inconsistent with pulmonary edema presentations and should **not** be memorized as a defining property.

## 6. Anaphylactic and neurogenic shock quick approach
- **Anaphylaxis:** recognize after exposure, support airway, **IM epinephrine is the key first-line drug**, oxygen/fluids and adjuncts as indicated. Antihistamines do **not** replace epinephrine for life-threatening symptoms.
- **Neurogenic shock:** spinal injury with hypotension plus inappropriate bradycardia; maintain spinal precautions, volume only as needed and appropriate vasopressor support.

## 7. Worked mini-vignettes

1. **Trauma, HR 128, SBP 105, cold/clammy, narrow pulse pressure:** hemorrhage possible despite “acceptable” SBP; shock index ≈1.22 → source search and reassessment.
2. **Peritonitis + fever + MAP 55 + confusion:** suspect septic shock/organ dysfunction → antibiotics, fluids as appropriate, vasopressor, prompt abdominal source control.
3. **Transfusion then acute hypoxemia and bilateral infiltrates with flat neck veins:** consider **TRALI** over TACO, but assess alternative causes.
4. **High spinal cord injury + warm skin + HR 48 + hypotension:** **neurogenic shock**; exclude associated hemorrhage.
5. **Postoperative tachycardia, worsening pain, minimal drain output:** investigate concealed bleeding or leak rather than assume the drain rules out pathology.

### Chapter 05 — rapid revision
- **Cold shock:** hypovolemic/cardiogenic/obstructive. **Warm shock:** early septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic.
- **Neurogenic shock → bradycardia.**
- **Hypovolemic: CVP/PCWP ↓; cardiogenic: generally ↑; early sepsis: SVR ↓.**
- **Shock index = HR/SBP; lactate trends indicate tissue stress/perfusion, not an absolute cause.**
- **Sepsis management: antibiotics + hemodynamic support + source control.**
- **MTP:** coagulopathy, hypothermia, **citrate-induced hypocalcemia**.
- **TRALI = inflammatory noncardiogenic edema; TACO = hydrostatic overload.**
