# 25 — Pediatric Surgery

> [!info] Source basis

## 1. Approach to a neonate requiring surgery

- Identify **immediately correctable hazards**: airway obstruction, hypoxemia, shock, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, electrolyte disturbances and sepsis.
- The neonatal history is unusually important: **antenatal ultrasound**, polyhydramnios, gestational age, birth weight, timing of meconium passage, bilious/nonbilious vomiting, abdominal distension, feeding difficulties and syndromic abnormalities.
- **Bilious (green) vomiting** indicates obstruction distal to the ampulla until proven otherwise and warrants urgent exclusion of **malrotation with volvulus**. A normal-looking abdomen does not rule it out.
- Stabilization: keep warm, stop feeds if obstruction suspected, gastric decompression when indicated, IV fluid/glucose and electrolytes, appropriate analgesia/antibiotics when indicated; avoid unsafe delay in surgical referral.
- Imaging is chosen by clinical problem: plain abdominal radiograph (gas pattern), ultrasound (pylorus/intussusception and other indications), upper GI contrast study (malrotation in stable infant), contrast enema for selected distal obstruction, targeted CT/MRI sparingly.

## 2. Congenital abdominal-wall defects

| Feature | **Omphalocele / exomphalos** | **Gastroschisis** |
|---|---|---|
| Defect | **Midline at umbilical ring** | Typically **right of umbilicus** |
| Sac | **Present**: amnion + peritoneum with intervening tissue | **Absent**; exposed inflamed bowel |
| Umbilical cord | Inserts into the sac | Inserts normally, separate from defect |
| Contents | Bowel ± **liver** | Usually bowel; bowel can be thickened/matted |
| Associations | Chromosomal/syndromic and cardiac anomalies common | Intestinal atresia, dysfunction and short-bowel risk more prominent |
| Physiologic problem | Associated anomalies, visceroabdominal disproportion | Fluid/heat loss, bowel injury and intestinal dysmotility |
| Immediate action | Protect sac; assess anomalies; avoid rupture | Cover bowel with sterile protection, minimize heat/fluid losses and torsion |
| Definitive care | Primary or staged reduction/closure depending on size/condition | Primary or staged silo reduction/closure after stabilization |

- **Doctut size terminology:** minor exomphalos <5 cm vs major >5 cm, with liver more likely in major lesions. Size alone does not dictate a single operation.
- Physiologic danger of forced closure: **raised intra-abdominal pressure** compromises venous return, ventilation and bowel perfusion.
- **Exam shorthand:** sac + midline = omphalocele; right paraumbilical + uncovered bowel = gastroschisis.

## 3. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH)

### Anatomy and mechanism
- **Bochdalek:** posterolateral defect, typically **left-sided**; stomach/intestine enter thorax during fetal development.
- **Morgagni:** anterior parasternal defect (foramen of Morgagni/Larrey), commonly right-sided in teaching sources; transverse colon/omentum may herniate.
- Fetal compression limits lung development → **pulmonary hypoplasia** and pulmonary hypertension; these, rather than the hole alone, determine immediate survival.

### Presentation and diagnosis
- Respiratory distress immediately after birth; **scaphoid abdomen**, displaced cardiac sounds, asymmetric breath sounds; bowel loops in thorax on CXR; NG/OG tube may project into thorax.
- Differentiate from cystic congenital pulmonary lesions and congenital eventration.

### Management principles
1. Avoid **vigorous bag–mask ventilation** because it inflates bowel in thorax and worsens lung compression.
2. Secure airway with endotracheal ventilation when needed, use **gastric decompression**.
3. Stabilize oxygenation and pulmonary vascular physiology; evaluate cardiac abnormalities.
4. **Repair after physiologic stabilization**, rather than urgent repair before resuscitation; laparotomy/thoracoscopic approaches vary.

> [!warning] Source discrepancy
> One Doctut passage prescribes a fixed 24–48-hour “honeymoon” before repair. Preserve the teaching point—**stabilize before repair**—without treating the hour count as a universal threshold.

## 4. Esophageal atresia and tracheoesophageal fistula (EA/TEF)

### Clinical reasoning
- Interrupted upper esophagus causes **excess salivation, choking/coughing/cyanosis during feeds**; a nasogastric tube coils in the upper esophageal pouch.
- Distal TEF lets air pass from trachea into stomach → **gas-filled abdomen**; pure atresia typically yields **gasless abdomen**.
- Most frequent classic variant: **EA with distal TEF (Gross type C)**. Pure EA (type A), proximal TEF, double fistula and isolated H-type TEF form the remainder.
- Associated malformations: **VACTERL**—vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal and limb abnormalities (not all present in every child).

### Diagnostic and management logic
- Attempt gentle passage of appropriately sized feeding tube; obtain radiograph showing coiled tube; assess abdomen gas and associated cardiac/renal anomalies.
- **Before surgery:** stop oral feeds, elevate head, suction upper pouch, manage aspiration/respiratory status; avoid blindly forcing tube.
- **Definitive:** ligate/divide fistula and restore esophageal continuity when anatomically feasible; long-gap EA may require staged repair.
- Complications: anastomotic leak/stricture, recurrent fistula, dysmotility, GERD, tracheomalacia and aspiration.

## 5. Intestinal atresia and neonatal bowel obstruction

| Condition | Mechanism | Typical clue | Important management principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Duodenal atresia** | Failure of embryological recanalization (classic teaching) | **Double-bubble**, early vomiting; often Down syndrome/annular pancreas | Stabilize, **duodenoduodenostomy** |
| **Duodenal web/stenosis** | Incomplete luminal obstruction | Later or intermittent symptoms, some distal gas | Web excision or bypass when required |
| **Jejunoileal atresia** | **Intrauterine mesenteric vascular insult** | Multiple dilated bowel loops, bilious emesis, distal gas paucity | Resection of compromised segment + anastomosis/selected stoma |
| **Meconium ileus** | Inspissated meconium, often **cystic fibrosis** | Distal ileal obstruction, soap-bubble appearance | Selected contrast enema; surgery if complex/perforated |
| **Meconium plug / small-left-colon** | Functional distal obstruction | Failure to pass meconium | Enema/evaluate associated pathology |
| **Hirschsprung disease** | Congenital distal aganglionosis | Failure to pass meconium, distension | Biopsy and definitive pull-through |
| **Malrotation with volvulus** | Abnormal midgut fixation → twist at narrow mesenteric root | Sudden **bilious emesis**, sick infant | Emergency assessment and **Ladd operation** |

### Jejunoileal atresia: source classification
- **Type I:** mucosal web with intact bowel and mesentery.
- **Type II:** fibrous cord between blind ends; mesentery intact.
- **Type IIIa:** complete separation plus **V-shaped mesenteric defect**.
- **Type IIIb:** **apple-peel / Christmas-tree** bowel, distal bowel supplied by a retrograde collateral route, shortened bowel.
- **Type IV:** multiple atresias (**string-of-sausages** appearance in revision source).

### Duodenal atresia and double bubble
- Classic double-bubble represents gas-filled **stomach + proximal duodenum**.
- Presence of distal gas may suggest incomplete stenosis/web rather than complete atresia; other proximal obstructions can also produce a similar pattern.
- **Bilious vs nonbilious** emesis depends on whether obstruction lies distal or proximal to the ampulla.
- The Doctut assertion “most common intestinal atresia: duodenal according to Bailey, jejunoileal according to Sabiston” is explicitly source-dependent and not reconciled here.

## 6. Malrotation and midgut volvulus

- Embryonic midgut normally rotates and fixes around the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). Abnormal fixation creates **narrow mesenteric base**, predisposed to torsion.
- **Volvulus** obstructs venous then arterial supply → bowel ischemia/necrosis; bilious vomiting is the key warning.
- Investigation in a stable infant may use **upper GI contrast series**; classic abnormal duodenojejunal junction, corkscrew configuration; ultrasound may show **whirlpool sign**.
- **Ladd procedure:** detorse volvulus (typically counterclockwise), divide peritoneal Ladd bands, widen mesenteric base, position bowel to reduce future twist; often appendectomy to avoid confusing later appendix location.
- Viability of bowel and delayed second look may determine resection vs preservation.

## 7. Hirschsprung disease

### Pathogenesis
- Failed **neural crest migration** → missing submucosal (**Meissner**) and myenteric (**Auerbach**) ganglion cells in distal bowel.
- Aganglionic segment cannot relax normally → **functional distal obstruction** and proximal colonic dilatation (**congenital aganglionic megacolon**).
- Most frequent involvement is **rectosigmoid**; long-segment and total colonic disease also occur.
- Associations include **Down syndrome** and **RET-pathway** abnormalities (multiple inherited patterns).

### Presentation
- Delayed passage of meconium (>48 hours in term neonate), distension and bilious vomiting; older children with longstanding constipation.
- **Rectal examination:** tight empty rectum; explosive fecal/gas passage after withdrawal may occur.
- Red flag: **Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis**—fever, distension, explosive diarrhea, sepsis; urgent care.

### Investigation
- **Contrast enema:** transition zone—narrow aganglionic distal segment, dilated proximal bowel; can be falsely negative in early neonatal period.
- **Anorectal manometry:** absent **rectoanal inhibitory reflex (RAIR)**.
- **Definitive diagnosis = rectal suction biopsy** demonstrating absent ganglion cells and hypertrophic nerve fibers; **acetylcholinesterase staining** classically increased (calretinin patterns also discussed in pathology literature).

### Treatment
- Stabilization, decompression/rectal washouts, treat enterocolitis; selected diversion when needed.
- Definitive **pull-through procedure** removes/bypasses aganglionic bowel and anastomoses ganglionated bowel close to anus (Swenson, Soave, Duhamel approaches).
- Complications: persistent constipation, enterocolitis, stricture, continence problems.

## 8. Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS)

- Infant typically **2–8 weeks** with progressive **projectile nonbilious vomiting**, hunger after vomiting, dehydration and weight faltering.
- Hypertrophied pyloric circular smooth muscle → gastric outlet obstruction.
- **Palpable olive** in right epigastrium; visible gastric peristalsis may be observed.
- **Hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis** results from gastric HCl losses (severe cases may develop paradoxical aciduria).
- **Ultrasound** usually diagnostic; source clinical cutoffs vary by age/technique and should not be used as rigid absolutes.
- **Treat dehydration/electrolytes before operation**; definitive **Ramstedt pyloromyotomy** (muscle splitting without mucosal perforation).

## 9. Intussusception

- Telescoping of proximal bowel into distal bowel, usually **ileocolic**; lymphoid hyperplasia common after viral illness.
- Intermittent colicky pain with leg flexion, vomiting; **currant-jelly stool is a late sign**; sausage-shaped abdominal mass, empty right iliac fossa (**Dance sign**).
- Ultrasound: **target/doughnut sign** (transverse) and **pseudokidney** (longitudinal).
- Stable uncomplicated cases: **air/hydrostatic enema reduction** with surgical backup. Peritonitis, perforation or failed reduction → surgery.
- In older child, search for a **pathologic lead point** (Meckel diverticulum, polyp, lymphoma).

## 10. Anorectal malformations (ARM)

- Spectrum from perineal fistula/imperforate anus to complex high malformations and cloacal anomalies.
- Examine perineum, passage of meconium via urethra/vagina, sacrum and urinary tract; evaluate **VACTERL** associations.
- Avoid forcing rectal instrumentation through unknown anatomy. Imaging/specialist assessment defines fistula and rectal pouch.
- Treatment varies from primary repair for low lesions to staged colostomy and **posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP)** for complex anatomy.
- Long-term outcomes: continence, constipation, urinary/reproductive function and pelvic-floor development.

## 11. Biliary atresia and neonatal cholestasis

- Progressive inflammatory obliteration of extrahepatic bile ducts → conjugated jaundice, **pale stools, dark urine**, hepatomegaly and cirrhosis.
- **Conjugated jaundice is never physiologic**; distinguish from breast-milk jaundice.
- Investigations in context: fractionated bilirubin, ultrasound, hepatobiliary imaging and selected liver biopsy/cholangiography.
- Definitive early operation: **Kasai portoenterostomy** to facilitate bile drainage; early referral is critical, with results generally better when performed early.
- Progressive failure may require **liver transplant**; see Chapter 27.

## 12. Pediatric hernia, hydrocele and other congenital conditions

- **Indirect inguinal hernia** from patent processus vaginalis: risk of incarceration; **herniotomy** is usual pediatric repair, not mesh by default.
- **Communicating hydrocele:** same persistent processus; source suggests observation early in life and surgery around 2 years if persistent, but timing depends on features/hernia concern.
- **Umbilical hernia:** many close spontaneously; source suggests observation until ~5 years if uncomplicated.
- **Hypospadias:** ventral urethral meatus, hooded foreskin and possible chordee; **avoid circumcision before reconstructive assessment** because foreskin may be needed. Source gives repair broadly 6 months–2 years.

## 13. Pediatric oncology cross-links

- Doctut emphasizes **neuroblastoma vs Wilms tumor**: neuroblastoma often crosses midline, calcifies, encases vessels and elevates urinary **VMA/HVA**; Wilms is an intrarenal mass often displacing vessels.
- The material belongs primarily in pediatric oncology; this chapter retains the surgical differential rather than duplicating complete chemotherapy/staging regimens.
