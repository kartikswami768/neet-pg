# Chapter 02: Primary & Secondary Dermatological Lesions

Accurate description of lesion morphology is the first step in clinical dermatology. A lesion should be characterised by its **primary or secondary nature, surface contour, palpability, size, contents, anatomical depth, distribution, colour, and evolution**. Morphology narrows the differential diagnosis but must be interpreted with the history, distribution, symptoms, and examination of the rest of the skin and mucosae.

## 2.1 Structural Foundations and Classification Principles

The skin has three principal anatomical compartments:

1. **Epidermis** — superficial, avascular, keratinising stratified squamous epithelium.
2. **Dermis** — collagen-rich connective tissue containing vessels, nerves, inflammatory cells, and skin appendages.
3. **Subcutis (hypodermis)** — deeper adipose tissue divided by fibrous septa.

Primary lesions are the initial, direct morphological manifestations of a disease process. Secondary lesions develop through evolution, rupture, shedding, trauma, scratching, infection, or healing of primary lesions. Vascular or extravasation lesions are described separately because their defining feature is whether blood remains within vessels or has entered the surrounding tissue.

Primary lesions are classified by:

- **Elevation:** flat, raised, or depressed.
- **Consistency and contents:** solid, fluid-filled, purulent, or oedematous.
- **Size and horizontal extent:** for many conventional clinical definitions, the dividing threshold is approximately **1 cm**. Some texts and lesion-classification systems use **5 mm** for selected categories; state the convention being used when a borderline lesion is described.
- **Tissue compartment:** epidermis, dermis, dermoepidermal junction, or subcutis.

A lesion's depth is particularly useful when distinguishing a superficial papule from a deeper nodule, and an intraepidermal blister from a subepidermal blister.

## 2.2 Primary Skin Lesions

Primary lesions arise directly from the underlying pathological process. They may be flat, solid and elevated, fluid-filled, purulent, or oedematous.

### 2.2.1 Flat, Non-Palpable Colour Changes

#### Macule

A **macule** is a flat, circumscribed, non-palpable area of colour change without elevation or depression relative to the surrounding skin. It is conventionally **less than 1 cm** in greatest diameter; some classification systems use a smaller cutoff, such as 5 mm.

Macules reflect a change in pigment or vascular colour without a meaningful increase in tissue volume. Important mechanisms include:

- **Hyperpigmentation:** increased melanin in the epidermis or increased melanophages in the dermis.
- **Hypopigmentation or depigmentation:** reduced or absent epidermal melanin, as in early vitiligo or some early leprosy lesions.
- **Vascular change:** dilatation of superficial vessels causing erythema, or colour change related to blood-derived pigment.

**Clinical examples**

- **Ephelis (freckle):** small, usually 1–10 mm, hyperpigmented macule on sun-exposed skin; pigmentation darkens with ultraviolet exposure and may fade during periods of reduced sun exposure.
- **Junctional melanocytic nevus:** flat, usually uniform tan-to-brown macule; nevus-cell nests are confined to the dermoepidermal junction.
- **Labial melanotic macule:** localised, usually benign mucosal pigmentation.
- **Early vitiligo:** focal depigmented macule caused by loss of functional melanocytes.
- **Indeterminate leprosy:** may present as an ill-defined hypopigmented macule with subtle or early sensory impairment.

#### Patch

A **patch** is a flat, non-palpable area of colour change that is morphologically similar to a macule but is conventionally **greater than 1 cm** in diameter (with some sources using 5 mm as the dividing threshold). The surface remains smooth and is neither elevated nor depressed.

Patches arise when the pigmentary, vascular, or inflammatory change extends over a larger horizontal field. Mechanisms include widespread melanocyte destruction, vascular malformation, and regional inflammatory or metabolic change.

**Clinical examples**

- **Vitiligo:** sharply demarcated depigmented patches due to autoimmune destruction of epidermal melanocytes.
- **Pityriasis rosea herald patch:** typically an oval erythematous patch, often 2–10 cm, with a collarette of scale along its inner margin; it precedes the more widespread eruption.
- **Stasis dermatitis:** erythematous to hyperpigmented eczematous patches on the lower legs in chronic venous insufficiency, with hemosiderin contributing to pigmentation.
- **Café-au-lait macule/patch:** uniform light-brown pigmentation; size and number help determine its clinical significance.

### 2.2.2 Solid, Elevated Lesions

#### Papule

A **papule** is a small, circumscribed, solid, elevated and palpable lesion, conventionally **less than 1 cm** in diameter. It usually involves the epidermis or superficial papillary dermis. In the classic morphological distinction, its horizontal width is greater than or equal to its vertical depth; a deeper lesion with a substantial dermal or subcutaneous component is more appropriately described as a nodule.

Papules may develop through several mechanisms:

1. **Epidermal hyperplasia:** focal acanthosis or hyperkeratosis.
2. **Dermal cellular infiltration:** accumulation of lymphocytes, histiocytes, eosinophils, or other inflammatory cells in the papillary dermis.
3. **Follicular plugging:** retained keratin and sebum in a pilosebaceous opening.
4. **Metabolic deposition:** accumulation of material such as amyloid, mucin, or lipid in the skin.

**Clinical examples**

- **Acne vulgaris:** inflammatory papules associated with an obstructed and inflamed pilosebaceous unit.
- **Verruca vulgaris:** hyperkeratotic papules caused by human papillomavirus, with epidermal hyperplasia and koilocytosis.
- **Lichen planus:** pruritic, violaceous, polygonal, flat-topped papules; fine white Wickham striae may be visible.
- **Pityriasis rubra pilaris:** red-orange follicular papules with central keratin plugs, producing a characteristic rough or “nutmeg-grater” feel on the dorsum of the hands.

#### Plaque

A **plaque** is a broad, elevated, solid, usually flat-topped lesion, conventionally **greater than 1 cm** in diameter. It may develop as a primary plateau-like elevation or through the confluence of adjacent papules. Its horizontal surface area is large relative to its height.

Plaques commonly reflect extensive epidermal hyperplasia, hyperkeratosis, and/or dermal inflammatory infiltration. Depending on the disease, histology may show acanthosis, elongation of rete ridges, parakeratosis, and a dense inflammatory infiltrate.

**Clinical examples**

- **Chronic plaque psoriasis:** sharply demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery-white scale, commonly on extensor surfaces, scalp, and lumbosacral region. Histology may show parakeratosis, regular elongation of rete ridges, thinning of suprapapillary plates, Munro microabscesses, and Kogoj spongiform pustules. Scraping may produce the Auspitz sign.
- **Nummular (discoid) eczema:** coin-shaped, intensely pruritic erythematous plaques, sometimes with vesicles, oozing, or crust.
- **Lupus vulgaris:** reddish-brown plaque that may reveal apple-jelly nodules on diascopy and may expand peripherally with central atrophy and scarring.

#### Nodule

A **nodule** is a solid, elevated lesion with a substantial dermal or subcutaneous component. It is usually larger than a papule, often around **1 cm or more**, although size thresholds vary. The defining feature is depth: the vertical depth is greater than, or at least comparable to, the lesion's surface dimensions.

**Clinical examples:** dermatofibroma, rheumatoid nodule, erythema nodosum, and keratoacanthoma.

### 2.2.3 Fluid-Filled Lesions

#### Vesicle

A **vesicle** is a small, circumscribed, elevated, fluid-filled lesion, conventionally **less than 1 cm** in diameter; some systems use a 5 mm cutoff. Its contents are usually clear or serous, although they may appear pale yellow.

Vesicles form when fluid accumulates within or beneath the epidermis. Important mechanisms include:

- **Spongiosis:** intercellular epidermal oedema stretches the spaces between keratinocytes and may form intraepidermal microvesicles, classically in acute eczema.
- **Ballooning degeneration and viral cytopathic change:** viral infection can cause keratinocyte swelling, intracellular oedema, loss of cellular cohesion, and vesicle formation.
- **Acantholysis:** loss of desmosomal adhesion can create an intraepidermal cleft.
- **Subepidermal separation:** cleavage at the dermoepidermal junction can also produce fluid-filled lesions.

**Clinical examples**

- **Varicella and herpes zoster:** grouped vesicles on an erythematous base; zoster lesions follow a dermatome.
- **Herpes labialis:** recurrent grouped painful vesicles near the vermilion border.
- **Acute eczematous dermatitis:** intensely pruritic, sometimes weeping vesicles associated with spongiosis.

#### Bulla

A **bulla** is a large, circumscribed, elevated, fluid-filled blister, conventionally **greater than 1 cm** in diameter (some systems use 5 mm). Contents may be serous, serosanguinous, or haemorrhagic. Bullae may be flaccid or tense depending mainly on the level of tissue cleavage.

| Feature | Intraepidermal blister | Subepidermal blister |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Principal structure affected | Desmosomal adhesion, commonly desmoglein 1 and/or 3. | Hemidesmosomal or basement-membrane-zone proteins, such as BP180 and BP230. |
| Typical split | Within the epidermis; pemphigus vulgaris classically produces a suprabasal split. | Beneath the epidermis at the basement membrane zone. |
| Clinical character | Usually flaccid and fragile; ruptures readily. | Usually tense and more resistant to rupture because the full epidermis forms the blister roof. |
| Nikolsky sign | Often positive in pemphigus disorders. | Usually negative in bullous pemphigoid. |
| Examples | Pemphigus vulgaris, pemphigus foliaceus, bullous impetigo. | Bullous pemphigoid; dermatitis herpetiformis produces a subepidermal blistering pattern by a different immune mechanism. |

**Clinical correlates**

- **Pemphigus vulgaris:** autoantibodies against desmoglein 3, with or without desmoglein 1, cause acantholysis and a suprabasal split. Basal keratinocytes remain attached to the basement membrane, producing the “row of tombstones” appearance. Bullae are flaccid and rupture readily, often leaving erosions; Nikolsky and bulla-spread signs may be positive.
- **Bullous pemphigoid:** antibodies target basement-membrane-zone proteins, particularly BP180 and BP230. The intact epidermis forms a thick blister roof, producing tense bullae; Nikolsky sign is usually negative and eosinophils are commonly seen.
- **Bullous impetigo:** exfoliative toxins from *Staphylococcus aureus* cleave desmoglein 1, producing superficial, flaccid bullae that rupture and crust.

### 2.2.4 Pus-Filled Lesions

#### Pustule

A **pustule** is a circumscribed, elevated lesion containing purulent material. Pus consists mainly of neutrophils, cellular debris, and plasma proteins. Pustules are often small, from pinpoint size to less than 1 cm; a larger, deeper collection is more appropriately termed an abscess.

Pustules arise through neutrophil recruitment into the epidermis, subcorneal space, or pilosebaceous unit. They may be **infectious** (for example, bacterial or fungal) or **sterile** (for example, pustular psoriasis or some drug-related eruptions).

**Clinical examples**

- **Pustular psoriasis:** sterile pustules, often subcorneal; Kogoj spongiform pustules are a characteristic histological finding.
- **Acne vulgaris:** inflammatory pustules within pilosebaceous units.
- **Papulopustular rosacea:** central facial erythema with papules and pustules, characteristically without comedones.
- **Cutaneous anthrax:** sometimes historically termed a “malignant pustule”, but the lesion is not a true pus-filled pustule; it evolves toward a central black eschar, often with surrounding oedema and vesicles.

#### Abscess

An **abscess** is a localised collection of purulent material in the dermis or subcutaneous tissue. It is typically tender, warm, fluctuant, and surrounded by inflammatory erythema. Unlike a superficial pustule, an abscess is a deeper, space-occupying collection and may require drainage as part of treatment.

### 2.2.5 Oedematous and Encapsulated Lesions

#### Wheal

A **wheal (urtica)** is a transient, elevated, usually pruritic area of dermal oedema. It may appear as a small papule or a large coalescent plaque.

Wheals result from IgE-mediated or non-IgE-mediated mast-cell activation. Histamine and other mediators, including leukotrienes, cause vasodilatation and increased permeability of post-capillary venules. Plasma enters the superficial dermis, producing local oedema and sometimes lymphatic dilatation.

**Key diagnostic features**

- **Evanescence:** an individual wheal usually resolves completely within 24 hours in the same location, without scarring or residual pigmentation.
- **Pruritus:** often intense.
- **Colour:** typically a blanchable central pallor with a surrounding erythematous flare.

**Clinical examples:** acute urticaria triggered by foods, medicines, insect stings, or physical stimuli; dermographism; cold- or heat-induced urticaria.

#### Cyst

A **cyst** is an elevated, circumscribed cavity with a true epithelial lining and a lumen containing liquid or semisolid material.

- **Epidermoid cyst:** commonly occurs on the face, neck, and trunk; often has a central punctum and contains cheesy keratinaceous material. Histologically, the lining is stratified squamous epithelium with a granular layer.
- **Trichilemmal (pilar) cyst:** commonly occurs on the scalp; usually lacks a central punctum. Histologically, the lining lacks a granular layer and shows abrupt trichilemmal keratinisation; the contents are compact keratin.

## 2.3 Secondary Skin Lesions

Secondary lesions develop through evolution, rupture, shedding, trauma, scratching, chronic friction, infection, or healing of primary lesions.

### 2.3.1 Scale

A **scale** is visible flaking or shedding of corneocytes from the stratum corneum. It may result from abnormal keratinisation, accelerated epidermal turnover, or delayed shedding of the outer horny layer.

Important mechanisms include:

- **Hyperkeratosis:** thickening of the stratum corneum.
- **Parakeratosis:** retention of pyknotic nuclei in the stratum corneum, often reflecting rapid epidermal transit and incomplete cornification.
- Changes in the granular and spinous layers may contribute to scaling and overall epidermal thickening.

**Characteristic scale patterns**

- **Silvery-white (micaceous) scale:** thick, profuse scale of psoriasis; scraping may produce the candle-drop sign followed by the Auspitz sign.
- **Collarette scale:** a ring of scale whose inner edge is free while its outer edge remains attached; characteristic of pityriasis rosea.
- **Greasy yellow scale:** oily scale on erythematous skin in seborrhoeic areas such as the scalp, eyebrows, and nasolabial folds; characteristic of seborrhoeic dermatitis and cradle cap.
- **Fish-like (ichthyosiform) scale:** large, dry, polygonal scales, classically seen in ichthyosis vulgaris associated with *FLG* (filaggrin) defects.
- **Asbestos-like scale (pityriasis amiantacea):** thick, adherent, sheet-like scale binding proximal hair shafts into bundles.
- **Waxy or sandal keratoderma with nutmeg-grater papules:** waxy orange-yellow palmoplantar thickening together with red follicular papules bearing central keratin plugs on the dorsum of the hands; characteristic of pityriasis rubra pilaris.

### 2.3.2 Crust

A **crust** is dried exudate on the skin surface, composed of serum, blood, pus, or cellular debris. It develops when a vesicle, bulla, or pustule ruptures, or when trauma or inflammation disrupts the epidermal surface and the exudate dries in air.

**Clinical variations**

- **Honey-coloured (meliceric) crust:** dried purulent exudate, classically seen in non-bullous impetigo caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* and/or *Streptococcus pyogenes*.
- **Haemorrhagic crust:** dark red to black crust formed from dried blood, for example after trauma, severe excoriation, or necrotising inflammation.
- **Serous crust:** clear or yellowish dried serum over an oozing lesion, commonly in acute eczematous dermatitis.

### 2.3.3 Excoriation

An **excoriation** is a linear or punctate traumatic loss of epidermis caused by scratching, picking, or rubbing. It commonly follows intense pruritus. Fingernails remove the stratum corneum and may disrupt the superficial epidermis; superficial excoriations generally heal without a scar, although deeper injury or secondary infection may complicate healing.

**Clinical correlates:** scratch marks in atopic dermatitis, scabies, pediculosis, and other pruritic disorders; self-induced geometric erosions or excoriations in dermatitis artefacta and neurotic excoriations.

### 2.3.4 Lichenification

**Lichenification** is thickened, rough, leathery skin with hyperpigmentation and accentuated, deepened skin markings. It develops after repeated rubbing or scratching over a prolonged period.

The classic clinical triad is:

1. Increased skin thickness and induration.
2. Increased pigmentation, often post-inflammatory.
3. Exaggerated skin lines and deepened grid-like markings.

Histologically, lichenification shows marked acanthosis, hyperkeratosis, broadening and elongation of rete ridges, and sometimes papillary dermal fibrosis.

**Clinical examples:** lichen simplex chronicus, chronic atopic dermatitis, and chronic stasis dermatitis.

### 2.3.5 Tissue-Loss Lesions: Erosion, Ulcer, and Fissure

- **Erosion:** partial loss of the epidermis. The basement membrane remains intact, so healing is usually without scarring. Example: a ruptured superficial blister.
- **Ulcer:** full-thickness loss of the epidermis extending into the dermis or subcutis, with disruption of the basement membrane. Healing generally occurs with scarring. Examples include arterial, venous, neuropathic diabetic, and pyoderma gangrenosum ulcers.
- **Fissure:** a linear crack or cleft that extends through the epidermis and may reach the dermis. Examples include palmoplantar eczema, angular cheilitis, and perianal fissures. Scarring depends on depth and chronicity.

## 2.4 Vascular and Extravasation Lesions

Red or purple lesions should be assessed for **blanching**. Diascopy (vitropression) using a transparent glass slide can help distinguish intravascular redness from extravasated blood.

| Feature | Blanchable lesions | Non-blanchable lesions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mechanism | Blood remains inside dilated superficial vessels and is displaced by pressure. | Erythrocytes have left the vascular lumen and remain in the dermis or subcutis. |
| Examples | Erythema and telangiectasia. | Petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses, and haematomas. |
| Clinical significance | Supports an intravascular process; diascopy may help clarify the colour change. | Supports extravasation; palpable purpura raises concern for inflammatory vessel injury such as small-vessel vasculitis. |

### 2.4.1 Petechiae, Purpura, Ecchymoses, and Haematoma

| Lesion | Common size convention | Morphology / palpability | Typical associations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Petechiae** | Less than 2 mm. | Pinpoint, non-blanching red-purple macules. | Thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, meningococcaemia, and other causes of capillary bleeding. |
| **Purpura** | Approximately 2–5 mm in one common scheme; some references use a broader range (for example, 3–10 mm). | Non-blanching red-purple macules; may be palpable when associated with vascular inflammation. | Thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, and small-vessel vasculitis. |
| **Ecchymosis** | Greater than 5 mm in one convention; some references reserve the term for lesions greater than 1 cm. | Larger, usually flat area of extravasated blood (bruise). | Trauma, thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, anticoagulant effect, and other coagulopathies. |
| **Haematoma** | Variable. | Localised, often palpable or fluctuant collection of blood within dermis, subcutis, or deeper fascial planes. | Blunt trauma, vessel injury, or severe bleeding tendency. |

The size cutoffs vary between references; the most important distinction is morphology and the presence of extravasated blood rather than treating a single measurement as universal.

Because extravasated erythrocytes lie outside vessels, petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses, and haematomas do **not** blanch with pressure. In contrast, erythema caused by vascular dilatation generally blanches.

**Clinical correlates and evolution**

- **Palpable purpura:** suggests inflammatory injury of small cutaneous vessels, classically leukocytoclastic vasculitis. Immune-complex-mediated vascular damage allows erythrocytes to extravasate and produces palpable inflammatory elevation.
- **Traumatic haematoma:** rupture of subcutaneous vessels after blunt trauma may produce a painful, fluctuant, expanding collection.
- **Ecchymosis:** may follow trauma or occur with thrombocytopenia and systemic coagulation disorders.
- **Colour evolution of a bruise:** fresh blood appears red or blue-purple; haem breakdown produces biliverdin (green), followed by bilirubin and hemosiderin-associated yellow-brown discolouration.

**Telangiectasia** is a visible, persistent dilatation of superficial small vessels (capillaries or venules). It is generally blanchable because the blood remains within the vascular lumen.
