# Orthopaedics Chapter 03: Lower Limb Trauma, Pelvic Ring Disruptions & Foot Pathology

## 1. Pelvic Ring Disruptions & Acetabular Fractures

### 1.1 Biomechanics of Pelvic Ring Stability
The pelvic ring is a rigid biomechanical structure composed of the sacrum, ilium, ischium, and pubis, bound together by strong ligamentous complexes (posterior sacroiliac, sacrotuberous, sacrospinous, and pubic symphyseal ligaments). Anatomically, the pelvic ring functions analogous to a pretzel: a disruption in one part of the ring is almost invariably accompanied by a second break or ligamentous disruption elsewhere in the circuit.

Pelvic ring disruptions are classified using two primary biomechanical systems:

* **Young-Burgess Classification** (Based on force vector mechanism):
  1. *Anteroposterior Compression (APC)*: Caused by head-on collisions or crush injuries.
     - **APC I**: Diastasis of pubic symphysis $<2.5\text{ cm}$ with intact posterior ligaments; stable.
     - **APC II ("Open-Book Pelvis")**: Diastasis $>2.5\text{ cm}$ with disruption of pubic symphysis, anterior sacroiliac, sacrospinous, and sacrotuberous ligaments. Posterior sacroiliac ligaments remain intact; rotationally unstable, vertically stable.
     - **APC III**: Complete disruption of pubic symphysis and both anterior/posterior sacroiliac ligaments; rotationally and vertically unstable.
  2. *Lateral Compression (LC)*: Caused by side-impact motor vehicle accidents or lateral falls.
     - **LC I**: Sacral crush fracture on side of impact with ipsilateral pubic ramus fracture; stable.
     - **LC II**: Crescent fracture of iliac wing on side of impact; rotationally unstable.
     - **LC III ("Windswept Pelvis")**: Ipsilateral LC fracture combined with contralateral APC ("open-book") injury.
  3. *Vertical Shear (VS)*: Caused by high-energy vertical axial loading (e.g., fall from height landing on an extended lower extremity). Complete disruption of anterior and posterior sacroiliac complex; gross rotational and vertical instability with vertical displacement of the hemipelvis.

* **Tile Classification** (Based on mechanical stability):
  - **Type A**: Stable (pelvic ring intact; e.g., avulsion fractures of ASIS/AIIS, isolated ramus fractures).
  - **Type B**: Rotationally unstable, vertically stable (incomplete posterior disruption; APC II, LC II).
  - **Type C**: Rotationally and vertically unstable (complete posterior disruption; APC III, VS).

### 1.2 Acute Emergency Complications of Pelvic Fractures
High-energy pelvic disruptions represent life-threatening surgical emergencies due to severe retroperitoneal hemorrhage and associated pelvic visceral trauma.

* **Retroperitoneal Hemorrhage**: The primary cause of death in severe pelvic fractures. Up to $90\%$ of pelvic bleeding originates from low-pressure **presacral and lumbar venous plexuses** and fractured cancellous bone surfaces, while $10\%$ arises from arterial disruption (branches of internal iliac artery, particularly the **superior gluteal artery** and **internal pudendal artery**).
  - *Immediate Stabilization*: Application of a pelvic binder or sheet centered over the **greater trochanters** (not the iliac crests) to reduce pelvic volume, re-approximate bone surfaces, and promote venous tamponade.
* **Urethral & Bladder Injuries**:
  - *Posterior Urethral Disruption*: Highly prevalent in males with pubic ramus fractures or symphyseal diastasis; occurs at the **membranous urethral junction** (prostatomembranous disruption). Classical triad includes blood at the external urethral meatus, high-riding prostate on digital rectal examination (DRE), and inability to void. Retrograde Urethrography (RUG) must be performed prior to blind Foley catheterization.
  - *Bladder Rupture*: Can be extraperitoneal (associated with pubic ramus spicules; managed conservatively with catheter drainage) or intraperitoneal (dome rupture due to burst injury of full bladder; requires immediate open surgical repair).

### 1.3 Acetabular Fractures & Surgical Approaches
Acetabular fractures are intra-articular disruptions of the hip socket resulting from axial loads transmitted through the head of the femur.

* **Judet-Letournel Classification**: Categorizes fractures into 5 Elementary Types (Posterior Wall, Posterior Column, Anterior Wall, Anterior Column, Transverse) and 5 Associated Types (T-Shaped, Posterior Column + Wall, Transverse + Wall, Anterior + Posterior Hecht, Both Column).
  - *Both Column Fracture*: Characterized by "floating acetabulum" with complete separation of all articular segments from the posterior ilium; pathognomonic **Spur Sign** seen on obturator oblique radiograph.
* **Surgical Approaches**:
  - *Kocher-Langenbeck Approach*: Posterior approach utilized for posterior wall and posterior column fractures.
  - *Ilioinguinal / Anterior Intrapelvic (Modified Stoppa) Approach*: Utilized for anterior column and anterior wall fractures.

## 2. Femoral Neck & Intertrochanteric Fractures

### 2.1 Intracapsular Femoral Neck Fractures
Femoral neck fractures are intracapsular injuries occurring between the articular margin of the femoral head and the intertrochanteric line. They are common in elderly osteoporotic women following low-energy falls and in young adults following high-energy trauma.

#### Vascular Anatomy & Pathophysiology of Necrosis
The blood supply to the femoral head relies primarily on an ascending cervical retinacular arterial ring derived from the **Medial Femoral Circumflex Artery (MFCA)** (specifically its deep branch). These retinacular vessels run subperiosteally along the femoral neck inside the joint capsule. Displacement of an intracapsular femoral neck fracture tears these retinacular vessels, cutting off retrograde blood flow to the femoral head and placing the patient at extreme risk of **Avascular Necrosis (AVN)** ($20\text{--}35\%$) and **Non-Union** ($15\text{--}30\%$). Intracapsular fluid pressure (hemarthrosis) further tamponades residual microvascular perfusion.

#### Classification Systems

* **Garden Classification** (Evaluates displacement on AP pelvic radiograph):
  - **Stage I**: Incomplete or valgus-impacted fracture; trabeculae of femoral head aligned in valgus angle ($>160^\circ$). Stable.
  - **Stage II**: Complete fracture without displacement; trabecular alignment preserved ($160^\circ$).
  - **Stage III**: Complete fracture with partial displacement ($<50\%$); medial trabeculae of femoral head out of alignment with acetabulum.
  - **Stage IV**: Complete fracture with total displacement ($100\%$); femoral head trabeculae re-align normally in acetabulum while shaft flexes and externally rotates proximally.

* **Pauwels Classification** (Evaluates angle of fracture line relative to horizontal plane):
  - **Type I**: Fracture angle $<30^\circ$. Dominant force is compressive; highly stable, high union rate.
  - **Type II**: Fracture angle $30^\circ\text{--}50^\circ$. Combined compressive and shear forces.
  - **Type III**: Fracture angle $>50^\circ$. Dominant force is vertical shear; highly unstable, high non-union and displacement rate.

#### Surgical Management Protocols
* **Young / Active Patients (Age $<60\text{--}65$ years)**: Preserve native femoral head regardless of displacement. Emergency closed/open reduction and rigid internal fixation using **3 parallel Cannulated Cancellous Screws (CCS)** arranged in an inverted triangle pattern, or a Dynamic Hip Screw (DHS) with an anti-rotation screw.
* **Elderly Patients (Age $>65$ years)**:
  - *Un-displaced (Garden I & II)*: In-situ fixation with 3 cannulated cancellous screws.
  - *Displaced (Garden III & IV)*: High risk of AVN and non-union makes head preservation impractical. Arthroplasty is indicated:
    - **Hemiarthroplasty** (Unipolar or Bipolar): Indicated for low-demand, frail elderly individuals.
    - **Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA)**: Indicated for active, independent elderly individuals without cognitive impairment.

### 2.2 Extracapsular Intertrochanteric Fractures
Intertrochanteric fractures occur in the cancellous region between the greater and lesser trochanters, outside the hip joint capsule.

* **Pathophysiology**: Because this region consists of dense, well-vascularized cancellous bone surrounded by an abundant muscular bed, **avascular necrosis and non-union are rare** ($<1\%$). However, because the fracture is exposed to powerful muscular pull (iliopsoas on lesser trochanter pulling proximally/internally, gluteus medius on greater trochanter pulling laterally, adductors on shaft pulling medially), malunion in **coxa vara** and shortening are common complications.
* **Classification**: Classified as Stable vs. Unstable (Evans and AO/OTA systems). Unstability is defined by loss of posteromedial cortical support (comminution of lesser trochanter) or reverse obliquity pattern.
* **Surgical Fixation**:
  - *Stable Fractures*: **Dynamic Hip Screw (DHS)** with sideplate.
  - *Unstable / Reverse Obliquity Fractures*: **Proximal Femoral Nailing (PFN)** or Long Intramedullary Nail (load-sharing device providing superior biomechanical resistance against collapse).

## 3. Hip Dislocations & Femoral Shaft Trauma

### 3.1 Hip Dislocations
Hip dislocations require severe high-energy kinetic impact and represent orthopedic emergencies requiring prompt reduction within 6 hours to minimize the risk of secondary femoral head avascular necrosis.

| Clinical Feature | Posterior Dislocation (90%) | Anterior Dislocation (10%) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mechanism of Injury** | "Dashboard injury" (axial impact to flexed knee in MVA) | Forced abduction, external rotation, and extension |
| **Limb Posture** | **Flexed, Adducted, and Internally Rotated** | **Flexed, Abducted, and Externally Rotated** |
| **Apparent Length** | True Shortening | Apparent Lengthening / Shortening depending on pubic vs obturator type |
| **Palpable Head** | Prominent posterolateral gluteal mass | Prominent mass in femoral triangle / groin |
| **Nerve at Risk** | **Sciatic Nerve** (Peroneal division > Tibial division) | Femoral Nerve / Obturator Nerve |
| **Radiological Findings** | Femoral head lies superior/lateral to acetabulum | Femoral head lies inferior/medial to acetabulum |
| **Eponymous Lesions** | Posterior acetabular rim wall fracture (Thompson-Epstein) | Femoral head indentation fracture (Pipkin classification) |

* **Reduction Techniques**: Allendorf maneuver, Stimson's gravity technique, Bigelow's maneuver.

### 3.2 Femoral Shaft Fractures & Fat Embolism Syndrome
Femoral shaft fractures result from high-velocity blunt trauma.

* **Vascular Loss**: Associated with substantial internal hemorrhage within the tight thigh compartment, leading to acute blood loss of **1.0 to 1.5 Liters per femur**.
* **Management**: Closed reduction and definitive fixation via **Reamed Intramedullary Interlocking Nailing (IMN)** within 24 hours (Damage Control Orthopedics).
* **Fat Embolism Syndrome (FES)**:
  - *Pathophysiology*: Disruption of intramedullary fat droplets into damaged venous sinusoids during fracture or reaming. Microemboli occlude pulmonary capillaries and undergo hydrolysis by serum lipase into toxic free fatty acids, causing capillary endothelial damage.
  - *Clinical Triad* (Occurs 24–72 hours post-injury):
    1. **Respiratory Distress**: Hypoxemia ($PaO_2 <60\text{ mmHg}$), tachypnea, pulmonary infiltrates ("snowstorm appearance" on CXR).
    2. **Neurological Dysfunction**: Confusion, lethargy, stupor, coma.
    3. **Petechial Rash**: Non-blanching petechiae over conjunctiva, anterior chest wall, axillae, and neck.
  - *Gurd and Wilson Criteria*: Requires 1 Major + 4 Minor criteria for clinical diagnosis.

### 4.1 Patellar Fractures & Extensor Mechanism
Patellar fractures are intra-articular injuries caused by direct blow to the knee or eccentric contraction of the quadriceps muscle.

* **Clinical Assessment**: Inability to perform an active straight leg raise (SLR) indicates complete disruption of the extensor mechanism (patellar retinacula).
* **Fixation**: Transverse fractures with extensor disruption are treated with open reduction and **Tension Band Wiring (TBW)** over two parallel K-wires, converting tensile forces on the anterior surface into compressive forces at the articular surface during knee flexion.

### 4.2 Knee Dislocations & Vascular Emergency
Knee dislocation refers to complete disruption of the tibiofemoral articulation involving at least 3 of the 4 major knee ligaments (ACL, PCL, MCL, LCL).

* **Popliteal Artery Transection / Thrombosis**: The popliteal artery is tethered proximally at the adductor hiatus and distally at the soleal arch, making it exceptionally vulnerable to shear traction injuries during anterior/posterior knee dislocation.
  - *Emergency Protocol*: Immediate manual reduction followed by serial neurovascular examinations. An **Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) $<0.90$** or absent distal pulses demands immediate **CT Angiography** or emergency vascular exploration. Warm ischemia time $>6\text{ hours}$ leads to an amputation rate $>80\%$.
* **Nerve Injury**: **Common Peroneal Nerve** traction injury occurs in up to $40\%$ of lateral column dislocations.

## 5. Tibia, Ankle & Foot Trauma Mechanics

### 5.1 Tibial Shaft Fractures & Compartment Syndrome
Because one-third of the tibial surface is subcutaneous and enclosed within tight fascial boundaries, tibial shaft fractures are the most common long bone fractures overall and represent the **most common site for Acute Compartment Syndrome**.

#### Acute Compartment Syndrome (ACS)
* **Pathophysiology**: Elevation of interstitial tissue pressure within a closed fascial compartment compromises capillary perfusion pressure (CPP = MAP − ICP), leading to tissue ischemia and necrosis.
* **The 6 Ps**: Pain out of proportion to injury (earliest and most sensitive), Pain on passive stretch of compartment muscles, Paresthesia, Pallor, Paralysis, Pulselessness (late sign indicating irreversible tissue death).
* **Diagnosis**: Delta Pressure ($\Delta P = \text{Diastolic BP} - \text{Intracompartmental Pressure}$). A $\Delta P \le 30\text{ mmHg}$ or absolute pressure $>30\text{--}40\text{ mmHg}$ is a definitive indication for emergency surgical **Four-Compartment Fasciotomy** (double incision technique creating anterolateral and posteromedial releases).

### 5.2 Ankle Fractures & Lauge-Hansen Classification
The Lauge-Hansen system categorizes ankle fractures based on the **position of the foot** at the time of injury and the **direction of the displacing force**:

1. **Supination-External Rotation (SER)** (Most common, 60–70%):
   - Stage 1: Anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL) disruption.
   - Stage 2: Short oblique/spiral fracture of lateral malleolus (distal to proximal direction).
   - Stage 3: Posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL) disruption / posterior malleolus fracture.
   - Stage 4: Transverse fracture of medial malleolus / deltoid ligament rupture.
2. **Supination-Adduction (SAD)**:
   - Stage 1: Transverse avulsion fracture of lateral malleolus below joint line.
   - Stage 2: Vertical fracture of medial malleolus.
3. **Pronation-External Rotation (PER)**:
   - Stage 1: Transverse medial malleolar fracture / deltoid ligament rupture.
   - Stage 2: AITFL disruption.
   - Stage 3: High spiral fibular fracture (Maisonneuve fracture pattern).
   - Stage 4: PITFL disruption / posterior malleolus fracture.
4. **Pronation-Abduction (PAB)**:
   - Stage 1: Medial malleolus transverse fracture.
   - Stage 2: AITFL and PITFL disruption.
   - Stage 3: Bending oblique fibular fracture above syndesmosis with lateral butterfly fragment.

* **Maisonneuve Fracture**: High proximal fibular neck fracture associated with disruption of the medial malleolus/deltoid ligament, interosseous membrane, and ankle syndesmosis.

### 5.3 Foot Fractures & Eponymous Injuries

#### Calcaneus Fractures
Calcaneal fractures are the most common tarsal fractures, resulting from axial loading (falls from height onto heels; "Lover's Fracture"). Associated with burst fractures of the thoracolumbar junction ($T_{12}\text{--}L_2$) in $10\%$ of cases.
* **Böhler's Angle**: Formed by the intersection of a line drawn from the highest point of the posterior tuberosity to the posterior articular facet, and a line from the posterior facet to the anterior process. Normal = **$20^\circ\text{--}40^\circ$**. Reduced or flattened ($<20^\circ$) in calcaneal depressed fractures.
* **Critical Angle of Gissane**: Formed by the struts of the lateral calcaneal body. Normal = **$120^\circ\text{--}145^\circ$**; increased in intra-articular fractures.

#### Talus Fractures
Talus neck fractures ("Aviator's Fracture") occur from severe dorsiflexion of the ankle.
* **Vascular Vulnerability**: Blood supply enters the talus via the artery of the tarsal canal, artery of the tarsal sinus, and deltoid branches. Talar neck displacement risks total avascular necrosis of the talar body (Hawkins Classification Types I–IV).
* **Hawkins' Sign**: Subchondral osteopenia observed on AP ankle radiograph 6–8 weeks post-injury. Indicates preserved blood supply and active bone remodeling (a negative Hawkins sign indicates AVN).

#### Metatarsal & Midfoot Disruptions
* **Jones Fracture**: Transverse fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction of the 5th metatarsal base ($1.5\text{--}3\text{ cm}$ distal to tuberosity). Located in a vascular watershed zone; high incidence of non-union requiring operative screw fixation. Differentiated from **Pseudo-Jones Fracture** (avulsion fracture of 5th metatarsal tuberosity by Peroneus Brevis tendon).
* **Lisfranc Fracture-Dislocation**: Disruption of the tarsometatarsal joint complex. The **Lisfranc Ligament** anchors the medial cuneiform to the base of the 2nd metatarsal (there is no intermetatarsal ligament between the 1st and 2nd metatarsals). Loss of alignment leads to collapse of the longitudinal arch.
* **Chopart Fracture-Dislocation**: Disruption through the transverse tarsal / midtarsal joint complex (talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints).
