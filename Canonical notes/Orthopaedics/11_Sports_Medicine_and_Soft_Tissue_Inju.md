# Orthopaedics Chapter 11: Sports Medicine, Ligamentous Injuries & Cumulative Trauma

## 11.1 Knee Ligament Biomechanics & Traumatic Disruption

![[images/Knee_Ligaments.png]]

*ACL and PCL relationships — schematic.*


The knee joint relies heavily on static ligamentous constraints and dynamic muscular stabilizers for functional stability. Ligamentous injuries are common in contact sports, high-energy trauma, and pivot-deceleration mechanisms.

### Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Pathology
* **Anatomy & Function**: Arises from the anterior intercondylar fossa of the tibia and inserts onto the posterior aspect of the medial surface of the lateral femoral condyle. Composed of two distinct functional bundles: the **Anteromedial (AM) bundle** (tight in flexion; primary restraint to anterior tibial translation) and the **Posterolateral (PL) bundle** (tight in extension; primary restraint to rotational instability).
* **Mechanism of Injury**: Non-contact sudden deceleration, pivoting, twisting, or valgus stress applied to a flexed knee.
* **Clinical Presentation**: Patients report an audible "pop" at the time of injury, followed by immediate severe pain and rapid joint swelling (**hemarthrosis within 1–2 hours**).
* **Physical Examination Tests**:
  * **Lachman Test**: The **most sensitive clinical test** ($>95\%$ sensitivity). Performed with the knee flexed at $20\text{--}30^\circ$. Anterior translation of the tibia with a soft or mushy end-point indicates complete ACL rupture.
  * **Anterior Drawer Test**: Performed with the knee flexed at $90^\circ$. Less sensitive than the Lachman test due to hamstrings spasm and posterior horn meniscal wedging.
  * **Pivot-Shift Test**: Demonstrates rotational subluxation of the lateral tibial plateau relative to the lateral femoral condyle as the knee is brought from extension to flexion with valgus and internal rotation torque.
* **Associated Pathognomonic Sign**: **Segond Fracture** — an avulsion fracture of the lateral capsular margin / anterolateral ligament insertion on the proximal lateral tibia, highly specific for an ACL tear.

### Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) Pathology
* **Anatomy & Function**: Arises from the posterior intercondylar fossa of the tibia and inserts into the anterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle. Composed of the **Anterolateral (AL) bundle** (tight in flexion) and **Posteromedial (PM) bundle** (tight in extension). Acts as the primary restraint to posterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur.
* **Mechanism of Injury**: Direct anterior blow to the flexed proximal tibia ("dashboard injury" in motor vehicle accidents or falling onto a flexed knee with foot plantarflexed).
* **Clinical Examination**:
  * **Posterior Sag Sign (Godfrey's Test)**: With both hips and knees flexed to $90^\circ$, the proximal tibia sags posteriorly relative to the femoral condyles due to gravity.
  * **Posterior Drawer Test**: Performed at $90^\circ$ knee flexion; posterior displacement of the tibia confirms PCL disruption.

### Collateral Ligament & Multiligament Complex Injuries
* **Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL)**: Primary constraint to valgus stress. Arises from the medial femoral epicondyle and inserts into the periosteum of the proximal medial tibia. Tested via **Valgus Stress Test** at $0^\circ$ (tests MCL + posterior capsule) and $30^\circ$ knee flexion (isolates superficial MCL).
* **Lateral Collateral Ligament (LCL)**: Primary constraint to varus stress. Extra-articular cord-like structure extending from lateral epicondyle to fibular head. Tested via **Varus Stress Test** at $30^\circ$ flexion.
* **O'Donoghue's Unhappy Triad**: Simultaneous traumatic disruption of:
  1. **Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL)**
  2. **Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL)**
  3. **Lateral Meniscus** (traditionally described as Medial Meniscus, but acute biomechanical studies demonstrate lateral meniscus tears are more common in acute ACL disruptions).

## 11.2 Meniscal Mechanics, Cartilage & Patellofemoral Pathology

![[images/Meniscal_Tears.png]]

*Major meniscal tear patterns — schematic.*


### Meniscal Pathophysiology & Vascular Zones
The medial and lateral menisci are fibrocartilaginous structures that function in shock absorption, load transmission (distributing $50\text{--}70\%$ of load), joint congruity, and joint lubrication.
* **Vascular Architecture**:
  * **Red-Red Zone** (Outer 10–25%): Fully vascularized; excellent potential for surgical meniscal repair.
  * **Red-White Zone** (Middle region): Partial vascularity; variable repair potential.
  * **White-White Zone** (Inner 66%): Avascular; receives nutrition exclusively via synovial fluid diffusion. Unrepairable; managed via partial meniscectomy when symptomatic.
* **Mechanism of Meniscal Tear**: Rotational twisting torque on a weight-bearing, flexed knee joint.
* **Clinical Features**: Delayed joint effusion (developing overnight or 12–24 hours post-injury), mechanical **joint locking** (inability to fully extend the knee, typical of **Bucket-Handle Tears**), popping, and joint line tenderness.
* **Diagnostic Tests**:
  * **McMurray's Test**: Pain or a palpable click elicited over the joint line during passive knee extension combined with external rotation (tests medial meniscus) or internal rotation (tests lateral meniscus).
  * **Apley's Grind Test**: Patient prone with knee flexed $90^\circ$; axial compression and rotation elicit pain in meniscal tears, whereas distraction and rotation elicit pain in collateral ligament injuries.
  * **Thessaly Test**: Single-leg standing twisting test at $20^\circ$ knee flexion.

### Osteochondritis Dissecans (OCD) & Patellofemoral Disorders
* **Osteochondritis Dissecans**: Focal subchondral bone necrosis followed by decalcification and detachment of overlying hyaline cartilage, forming an intra-articular loose body ("joint mouse").
  * *Classic Location*: Posterolateral aspect of the **Medial Femoral Condyle** ($75\%$ of cases).
  * *Clinical Sign*: **Wilson's Sign** (pain on extension of the knee with internal rotation, relieved by external rotation).
* **Acute Patellar Dislocation**: Displaces **laterally** due to the natural Q-angle. Disruption of the **Medial Patellofemoral Ligament (MPFL)**. Elicited by **Fairbank's Patellar Apprehension Test**.

## 11.3 Overuse Tendinopathies & Enthesopathies

Overuse syndromes stem from repetitive microtrauma exceeding the physiological reparative capacity of tendinous insertions.

| Disorder | Anatomical Site / Tendon Involved | Clinical Test / Diagnostic Sign | Key Pathological Features |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Lateral Epicondylitis** (*"Tennis Elbow"*) | Origin of **Extensor Carpi Radialis Brevis (ECRB)** at lateral epicondyle | **Cozen's Test** & **Mill's Test**: Resisted wrist extension with elbow in full extension | Angiofibroblastic hyperplasia, micro-tearing of ECRB origin |
| **Medial Epicondylitis** (*"Golfer's Elbow"*) | Origin of **Pronator Teres** and **Flexor Carpi Radialis** at medial epicondyle | Resisted forearm pronation and wrist flexion | Microtrauma at common flexor tendon origin |
| **De Quervain's Tenosynovitis** | 1st Dorsal Extensor Compartment: **Abductor Pollicis Longus (APL)** & **Extensor Pollicis Brevis (EPB)** | **Finkelstein's Test**: Severe pain over radial styloid when thumb is tucked into fist and wrist ulnar deviated | Thickening of first extensor retinaculum |
| **Plantar Fasciitis** | Insertion of plantar fascia into medial calcaneal tubercle | Severe pain with **first steps in the morning**, improving with walking | Micro-tearing of plantar aponeurosis; heel spur is incidental |
| **Achilles Tendon Rupture** | Hypovascular watershed zone (2–6 cm proximal to calcaneal insertion) | **Thompson's Calf Squeeze Test**: Lack of passive plantarflexion when squeezing gastrocnemius | Sudden snap during explosive push-off; palpable defect |

## 11.4 Shoulder Rotator Cuff & Labral Pathology

![[images/Rotator_Cuff.png]]

*Rotator cuff — schematic.*


### Rotator Cuff Anatomy & Biomechanics
The rotator cuff complex dynamically stabilizes the glenohumeral joint, clamping the humeral head into the shallow glenoid fossa. It is composed of four muscles (Mnemonic **SITS**):
1. **Supraspinatus** (Suprascapular nerve): Initiates abduction ($0\text{--}15^\circ$). Tested via **Empty Can Test (Jobe's Test)**. Most common tendon involved in cuff tears and subacromial impingement due to hypovascularity in **Codman's Critical Zone**.
2. **Infraspinatus** (Suprascapular nerve): External rotation of the shoulder with arm at side.
3. **Teres Minor** (Axillary nerve): External rotation of the shoulder in abduction. Tested via **Hornblower's Sign**.
4. **Subscapularis** (Upper and lower subscapular nerves): Internal rotation of the shoulder. Tested via **Gerber's Lift-Off Test** and **Belly-Press Test**.

### Subacromial Impingement & Rotator Cuff Tears
* **Pathophysiology**: Compression of the supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa between the humeral head and the coracoacromial arch during overhead abduction.
* **Impingement Signs**:
  * **Neer's Impingement Sign**: Passive forward flexion of the arm in internal rotation elicits pain as the greater tuberosity impings against the anterior acromion.
  * **Hawkins-Kennedy Test**: Passive forward flexion to $90^\circ$ followed by internal rotation forces the greater tuberosity under the coracoacromial ligament.
* **Full-Thickness Rotator Cuff Tear**: Elicits a positive **Drop Arm Test** (inability to smoothly lower the arm from $90^\circ$ abduction).

### Glenoid Labral Lesions
* **SLAP Lesion (Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior)**: Tear of the superior glenoid labrum starting posteriorly and extending anteriorly, involving the origin anchor of the **Long Head of Biceps Tendon**. Elicited by **Speed's Test** and **O'Brien's Active Compression Test**.
* **Bankart Lesion**: Avulsion of the anteroinferior glenoid labrum along with the inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHLC) complex following anterior shoulder dislocation.
