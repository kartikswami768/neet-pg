# Orthopaedics Chapter 10: Sports Medicine & Soft Tissue Injuries

## 1. Knee Ligamentous Biomechanics & Clinical Diagnostics



*ACL and PCL relationships — schematic.*


- Knee ligament stability relies on a complex spatial arrangement of intra-articular cruciate ligaments and extra-articular collateral ligaments.
- Ligament disruption alters joint kinematics, predisposing to accelerated articular cartilage wear and secondary osteoarthritis.

### 1.1 Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Injury
* **Biomechanics & Function**: The ACL originates from the posteromedial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle and inserts into the anterior intercondylar area of the tibia. It functions as the primary restraint against anterior tibial translation (providing $85\%$ of resistance) and a secondary restraint against internal rotational torque.
* **Mechanism of Injury**: Typically non-contact deceleration, pivoting, or landing from a jump with the knee in slight flexion, valgus, and external rotation.
* **Clinical Presentation**: Patients report an immediate audible "pop" or tearing sensation, accompanied by severe pain and rapid, tense intra-articular **hemarthrosis within 1–2 hours** (the most common cause of acute traumatic hemarthrosis).
* **Diagnostic Tests**:
  * **Lachman Test**: Performed at $20\text{--}30^\circ$ of knee flexion. The femur is stabilized while the proximal tibia is translated anteriorly. **Most sensitive clinical test** for ACL rupture (evaluates soft or absent end-point).
  * **Anterior Drawer Test**: Performed at $90^\circ$ of knee flexion. Less sensitive in acute injury due to hamstring guarding and posterior meniscus wedging.
  * **Pivot Shift Test**: Evaluates rotational instability. Reconstructs dynamic subluxation and reduction of the lateral tibial plateau; pathognomonic for complete ACL deficiency.
* **Pathognomonic Radiological Sign**: **Segond Fracture** — an avulsion fracture of the lateral tibial condyle at the site of insertion of the anterolateral ligament; highly specific for ACL rupture ($>90\%$).

### 1.2 Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) Injury
* **Biomechanics & Function**: Originates from the anterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle and inserts onto the posterior intercondylar fossa of the tibia below the articular surface. Functions as the primary restraint against posterior tibial translation.
* **Mechanism of Injury**: "Dashboard injury" in motor vehicle accidents (direct posterior force directed at the anterior proximal tibia with the knee flexed $90^\circ$) or falling onto a flexed knee with the foot plantarflexed.
* **Diagnostic Tests**:
  * **Posterior Drawer Test**: Performed at $90^\circ$ knee flexion; posterior translation of proximal tibia indicates PCL disruption.
  * **Posterior Sag Sign (Godfrey's Test)**: Patient lying supine with hips and knees flexed to $90^\circ$. Loss of normal anterior tibial tuberosity prominence relative to the femur when viewed from the side.

### 1.3 Collateral Ligament Injuries & O'Donoghue's Unhappy Triad
* **Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL)**: Primary restraint to valgus stress. Injured by direct lateral contact blow to the knee. Tested via **Valgus Stress Test** at $0^\circ$ (evaluates MCL + joint capsule) and $30^\circ$ knee flexion (isolated MCL). Most commonly injured knee ligament; high capacity for non-surgical healing.
* **Lateral Collateral Ligament (LCL)**: Primary restraint to varus stress. Tested via **Varus Stress Test** at $0^\circ$ and $30^\circ$ knee flexion.
* **O'Donoghue's Unhappy Triad**: Classic severe multi-ligamentous injury resulting from a high-energy valgus impact to the flexed knee, consisting of simultaneous tears of:
  1. **Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL)**
  2. **Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL)**
  3. **Medial Meniscus** (or Lateral Meniscus in modern dynamic modeling)

| Knee Ligament | Primary Function | Primary Mechanism | Most Sensitive Test |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ACL** | Restraint to anterior tibial translation | Pivoting / Deceleration / Valgus | **Lachman Test** |
| **PCL** | Restraint to posterior tibial translation | Direct anterior tibial blow ("Dashboard") | **Posterior Sag Sign** |
| **MCL** | Restraint to valgus stress | Lateral blow to knee | **Valgus Stress at $30^\circ$** |
| **LCL** | Restraint to varus stress | Medial blow to knee | **Varus Stress at $30^\circ$** |

## 2. Meniscal Pathology & Articular Cartilage Lesions



*Major meniscal tear patterns — schematic.*


The medial and lateral menisci are crescentic fibrocartilaginous structures that function in load transmission ($50\text{--}70\%$ of compressive load), shock absorption, joint lubrication, and secondary knee stabilization.

### 2.1 Meniscal Biomechanics & Vascular Zones
* **Anatomical Differences**: The **Medial Meniscus** is C-shaped, larger, and firmly attached to the deep medial collateral ligament, making it less mobile and **more susceptible to tear** ($3:1$ ratio compared to lateral). The **Lateral Meniscus** is more circular (O-shaped), smaller, and highly mobile.
* **Vascularity (Nutritional Zones)**:
  * *Red-Red Zone* (Peripheral 10–25%): Well-vascularized via genicular capillary plexuses; high capacity for primary surgical repair.
  * *Red-White Zone* (Middle third): Intermediate vascularity.
  * *White-White Zone* (Central avascular third): Nourished solely by synovial fluid diffusion; no healing capacity; requires partial meniscectomy when torn.

### 2.2 Clinical Diagnostics for Meniscal Tears
* **Mechanism**: Rotational / twisting force applied to a flexed, weight-bearing knee.
* **Clinical Symptoms**: Localized joint line pain, mechanical joint **locking** (inability to fully extend the knee due to displaced fragment), catching, giving way, and delayed recurrent effusion occurring $12\text{--}24\text{ hours}$ post-injury.
* **Pathognomonic Physical Tests**:
  * **Joint Line Tenderness**: Most sensitive physical sign ($85\%$).
  * **McMurray Test**: Patient supine with knee flexed. Internal rotation of tibia + varus stress evaluates lateral meniscus; external rotation of tibia + valgus stress evaluates medial meniscus as knee is slowly extended. Positive test = palpable or audible "click" or pain over joint line.
  * **Apley's Grind (Compression) Test**: Patient prone with knee flexed $90^\circ$. Axial compression + rotational torque elicits pain in meniscal tear (distinguished from distraction torque, which elicits collateral ligament pain).
  * **Thessaly Test**: Dynamic weight-bearing test; patient stands on single leg flexed $20^\circ$ and rotates body inward/outward. Highly accurate for meniscal tears.

### 2.3 Osteochondritis Dissecans (OCD)
* **Pathophysiology**: Localized avascular necrosis of subchondral bone leading to softening, micro-fracture, and potential detachment of the overlying articular hyaline cartilage, forming a loose intra-articular body ("joint mouse").
* **Predilection Site**: Classical location is the **lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle** ($85\%$ of cases).
* **Clinical Presentation**: Vague knee pain, swelling, locking, and giving way in adolescent athletes.
* **Special Clinical Test**: **Wilson's Test** — pain elicited on internal rotation of the tibia during knee extension from $90^\circ$ to $30^\circ$, which is relieved immediately by external rotation of the tibia.

## 3. Overuse Tendinopathies & Enthesopathies

Overuse tendinopathies stem from repetitive micro-trauma exceeding the physiological tendon repair threshold, leading to mucoid degeneration, hypervascularity, and unorganized collagen bundles rather than acute histological inflammation.

### 3.1 Lateral & Medial Epicondylitis
* **Lateral Epicondylitis ("Tennis Elbow")**:
  * *Pathology*: Micro-tearing and angiofibroblastic tendinosis at the origin of the **Extensor Carpi Radialis Brevis (ECRB)** tendon at the lateral epicondyle.
  * *Mechanism*: Repetitive wrist extension and forearm supination (backhand tennis stroke).
  * *Clinical Tests*:
    * **Cozen's Test**: Pain over lateral epicondyle on resisted wrist extension with the elbow fully extended and forearm pronated.
    * **Mill's Test**: Pain elicited by passive wrist flexion and forearm pronation while extending the elbow.
* **Medial Epicondylitis ("Golfer's Elbow")**:
  * *Pathology*: Tendinopathy involving the common flexor-pronator tendon origin (**Pronator Teres** and **Flexor Carpi Radialis**) at the medial epicondyle.
  * *Mechanism*: Repetitive wrist flexion and forearm pronation (forehand golf swing / pitching).
  * *Clinical Test*: Pain over medial epicondyle on resisted wrist flexion and pronation.

### 3.2 De Quervain's Stenosing Tenosynovitis
* **Pathophysiology**: Fibrous thickening and narrowing of the **1st dorsal extensor compartment** of the wrist, leading to painful friction of the traversing tendons:
  1. **Abductor Pollicis Longus (APL)**
  2. **Extensor Pollicis Brevis (EPB)**
* **Clinical Presentation**: Pain and swelling over the radial styloid process aggravated by thumb movements.
* **Pathognomonic Test**: **Finkelstein's Test** — the patient tucks the thumb inside a closed fist, and the examiner passively flexes the wrist toward the ulnar side. A sharp, severe reproducing pain over the radial styloid confirms the diagnosis.

### 3.3 Plantar Fasciitis
* **Pathophysiology**: Repetitive strain and micro-tearing at the insertion of the thick plantar aponeurosis into the **medial tubercle of the calcaneus**.
* **Clinical Feature**: Characteristically severe, sharp heel pain experienced upon taking the **very first steps in the morning** or after prolonged rest, which gradually lessens after walking as the fascia warms up.
* **Physical Examination**: Marked focal tenderness directly over the anteromedial calcaneal tuberosity, aggravated by passive dorsiflexion of the toes (Windlass mechanism).

### 3.4 Achilles Tendon Rupture
* **Pathophysiology**: Complete transverse disruption of the Achilles tendon (gastrocnemius-soleus complex), occurring typically $2\text{--}6\text{ cm}$ proximal to its calcaneal insertion (critical watershed hypo-vascular zone).
* **Demographics**: Middle-aged "weekend warriors" engaged in explosive push-off sports (racquetball, basketball).
* **Clinical Presentation**: Patient experiences a sudden, sharp pain described as if "being kicked in the back of the calf," often hearing an audible snap, followed by immediate weakness in plantarflexion.
* **Diagnostic Physical Signs**:
  * Palpable tendon gap (sulcus sign).
  * **Thompson's Calf Squeeze Test**: Patient prone with feet hanging off the table. The examiner squeezes the calf muscle belly. A positive test = **absence of passive ankle plantarflexion**, indicating complete tendon disruption.
  * **Matles Test**: Increased dorsiflexion of the affected foot when knees are flexed to $90^\circ$ in the prone position.

## 4. Shoulder Rotator Cuff & Labral Pathology



*Rotator cuff — schematic.*


The shoulder girdle sacrifices structural stability for multi-axial mobility. Dynamic stabilization is provided by the rotator cuff muscle complex (**SITS** muscles) centered within the coracoacromial arch.

### 4.1 Rotator Cuff Anatomy & Specific Diagnostics

The rotator cuff consists of four musculotendinous units surrounding the glenohumeral joint:

1. **Supraspinatus**:
   * *Function*: Initiates early arm abduction ($0\text{--}15^\circ$) and depresses humeral head.
   * *Vulnerability*: Passes under the coracoacromial arch; avascular critical zone $1\text{ cm}$ from insertion on greater tuberosity is the **most common site of cuff tear**.
   * *Special Tests*: **Empty Can Test (Jobe's Test)** (weakness/pain on resisted elevation in scapular plane with arm internally rotated); **Neer's Impingement Sign** & **Hawkins-Kennedy Test**.
2. **Infraspinatus & Teres Minor**:
   * *Function*: External rotation of the humerus.
   * *Special Tests*: Resisted external rotation at $0^\circ$ abduction; **External Rotation Lag Sign**.
3. **Subscapularis**:
   * *Function*: Primary internal rotator of the humerus; inserts onto the lesser tuberosity.
   * *Special Tests*: **Gerber's Lift-Off Test** (inability to push hand away from lower back); **Belly-Press Test** (used if shoulder internal rotation is restricted).

### 4.2 Glenoid Labrum Pathology: SLAP vs. Bankart Lesions
* **SLAP Lesion (Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior)**:
  * *Pathology*: Disruption of the superior glenoid labrum starting posteriorly and extending anteriorly, involving the anchor site of the **Long Head of Biceps Tendon**.
  * *Mechanism*: Overhead throwing athletes (late cocking phase) or traction injury.
  * *Special Test*: **O'Brien's Active Compression Test** (pain elicited with arm flexed $90^\circ$, adducted $10^\circ$, and fully internally rotated/pronated, which is relieved when arm is supinated).
* **Bankart Lesion**:
  * *Pathology*: Detachment of the anteroinferior glenoid labrum from the glenoid rim secondary to acute **anterior shoulder dislocation**.
  * *Special Tests*: **Apprehension Test** and **Relocation Test** for anterior shoulder instability.
