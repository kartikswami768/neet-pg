# Orthopaedics Chapter 04: Peripheral Nerve Injuries, Brachial Plexus & Spine Disorders

## 1. Histology & Classifications of Peripheral Nerve Injuries

### 1.1 Microanatomy & Wallerian Degeneration
A peripheral nerve trunk consists of multiple axons bundled into fascicles. Individual nerve fibers are encased in **endoneurium**, fascicles are enclosed by **perineurium** (which provides the primary diffusion barrier and mechanical strength), and the outer nerve trunk is wrapped in **epineurium**.

When an axon is transected, it undergoes a predictable sequence of anterograde breakdown known as **Wallerian Degeneration**:
1. *Axonal Breakdown* (24–48 hours): Distal to the injury site, the axon and its surrounding myelin sheath undergo granular disintegration due to activation of ==ubiquitin-proteasome systems== and calcium-dependent neutral proteases (==calpains==).
2. *Phagocytosis* (Days 2–14): Hematogenous macrophages and resident Schwann cells phagocytose distal axonal and myelin debris.
3. *Schwann Cell Proliferation*: Proliferating Schwann cells line up inside intact endoneurial tubes to form continuous cellular cords known as the **Bands of Büngner**.
4. *Axonal Regeneration*: Regenerative neurite sprouts emerge from the proximal axon node. Guided by neurotrophic factors (NGF, BDNF) along the Bands of Büngner, axons advance at an average rate of **$1\text{ mm/day}$** (approximately $1\text{ inch/month}$).

### 1.2 Seddon & Sunderland Injury Classifications

| Seddon Category | Sunderland Degree | Histological Disruption | Pathophysiology & Conduction | Regeneration Potential |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Neurapraxia** | **1st Degree** | Axon intact; localized focal demyelination | Localized physiological conduction block; no Wallerian degeneration | Complete rapid recovery (days to 12 weeks) |
| **Axonotmesis** | **2nd Degree** | Axon disrupted; endoneurium intact | Wallerian degeneration occurs distally; intact endoneurial tube guides axon | Full recovery at $1\text{ mm/day}$ without surgery |
| **Axonotmesis** | **3rd Degree** | Axon and endoneurium disrupted; perineurium intact | Disorganized axonal sprouting inside fascicle; risk of intraneural fibrosis | Partial recovery; may require neurolysis |
| **Axonotmesis** | **4th Degree** | Axon, endoneurium, and perineurium disrupted; epineurium intact | Complete fascicular disruption; neuromas form in continuity | Requires excision of neuroma and end-to-end graft repair |
| **Neurotmesis** | **5th Degree** | Complete anatomical transection of nerve trunk | Complete severance of all layers including epineurium | No spontaneous recovery; mandates surgical nerve repair / graft |

## 2. Brachial Plexus Anatomy & Eponymous Palsies

The brachial plexus is formed by the ventral rami of spinal nerves **$C_5\text{--}T_1$** (Roots $\rightarrow$ Trunks $\rightarrow$ Divisions $\rightarrow$ Cords $\rightarrow$ Terminal Branches).


### 2.1 Erb-Duchenne Palsy (Upper Trunk Lesion)
Erb-Duchenne palsy results from damage to the **Upper Trunk ($C_5\text{--}C_6$)** at **Erb's Point** (the junction where 6 nerves meet: $C_5$ root, $C_6$ root, Upper trunk, Suprascapular nerve, Nerve to subclavius, and Anterior/Posterior divisions).

* **Mechanism of Injury**: Excessive lateral distraction of the head away from the shoulder during difficult vertex or breech delivery (shoulder dystocia in newborns) or a fall landing forcefully on the shoulder in adults.
* **Paralyzed Muscles**: Deltoid and Teres minor (Axillary nerve), Supraspinatus and Infraspinatus (Suprascapular nerve), Biceps brachii and Brachialis (Musculocutaneous nerve), Brachioradialis and Supinator (Radial nerve).
* **Clinical Posture ("Waiter's Tip" / "Policeman's Tip" Hand)**:
  - Shoulder: **Adducted and Internally Rotated** (due to loss of abductors [deltoid, supraspinatus] and external rotators [infraspinatus]).
  - Elbow: **Extended** (due to loss of flexors [biceps, brachialis]).
  - Forearm: **Pronated** (due to loss of supinators [biceps, supinator]).
  - Wrist: **Flexed**.
* **Reflexes**: Absent Biceps and Biceps-brachioradialis reflexes. Sensory loss occurs over the lateral aspect of the arm and forearm ($C_5\text{--}C_6$ dermatomes).

### 2.2 Klumpke's Palsy (Lower Trunk Lesion)
Klumpke's palsy results from injury to the **Lower Trunk ($C_8\text{--}T_1$)**.

* **Mechanism of Injury**: Hyper-abduction traction of the arm (e.g., catching a tree branch while falling from a height, or forced traction on a breech delivery with extended arms).
* **Nerves Involved**: Ulnar nerve and Median nerve (inner head).
* **Paralyzed Muscles**: All intrinsic muscles of the hand (interossei, lumbricals, hypothenar, thenar muscles).
* **Clinical Presentation ("True Claw Hand")**:
  - Loss of lumbricals (which normally flex MCP and extend IP joints) leads to uncontrolled unopposed extension at Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints by long extensors and flexion at Interphalangeal (IP) joints by long flexors across **all four fingers**.
* **Associated Horner's Syndrome**: Disruption of $T_1$ sympathetic preganglionic fibers running in the $T_1$ white rami communicantes produces ipsilateral **Ptosis** (drooping eyelid), **Miosis** (constricted pupil), **Anhidrosis** (lack of sweating on face/neck), and **Enophthalmos** (sunken eyeball).

## 3. Upper & Lower Extremity Nerve Lesions & Entrapment Neuropathies

### 3.1 Radial Nerve ($C_5\text{--}T_1$)
* **Sites of Injury**:
  1. *Axilla*: Crutch palsy / "Saturday Night Palsy" (compression over chair back). Paralyzes Triceps, Anconeus, and all posterior forearm extensors. Loss of extension at elbow, wrist, and MCP joints (**Wrist Drop + Finger Drop**), plus loss of triceps reflex and anesthesia over posterior arm/forearm and first dorsal web space.
  2. *Spiral Groove*: Associated with mid-shaft humeral fractures (Holstein-Lewis fracture). **Triceps flexor power and triceps reflex are preserved** (branches arise proximal to groove). Causes **Wrist Drop and Finger Drop**.
  3. *Distal / Radial Head*: Posterior Interosseous Nerve (PIN) entrapment (Frohse's arcade). Causes **Finger Drop only** (Extensor Carpi Radialis Longus is spared, preserving wrist extension with radial deviation; no sensory deficit).
* **Splinting**: Cock-up splint (holds wrist in extension to allow functional finger grasp).

### 3.2 Median Nerve ($C_6\text{--}T_1$) — "Labourer's Nerve"
* **High Lesion (Supracondylar Humerus Fracture / Elbow)**:
  - Paralyzes Pronator teres, Flexor carpi radialis, Flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), Flexor pollicis longus (FPL), radial half of Flexor digitorum profundus (FDP II & III), thenar muscles, and lateral two lumbricals.
  - **Hand of Benediction**: When attempting to make a fist, the index and middle fingers remain extended due to loss of FDS/FDP II & III, while the 4th and 5th fingers flex normally (ulnar nerve).
  - **Ape Thumb Deformity**: Thenar atrophy with loss of opponens pollicis causes the thumb to fall into the plane of the palm (adducted and extended).
* **Low Lesion (Wrist / Carpal Tunnel Syndrome)**:
  - FDS and FDP function are preserved (patient can flex fingers). Thenar motor loss (Abductor pollicis brevis, Opponens pollicis) leads to Ape Thumb Deformity.
* **Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS)**:
  - Compression of the median nerve within the carpal tunnel beneath the **Flexor Retinaculum** (Transverse Carpal Ligament). The tunnel contains 10 structures: Median Nerve + 4 FDS tendons + 4 FDP tendons + 1 FPL tendon.
  - *Clinical Signs*: Paresthesias over thumb, index, middle, and radial half of ring fingers (sparing the palm due to proximal palmar cutaneous branch entering above retinaculum). Nocturnal pain awakening patient.
  - *Diagnostic Tests*: **Phalen's Test** (maximal wrist flexion for 60 seconds reproduces symptoms), **Tinel's Sign** (percussion over retinaculum elicits tingling), **Durkan's Carpal Compression Test** (direct digital pressure over tunnel; most sensitive).
  - *Treatment*: Night splints in neutral position, local steroid injection, open or endoscopic Carpal Tunnel Release (division of flexor retinaculum).

### 3.3 Ulnar Nerve ($C_8\text{--}T_1$) — "Musician's Nerve"
* **Anatomical Vulnerability**: Passes behind the medial epicondyle in the cubital tunnel and enters the hand via **Guyon's Canal** (between pisiform and hook of hamate).
* **Clinical Deficits**:
  - Paralyzes Flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU), ulnar half of FDP (FDP IV & V), hypothenar muscles, all interossei (palmar and dorsal), adductor pollicis, and medial two lumbricals.
  - **Partial Claw Hand**: Clawing of 4th and 5th digits (hyperextension at MCP, flexion at IP joints).
  - **Ulnar Paradox**: A high lesion at the elbow results in *less severe clawing* than a low lesion at the wrist. This occurs because an elbow lesion paralyzes the ulnar half of FDP (FDP IV & V), eliminating the distal flexor force that accentuates IP joint flexion in clawing.
* **Eponymous Diagnostic Tests for Ulnar Nerve**:
  - **Froment's Sign**: Tests Adductor Pollicis (ulnar nerve). Patient holds a piece of paper between thumb and index finger. When examiner pulls paper, thumb IP joint flexes sharply (due to compensatory activation of FPL by median nerve).
  - **Jeanne's Sign**: Compensatory hyperextension of the thumb MCP joint during Froment's pinch test.
  - **Egawa's Sign**: Inability of the middle finger to abduct and adduct side-to-side when the hand is flat on a table (tests dorsal/palmar interossei).
  - **Card Test**: Inability to hold a card between adjacent extended fingers due to palmar interosseous weakness.

### 3.4 Lower Extremity Nerves & Entrapments
* **Sciatic Nerve ($L_4\text{--}S_3$)**: Injured in posterior hip dislocations or acetabular fractures. Causes paralysis of hamstrings (knee flexion) and all muscles below the knee, resulting in a flail foot and anesthesia of lower leg/foot (sparing saphenous distribution).
* **Common Peroneal Nerve ($L_4\text{--}S_2$)**: Wraps superficially around the neck of the fibula. Vulnerable to direct trauma, fibular neck fractures, or tight casts.
  - *Deficit*: Paralyzes anterior and lateral compartment muscles (tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus, peroneus longus/brevis).
  - *Clinical Result*: **Foot Drop** (loss of dorsiflexion and eversion). Patient demonstrates **Steppage Gait** (exaggerated hip/knee flexion to clear foot during swing phase) and slap foot gait.
* **Tarsal Tunnel Syndrome**: Compression of the Tibial Nerve behind the medial malleolus beneath the flexor retinaculum. Causes burning paresthesia on the plantar sole of the foot.
* **Meralgia Paresthetica**: Entrapment of the **Lateral Cutaneous Nerve of Thigh** as it passes under the inguinal ligament near the ASIS. Causes isolated sensory burning pain and paresthesia over the anterolateral thigh (no motor deficit).

## 4. Spinal Column Trauma & Acute Cord Syndromes

### 4.1 Denis Three-Column Concept of Spinal Stability
Denis divided the thoracolumbar spine into three mechanical vertical columns:

1. **Anterior Column**: Anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL), anterior two-thirds of the vertebral body, and anterior annulus fibrosus.
2. **Middle Column**: Posterior one-third of the vertebral body, posterior annulus fibrosus, and **Posterior Longitudinal Ligament (PLL)**.
3. **Posterior Column**: Posterior neural arch (pedicles, lamina, facet joints, spinous processes) and ligamentous complex (supraspinous, interspinous, ligamentum flavum).

* **Mechanical Instability**: Instability is defined as disruption of at least **2 adjacent columns**, or any disruption involving the **middle column** (e.g., burst fractures with retropulsed bone fragments into spinal canal).

### 4.2 Acute Spinal Cord Injury Patterns



*Cord syndrome comparison — schematic.*


| Spinal-cord syndrome | Classic pattern |
|---|---|
| Complete transection | Bilateral motor and sensory loss below lesion |
| Central cord | Upper limbs more affected than lower limbs |
| Anterior cord | Loss of motor, pain and temperature; dorsal column modalities relatively preserved |
| Brown–Séquard | Ipsilateral motor/proprioceptive loss, contralateral pain/temperature loss |

* **Complete Cord Transection**: Immediate flaccid paralysis, loss of all sensation, and loss of reflexes below lesion level (spinal shock phase), transitioning to spastic paraplegia/quadriplegia with hyperreflexia and Babinski signs.
* **Central Cord Syndrome**: Most common incomplete cord lesion. Occurs during hyperextension injuries in elderly patients with pre-existing cervical spondylosis.
  - *Mechanism*: Compression of centrally located cervical corticospinal tract fibers.
  - *Clinical Pattern*: **Upper extremity motor impairment is significantly greater than lower extremity motor impairment**, with variable sacral sensory sparing.
* **Anterior Cord Syndrome**: Associated with flexion-compression trauma or anterior spinal artery occlusion.
  - *Clinical Pattern*: Loss of bilateral motor function (corticospinal tract) and loss of pain and temperature sensation (spinothalamic tract) below lesion level. **Posterior column functions (vibration, deep pressure, and proprioception) remain fully intact**.
* **Brown-Séquard Syndrome (Cord Hemi-section)**: Caused by penetrative trauma or asymmetric cord compression.
  - *Ipsilateral Deficits*: Motor paralysis below lesion level (corticospinal tract) + loss of vibration, fine touch, and proprioception (dorsal column).
  - *Contralateral Deficits*: Loss of pain and temperature sensation (spinothalamic tract) starting **1 to 2 dermatomal levels below** the level of lesion.
* **SCIWORA (Spinal Cord Injury Without Radiographic Abnormality)**: Pertains primarily to pediatric patients ($<8\text{ years}$) where severe spinal cord traction/ischemia occurs without evidence of fracture or dislocation on plain radiographs due to inherent elasticity of the juvenile spine.

## 5. Intervertebral Disc Pathology, Stenosis & Deformities

### 5.1 Prolapsed Intervertebral Disc (PIVD)
PIVD results from tear of the outer annulus fibrosus allowing herniation of the central gelatinous nucleus pulposus, most commonly in a posterolateral direction (where the PLL is weakest). Over $95\%$ of lumbar disc herniations occur at **$L_4\text{--}L_5$** or **$L_5\text{--}S_1$**.

#### Radiculopathy Mapping

| Disc Herniation Level | Compressed Nerve Root | Motor Deficit | Reflex Deficit | Sensory Deficit Zone |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$L_3\text{--}L_4$ Disc** | **$L_4$ Root** | Quadriceps weakness (knee extension) | **Patellar Ankle Jerk** | Anterior thigh, medial lower leg & medial malleolus |
| **$L_4\text{--}L_5$ Disc** | **$L_5$ Root** | Extensor Hallucis Longus (EHL) & Tibialis Anterior (foot dorsiflexion) | None (Tibialis posterior reflex variable) | Dorsum of foot and **first web space** |
| **$L_5\text{--}S_1$ Disc** | **$S_1$ Root** | Gastrocnemius & Soleus (plantarflexion), Peroneus longus | **Achilles Ankle Jerk** | Lateral border of foot, sole, and pinky toe |

#### Clinical Provocative Tests
* **Straight Leg Raising Test (SLRT / Lasègue's Sign)**: Patient supine; examiner passively elevates extended leg. Positive if radicular leg pain is reproduced between $30^\circ\text{--}70^\circ$ elevation (stretches $L_5\text{--}S_1$ nerve roots over herniated disc).
* **Crossed SLRT (Well Leg Raising Test)**: Passive elevation of the unaffected leg reproduces radicular pain in the symptomatic leg. **Highly specific** ($>90\%$) for space-occupying disc herniation.
* **Femoral Stretch Test**: Patient prone; examiner passively hyperextends thigh with knee flexed. Positive if pain reproduces along anterior thigh (indicates upper lumbar root compression, $L_2\text{--}L_4$).

### 5.2 Lumbar Spinal Canal Stenosis
Lumbar canal stenosis involves narrowing of the central spinal canal, lateral recesses, or neural foramina caused by facet joint hypertrophy, ligamentum flavum thickening, and osteophyte formation.

* **Neurogenic Claudication**: Characterized by leg pain, numbness, heaviness, and weakness precipitated by standing or walking.
* **The "Shopping Cart" Sign**: Symptoms are **exacerbated by spinal extension** (standing, walking downhill; decreases canal volume) and **relieved by spinal flexion** (sitting, leaning forward over a shopping cart; opens spinal canal area).
* **Differentiating Claudication**:

| Feature | Neurogenic Claudication | Vascular Claudication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pathology** | Lumbar spinal canal stenosis | Peripheral arterial disease |
| **Pain Location** | Thighs, buttocks, diffuse lower leg | Calf muscle group |
| **Relieving Factor** | **Spinal flexion** (sitting, bending) | **Stopping motion** (standing still) |
| **Walking Uphill** | Better tolerated (spine flexed) | Worsened (higher metabolic muscular load) |
| **Peripheral Pulses** | Normal, palpable | Diminished or absent |

### 5.3 Spondylolysis, Spondylolisthesis & Structural Deformities

#### Spondylolysis & Spondylolisthesis


![Radiographic example illustrating measurement of vertebral slip. ](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Spondylolisthesis%20measurement%20on%20X-ray.svg)

*Visual reference: Radiographic example illustrating measurement of vertebral slip. [Original image and attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spondylolisthesis_measurement_on_X-ray.svg). Online image; internet required.*
* **Spondylolysis**: Defect or stress fracture in the **pars interarticularis** of the neural arch, most commonly affecting $L_5$ ($90\%$). Demonstrated on $45^\circ$ oblique lumbar radiographs as the **"Scotty Dog Sign"** with a collar or break across the dog's neck.
* **Spondylolisthesis**: Anterior displacement (slipping) of a proximal vertebral body relative to the caudal adjacent vertebra. Classified as Isthmic (secondary to pars defect in young athletes) or Degenerative (in older adults at $L_4\text{--}L_5$).
* **Meyerding Classification** (Based on percentage slip of vertebral body width):
  - *Grade I*: $0\text{--}25\%$ slip.
  - *Grade II*: $25\text{--}50\%$ slip.
  - *Grade III*: $50\text{--}75\%$ slip.
  - *Grade IV*: $75\text{--}100\%$ slip.
  - *Grade V (Spondyloptosis)*: $>100\%$ slip (complete anterior displacement off sacral promontory).

#### Scoliosis


![Cobb-angle measurement for scoliosis on a spine diagram. ](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Scoliosis%20cobb.svg)

*Visual reference: Cobb-angle measurement for scoliosis on a spine diagram. [Original image and attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Scoliosis_cobb.svg). Online image; internet required.*
Scoliosis is defined as a three-dimensional spinal curvature featuring a lateral curvature $>10^\circ$ on a standing AP radiograph.

* **Cobb's Angle**: Measured by drawing perpendicular lines from the top endplate of the most tilted superior end-vertebra and the bottom endplate of the most tilted inferior end-vertebra.
* **Adam's Forward Bending Test**: Asymmetry of the rib cage or lumbar spine (**rib hump**) during forward flexion demonstrates rotational vertebral deformity.
* **Risser Classification**: Evaluates skeletal maturity by measuring ossification progression across the iliac apophysis (Risser 0 = no ossification; Risser 5 = complete apophyseal fusion, signaling completion of skeletal growth).
