# Orthopaedics Chapter 09: Orthopaedic Oncology & Bone Tumours

![[09_Orthopaedic_Oncology_and_Bone_Tumour 2026-11-22 11.00.00.excalidraw]]

## 1. Diagnostic Triad & Biomechanical Principles in Musculoskeletal Oncology

- Musculoskeletal oncology requires a systematic diagnostic approach combining patient age, radiological appearance and lesion location, and histopathological evaluation.
- Relying on any single modality risks misdiagnosis, as benign entities like osteomyelitis or avascular necrosis can closely mimic malignant neoplasms both radiologically and histologically.

### 1.1 The Diagnostic Triad
Primary evaluation rests upon three essential diagnostic pillars:
* **Patient Age**: The single most predictive clinical variable. Bone tumors exhibit strict age-specific incidence peaks. For example, primary malignant tumors in children and adolescents are predominantly osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma, whereas bone tumors in adults over 50 years of age are overwhelmingly metastatic carcinoma or multiple myeloma.
* **Radiological Appearance & Anatomical Localization**: Standard two-plane radiographs evaluate lesion margins (zone of transition), cortical integrity, matrix mineralization (osteoid vs. chondroid), and exact anatomical positioning within the bone along longitudinal (epiphyseal, metaphyseal, diaphyseal) and transverse (centric, eccentric, cortical, juxtacortical) axes.
* **Histopathology**: Confirms cell lineage and mitotic grade. Biopsy must be performed after complete imaging staging (MRI/CT) along the planned future surgical incision line to prevent tumor seeding of contamination compartments.

### 1.2 Mechanics of Periosteal Reaction



*Laminated, spiculated and Codman triangle periosteal reactions — schematic.*

The periosteum responds to underlying cortical irritation or tumor expansion by laying down new reactive bone. The pattern of periosteal reaction reflects the rate of tumor growth and the biological aggressiveness of the underlying process:

| Periosteal Pattern | Growth Kinetics | Biological Aggressiveness | Representative Condition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Thick / Solid** | Indolent, slow-growing | Low / Benign | Osteoid osteoma, Osteomyelitis, Stress fracture |
| **Lamellated ("Onion-Skin")** | Intermittent / Concentric waves | High / Aggressive | Ewing sarcoma |
| **Spiculated ("Sunburst" / "Hair-on-End")** | Rapid perpendicular bone growth | Very High / Malignant | Osteosarcoma |
| **Codman's Triangle** | Rapid cortical breakthrough elevating periosteum | Extremely Aggressive / High Grade | High-grade Osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma |

### 1.3 Enneking Surgical Staging System for Bone Tumors
The Enneking System (Musculoskeletal Tumor Society [MSTS]) classifies musculoskeletal lesions based on Surgical Grade (G), Surgical Site / Compartment (T), and Regional/Distant Metastasis (M):
* **Surgical Grade (G)**: $G_0$ = Benign; $G_1$ = Low-grade malignant; $G_2$ = High-grade malignant.
* **Anatomical Compartment (T)**:
  * $T_1$ (Intracompartmental): Tumor contained within anatomical borders (e.g., intraosseous, subperiosteal, intrafascial compartment).
  * $T_2$ (Extracompartmental): Tumor extends beyond anatomical borders into adjacent soft tissues or interfascial planes.
* **Metastasis (M)**: $M_0$ = No regional or distant metastasis; $M_1$ = Regional nodal or distant organ metastasis (lung is the most common distant metastatic site).

## 2. Anatomic Localization & Differential Diagnosis Matrix



*Epiphyseal, metaphyseal and diaphyseal tumor location — schematic.*


Anatomical mapping within long bones provides immediate diagnostic narrowing, as specific cellular origins preferentially reside in distinct microenvironmental zones.

### 2.1 Epiphyseal Lesions
True epiphyseal tumors are rare and characteristically occur in close proximity to joint surfaces:
1. **Giant Cell Tumor (GCT / Osteoclastoma)**: Epiphyseal, expansile lytic lesion occurring **after epiphyseal growth plate closure** (skeletally mature adults, ages 20–40 years).
2. **Chondroblastoma (Codman's Tumor)**: Epiphyseal, calcified lytic lesion occurring **prior to epiphyseal growth plate closure** (skeletally immature children/adolescents, ages 10–20 years).

### 2.2 Metaphyseal Lesions
The metaphysis is the most common site for primary bone tumors due to high vascularity and rapid cellular turnover during active skeletal growth:
* **Malignant**: Osteosarcoma (most common).
* **Benign / Non-Neoplastic**: Osteochondroma (exostosis), Non-Ossifying Fibroma (NOF), Simple Bone Cyst (SBC), Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC), Osteoblastoma.

### 2.3 Diaphyseal Lesions
Diaphyseal bone tumors frequently involve the marrow cavity or cortical diaphysis:
* **Malignant**: Ewing sarcoma, Multiple Myeloma, Lymphoma of bone, Adamantinoma (tibia).
* **Benign**: Osteoid Osteoma, Fibrous Dysplasia, Histiocytosis X (Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis).

## 3. Benign Bone Tumors & Non-Neoplastic Cystic Lesions

### 3.1 Osteochondroma (Cartilaginous Exostosis)


![Radiograph of femoral exostosis in a child.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/EXOSTOSE.jpg)

*Visual reference: Radiograph of femoral exostosis in a child. [Original image and attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:EXOSTOSE.jpg). Online image; internet required.*
* **Pathophysiology**: Most common benign bone tumor overall. Represents an aberrant outgrowth of epiphyseal plate cartilage that ossifies endochondrally, developing into a bony stalk attached to parent bone metaphysis.
* **Anatomical Orientation**: Points **away from the adjacent joint** (along the pull of muscular tendons).
* **Radiological Hallmark**: Direct **continuity of parent bone cortex and medullary cavity** into the exostosis.
* **Malignant Transformation Risk**: Covered by a cartilaginous cap. A cartilaginous cap thickness $>1.5\text{ cm}$ in adults indicates malignant transformation to **Chondrosarcoma**. Multiple lesions occur in **Hereditary Multiple Exostoses (HME)**, an autosomal dominant disorder with an increased risk of chondrosarcoma transformation ($5\text{--}10\%$).

### 3.2 Osteoid Osteoma & Osteoblastoma
* **Osteoid Osteoma**:
  * *Clinical Features*: Diaphyseal cortical lesion in young adults (ages 10–25 years). Characterized by **severe nocturnal pain** caused by high tumor prostaglandin $E_2$ production.
  * *Pharmacological Hallmark*: Pain is **dramatically and completely relieved within 20–30 minutes by Aspirin or NSAIDs**.
  * *Radiology*: Radiolucent central **nidus** ($<2\text{ cm}$ in diameter) surrounded by dense, sclerotic reactive cortical bone.
  * *Treatment*: CT-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) or surgical excision of the nidus.
* **Osteoblastoma**: Histologically identical to osteoid osteoma but larger ($>2\text{ cm}$ nidus), commonly located in posterior elements of spine (vertebral arches), causes dull ache **not relieved by aspirin**, and lacks surrounding dense cortical reaction.

### 3.3 Giant Cell Tumor (GCT / Osteoclastoma)


![Illustrative radiograph of a giant-cell tumour of bone.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Giant%20cell%20tumor%20of%20bone08.JPG)

*Visual reference: Illustrative radiograph of a giant-cell tumour of bone. [Original image and attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Giant_cell_tumor_of_bone08.JPG). Online image; internet required.*
* **Pathophysiology**: Locally aggressive, benign epiphyseal tumor composed of mononuclear stromal cells (true neoplastic component) secreting **RANKL**, which recruits abundant multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells.
* **Radiological Appearance**: Expansile, eccentric, purely lytic lesion in the epiphysis extending to subchondral bone ("soap-bubble appearance") without sclerotic margins.
* **Histology**: Prominent multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells containing 50–100 nuclei evenly distributed among mononuclear stromal cells.
* **Management**: Extended curettage with high-speed burr, local adjuvants (phenol, liquid nitrogen, or bone cement/PMMA), or targeted biological therapy using **Denosumab** (monoclonal antibody against RANKL).

### 3.4 Simple Bone Cyst (SBC) & Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC)
* **Simple Bone Cyst (Unicameral Bone Cyst)**:
  * Fluid-filled metaphyseal cavity occurring in children (proximal humerus and proximal femur).
  * *Pathognomonic Radiological Sign*: **Fallen Fragment Sign** — a cortical fracture fragment settles at the dependent bottom of the fluid-filled cyst following pathological fracture.
* **Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC)**:
  * Eccentric, expansile, blood-filled cavernous space in metaphysis in adolescents.
  * *Radiological / MRI Sign*: Characteristic **fluid-fluid levels** on MRI caused by blood stratification of varying densities.

### 3.5 Fibrous Dysplasia
* **Pathophysiology**: Congenital developmental defect where normal bone marrow is replaced by benign fibro-osseous tissue with disorganized immature woven bone trabeculae lacking osteoblastic rimming ("Chinese character" / "C-shaped" trabeculae).
* **Radiological Features**: Intramedullary osteopenic lesion with a **ground-glass matrix** appearance. In the proximal femur, recurrent micro-fractures lead to a progressive varus deformity known as the **Shepherd's Crook Deformity**.
* **McCune-Albright Syndrome**: Triad of **Polyostotic Fibrous Dysplasia**, **Café-au-lait skin macules** with irregular borders ("Coast of Maine"), and **Precocious Puberty** (endocrinopathy).

## 4. Malignant Primary Bone Tumors

### 4.1 Osteosarcoma
* **Pathophysiology**: Most common primary non-hematological malignant bone tumor. Characterized by direct production of malignant **osteoid** or bone matrix by pleomorphic tumor cells.
* **Demographics**: Bimodal distribution; major peak in adolescents (10–20 years during pubertal growth spurt) and minor secondary peak in elderly adults ($>65\text{ years}$) secondary to Paget's disease or prior radiation.
* **Predilection Site**: Metaphysis of long bones around the knee joint ($50\%$ cases involve distal femur or proximal tibia).
* **Radiological Features**:
  * Mixed lytic and sclerotic metaphyseal lesion.
  * **Sunburst Pattern** / **Hair-on-End Appearance**: Perpendicular mineralized tumor osteoid.
  * **Codman's Triangle**: Triangular elevation of periosteum at proximal/distal tumor margins.
* **Management Protocol**: Neoadjuvant multi-agent chemotherapy (Doxorubicin, Cisplatin, High-dose Methotrexate, Ifosfamide) $\rightarrow$ Wide limb-salvage surgical resection $\rightarrow$ Post-operative adjuvant chemotherapy.

### 4.2 Ewing Sarcoma


![Paediatric tibial radiograph illustrating Ewing sarcoma.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Ewing%20sarcoma%20tibia%20child%20(cropped).jpg)

*Visual reference: Paediatric tibial radiograph illustrating Ewing sarcoma. [Original image and attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ewing_sarcoma_tibia_child_(cropped).jpg). Online image; internet required.*
* **Pathophysiology**: Highly malignant neuroectodermal small round blue cell tumor arising from primitive neural crest cells.
* **Cytogenetics**: Driven by characteristic chromosomal translocation **$t(11;22)(q24;q12)$**, generating the **EWS-FLI1** fusion oncogene.
* **Demographics & Site**: Children and young adolescents (ages 5–15 years); located in the **diaphysis** of long bones (femur, tibia) and flat pelvic bones.
* **Clinical Presentation**: Frequently mimics acute osteomyelitis, presenting with localized bone pain, fever, warmth, leukocytosis, and elevated ESR.
* **Radiological Feature**: Concentric, multi-layered periosteal reaction producing a classic **Onion-Skin Pattern**.
* **Histology**: Sheets of uniform small round blue cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, glycogen-rich cytoplasm (Periodic Acid-Schiff [PAS] positive), and **Homer-Wright pseudorosettes**.

### 4.3 Chondrosarcoma
* **Pathophysiology**: Malignant cartilage-producing tumor occurring in mature adults ($>40\text{ years}$).
* **Anatomical Distribution**: Involves axial skeleton and flat bones (pelvis, scapula, ribs) and proximal shafts of femur and humerus.
* **Radiology**: Destructive osteolytic lesion with punctate, ring-and-arc, or **popcorn calcifications** within the cartilaginous matrix.
* **Therapeutic Resistance**: Highly resistant to standard chemotherapy and radiation therapy; primary treatment relies entirely on **wide surgical excision**.

### 4.4 Multiple Myeloma


![Skull radiograph with multiple punched-out osteolytic lesions; finding is not specific to myeloma.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Osteolytic%20Lesions.jpg)

*Visual reference: Skull radiograph with multiple punched-out osteolytic lesions; finding is not specific to myeloma. [Original image and attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Osteolytic_Lesions.jpg). Online image; internet required.*
* **Pathophysiology**: Most common primary malignant tumor of bone overall in adults ($>50\text{ years}$). Monoclonal neoplastic proliferation of plasma cells within the bone marrow.
* **Pathognomonic Manifestations (CRAB Criteria)**:
  * **C**alcium Elevation (Hypercalcemia).
  * **R**enal Insufficiency (Myeloma kidney / light chain cast nephropathy).
  * **A**nemia (Normocytic normochromic).
  * **B**one Lesions (Punched-out lytic lesions).
* **Radiological Hallmarks**: Multiple, sharply demarcated, **punched-out osteolytic lesions** without surrounding reactive sclerosis (due to osteoblast inhibition by DKK-1) across the skull ("rain-drop skull"), spine, and pelvis.

## 5. Metastatic Bone Disease & Pathological Fractures

Metastatic carcinomas represent the most common malignant bone tumors overall, outnumbering primary bone malignancies by a ratio of $25:1$.

### 5.1 Osteolytic vs. Osteoblastic Metastases
Metastatic bone lesions disrupt normal remodeling kinetics, presenting as lytic, blastic, or mixed processes:
* **Osteolytic Metastases**: Driven by tumor secretion of PTHrP, IL-6, and RANKL, stimulating osteoclast-mediated bone destruction.
  * *Primary Sources*: **Renal Cell Carcinoma**, **Thyroid Carcinoma**, **Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer**, Gastrointestinal malignancies, and Triple-Negative Breast Cancer.
  * *Complications*: Severe bone pain, hypercalcemia of malignancy, and acute pathological fractures.
* **Osteoblastic (Sclerotic) Metastases**: Driven by tumor secretion of ET-1 and BMPs, stimulating uncoupled reactive osteoblast bone formation.
  * *Primary Source*: **Prostate Carcinoma** (overwhelmingly osteoblastic), Carcinoid tumors, and Small Cell Lung Cancer.
* **Mixed Metastases**: Commonly seen in **Breast Carcinoma** (combines osteolytic and osteoblastic elements).

### 5.2 Mirels Scoring System for Impending Pathological Fractures
The Mirels Scoring System quantifies pathological fracture risk in long bones affected by metastatic disease. A cumulative score $\ge 8$ points indicates high fracture risk and warrants **prophylactic surgical internal fixation** before weight-bearing:

| Score Metric | 1 Point | 2 Points | 3 Points |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Site** | Upper Limb | Lower Limb | Peritrochanteric (Hip) |
| **Pain Level** | Mild | Moderate | Functional / Severe |
| **Lesion Type** | Blastic | Mixed | Lytic |
| **Cortical Size** | $<1/3$ width | $1/3\text{--}2/3$ width | $>2/3$ width |
