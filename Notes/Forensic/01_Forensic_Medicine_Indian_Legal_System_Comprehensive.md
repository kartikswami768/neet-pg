> **Scope of this source:** The supplied 28-page PDF is a focused medico-legal teaching module. Its substantive content covers the **Indian legal system, evidence and witnesses, medical law and ethics, medical negligence, consent in medical practice, and medical-record maintenance**. It does **not** contain substantive chapters on mechanical injuries, asphyxia, drowning, firearm injuries, identification, toxicology/poisoning, sexual offences, obstetric forensic medicine, or forensic psychiatry. Those topics have therefore not been invented or appended merely to match a generic FMT syllabus.

> **Reconstruction method:** The PDF was reviewed as a complete document and its pages were visually inspected after rendering. Obvious OCR errors were corrected. Where a legally or clinically important statement from the source is outdated or incomplete, the original teaching point is retained and a clearly labelled **Current verification / update** is provided.

> **Important date:** Current legal references in this document are checked against sources available on **20 September 2026**. The PDF itself is a 2023 teaching source and uses the former IPC/CrPC/Indian Evidence Act framework.

# 1. Foundations of Forensic Medicine and Indian Legal System

## 1.1 Forensic medicine

**Source PDF definition (p. 1):**

> Forensic medicine is the application of medical knowledge to legal purposes, to aid in the administration of justice.

This is the core relationship between medicine and law: medical knowledge is used to answer questions that arise during legal investigation, adjudication, certification, and administration of justice.

## 1.2 Medical jurisprudence

**Source PDF definition (p. 1):**

> Medical jurisprudence is the application of knowledge of law in relation to the practice of medicine.

### Etymology given in the source

- **Juris** = law
- **Prudentia** = knowledge

The PDF therefore distinguishes:

| Concept | Source definition / emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Forensic medicine** | Application of medical knowledge to legal purposes |
| **Medical jurisprudence** | Application of knowledge of law to the practice of medicine |
| **Medical ethics** | Moral principles guiding medical conduct |
| **Medical etiquette** | Professional courtesy, especially between doctors |

---

## 1.3 Medical ethics vs medical etiquette

### Medical ethics

The PDF describes medical ethics as:

- A set of moral principles.
- Principles guiding doctors in dealing with:
  - Patients
  - Other doctors
  - The State/society
- A form of professional conduct imposed through the medical regulatory framework.
- Violation may result in professional disciplinary action.

### Medical etiquette

The PDF describes etiquette as:

- Conventional professional courtesy.
- Particularly relevant to dealings between doctors/colleagues.
- Example given: courtesy toward fellow doctors and their families, including the source's teaching point that a doctor should not charge a colleague/family member for consultation.

> **Interpretive note:** The simple teaching distinction in the PDF - "ethics = enforceable professional conduct; etiquette = courtesy" - is useful for exams, but etiquette should not be assumed to be legally irrelevant in every circumstance. A conduct issue can become disciplinary if it falls within an enforceable professional-conduct rule.

## 1.4 Historical statutory framework in the PDF vs current framework

The PDF repeatedly uses the older Indian legal framework:

- **Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC) - criminal offences and punishments**
- **Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC) - criminal procedure**
- **Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (IEA) - evidence law**

These are now historical references for current practice because the three central replacement codes came into force on **1 July 2024**.

| PDF / historical framework | Current framework | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Indian Penal Code, 1860 | **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)** | In force from 1 July 2024 |
| Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 | **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** | In force from 1 July 2024 |
| Indian Evidence Act, 1872 | **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** | In force from 1 July 2024 |

**Current verification:** India Code identifies BNS, BNSS and BSA as the operative statutes from 1 July 2024. Their repeal provisions also preserve certain earlier proceedings and liabilities where applicable, so an old case can still require reference to the previous law. [See External Verification Sources.]

> **Exam note:** When a question is explicitly based on an old textbook/PDF, recognize the old section number; when asked for the **current law**, use BNS/BNSS/BSA unless the question specifically concerns a pre-1 July 2024 proceeding.

## 1.5 Civil case vs criminal case

The source presents the following basic comparison:

| Feature | Civil case | Criminal case |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Dispute involving private rights / compensation | Offence against law/state/society |
| Parties | Plaintiff vs defendant | Prosecution/state vs accused/defendant |
| Typical objective | Compensation/remedy | Punishment for an offence |
| Burden of proof | Generally on the claimant/plaintiff | On the prosecution |
| Standard described in PDF | Probability / preponderance | Beyond reasonable doubt |
| Typical punishment/remedy | Monetary compensation | Fine and/or imprisonment |

### Important terminology

- **Plaintiff:** party bringing a civil action.
- **Defendant:** party defending the civil claim; the word is also used more broadly in some legal contexts.
- **Prosecution:** state/public case against an accused in a criminal proceeding.

> **Exam pearl:** Do not interchange the burden of proof or standard of proof in civil and criminal proceedings. Criminal conviction requires proof beyond reasonable doubt; civil liability is ordinarily decided on the balance/preponderance of probabilities.

## 1.6 Cognizable offence vs non-cognizable offence

### Source-PDF comparison

| Feature | Cognizable offence | Non-cognizable offence |
|---|---|---|
| Police power | Police may arrest without warrant, subject to statutory conditions | Police generally require warrant/appropriate Magistrate process for arrest |
| PDF examples | Murder, robbery, dowry death, suspicious death-related examples | Simple hurt, defamation |
| Core idea | More serious category in the procedural classification | Police powers are more restricted |

> **Source caveat:** The examples in the PDF are teaching examples rather than an exhaustive classification. The exact current classification of an offence must be checked in the applicable statute/schedule.

---

## 1.7 Summons case vs warrant case

### Source-PDF teaching rule

| Summons case | Warrant case |
|---|---|
| Case other than a warrant case | Case involving an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding the statutory threshold specified by the procedural law |

For the current BNSS classification, a **warrant-case** is an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life, or imprisonment for a term **exceeding two years**; a **summons-case** is a case relating to an offence that is not a warrant-case.

> **Do not confuse "warrant case" with "a case in which a warrant has been issued."** It is a statutory classification of the offence.

---

## 1.8 Bailable offence vs non-bailable offence

### Source-PDF teaching rule

- **Bailable offence:** bail is ordinarily available as a matter of statutory right subject to the conditions of law.
- **Non-bailable offence:** bail is **not** an automatic right; the competent court decides according to the applicable law.

> **Current correction:** The PDF's shorthand statement that bail "can't be granted" in non-bailable offences is too absolute. **Non-bailable does not mean non-bailable under all circumstances**; it means bail is not a right and is governed by judicial/statutory discretion.

---

## 1.9 Judicial Magistrate vs Executive Magistrate

### Source-PDF comparison

| Feature | Judicial Magistrate | Executive Magistrate |
|---|---|---|
| Broad role | Judicial adjudication / trial-related functions | Executive/administrative and preventive functions under criminal procedure |
| PDF examples | Chief Judicial Magistrate, JM First Class, JM Second Class | District Magistrate, Additional District Magistrate, Sub-Divisional Magistrate and other executive magistracy posts |
| Appointment/administration in source | Linked to the High Court/judicial structure | Linked to the State executive/administration |
| Core exam idea | Court/judicial work | Law-and-order / executive functions |

### Current BNSS position

BNSS section 23 provides sentencing powers for Magistrates, including:

- **Chief Judicial Magistrate:** may pass any sentence authorized by law **except** death, imprisonment for life, or imprisonment exceeding 7 years.
- **Magistrate of the First Class:** up to 3 years' imprisonment, fine up to ₹50,000, both, or community service within the statutory framework.
- **Magistrate of the Second Class:** up to 1 year, fine up to ₹10,000, both, or community service within the statutory framework.

> **Correction to the PDF:** The PDF table gives older sentencing figures, including a ₹5,000 limit for JM Second Class and a ₹10,000 figure for JM First Class. Those are historical figures and should not be used as current BNSS limits.

# 1.10 Inquest

## Definition

**Inquest** = an inquiry into the cause and circumstances of an **unnatural or suspicious death**.

The source's core phrase is:

> "Enquiry into the cause of unnatural death."

The important practical purpose is to document circumstances around death and provide an investigative basis for deciding whether further medico-legal investigation/autopsy is required.

## 1.10.1 Types of inquest systems mentioned in the PDF

| System | Status described in PDF |
|---|---|
| Police inquest | Followed in India; most common |
| Magistrate inquest | Followed in India |
| Coroner system | Abolished in India |
| Medical examiner system | Source associates it with the USA, not India |
| Procurator fiscal system | Source associates it with Scotland |

> **Current terminology:** Under the present BNSS, it is more precise to speak of a **police investigation/inquest under section 194** and **Magistrate inquiry under section 196** rather than simply importing the old CrPC terminology unchanged.

---

## 1.10.2 Police inquest

### Source-PDF workflow

**Death reported in a circumstance covered by the inquest provisions**  
↓  
**Investigating/inquest police officer**  
↓  
**Visit scene / examine body**  
↓  
**Examine witnesses/panchas**  
↓  
**Prepare inquest report (panchanama)**  
↓  
**Forward body for post-mortem/examination when indicated**

### Circumstances listed by the source

- Suicide
- Homicide
- Accident
- Death due to an animal
- Suspicious circumstances

The PDF identifies the old **CrPC section 174** as the statutory basis.

### Witnesses and report

- Witnesses in the source are referred to as **panchas**.
- The inquest report is described as a **panchanama**.
- The source describes the inquest officer/IO as having power to summon relevant witnesses for inquiry under the old CrPC section 175.

### Current legal position

The current BNSS section **194** deals with police procedure in specified cases of death, including suicide and deaths in suspicious circumstances. It requires the police to inform the nearest Executive Magistrate empowered to hold inquests and sets out additional requirements for deaths of women within seven years of marriage in specified circumstances.

> **Important correction:** Do not simply memorize "CrPC 174" as current law. For current practice, the corresponding BNSS provision is **section 194**.

---

## 1.10.3 Magistrate inquiry into cause of death

### Source-PDF teaching points

The PDF describes Magistrate inquest/inquiry as a more independent/superior form of inquiry compared with a police inquest.

It lists the following situations:

- Custodial death
- Disappearance in custody
- Rape alleged in custody
- Suspected dowry death
- Exhumation
- Death in an asylum/institutional setting
- Death in a Borstal/juvenile institution (historical terminology)

It associates the former **CrPC section 176** with Magistrate inquest.

### Current BNSS position - section 196

BNSS section 196 provides for **inquiry by a Magistrate into the cause of death**.

A Magistrate inquiry is mandatory in specified cases including:

- A person dying or disappearing while in police custody or other legally authorised custody.
- Alleged rape of a woman while she is in police or other legally authorised custody.

The Magistrate may also hold an inquiry, instead of or in addition to the police investigation, in other cases covered by the statutory framework.

The Magistrate may order **disinterment (exhumation)** when examination of an already buried body is considered necessary to discover the cause of death.

Where practicable, relatives known to the authorities are to be informed and allowed to remain present at the inquiry. The provision also contains a rule regarding forwarding the body for medical examination within 24 hours in the specified inquiry situation, unless reasons are recorded why this is not possible.

> **Current high-yield distinction:**
>
> - **BNSS 194** -> police inquest/report in specified deaths.
> - **BNSS 196** -> Magistrate inquiry into cause of death; special mandatory circumstances include specified custodial deaths/disappearances and custodial rape allegations; also provides the legal mechanism for exhumation in the relevant situation.

## 1.10.4 Dowry/suspicious death within seven years of marriage

The PDF states, in simplified form:

> Suicide or suspicious death of a married woman within 7 years of marriage.

### Current clarification

The **seven-year period is legally important**, but the modern statutory wording should not be reduced to "every suspicious death of every married woman within seven years is automatically a dowry death." The substantive offence of dowry death and the procedural inquest requirements have their own statutory elements. For current medico-legal work, record the actual circumstances carefully and apply the specific provisions of BNS/BNSS and the Dowry Prohibition Act as relevant.

# 1.12 Punishments, death sentence and mercy

## 1.12.1 Death sentence

The PDF states:

- Death sentence in India is limited by the **"rarest of rare"** doctrine.
- Judicial execution by hanging is the method taught in the PDF.
- The Sessions Court is the lowest level court shown as capable of passing a death sentence, subject to the statutory confirmation/appellate process.

### Current legal structure

A death sentence passed by a Sessions Court requires the statutory **confirmation procedure before the High Court**. Appeals/review/curative and mercy processes have separate legal routes.

### Important correction: mercy is not "President only"

The PDF states that pardoning a death sentence is only by the President. This is incomplete.

- **Article 72 of the Constitution** gives the President powers of pardon, reprieve, respite, remission and commutation in specified cases, including death sentences.
- **Article 161** gives the Governor corresponding powers in relation to matters within the State's executive sphere.
- The current BNSS contains a statutory procedure for mercy petitions in death-sentence cases.

> **Exam pearl:** Distinguish **judicial process** (trial, confirmation, appeal) from **constitutional clemency** (President/Governor) and the statutory procedure under BNSS.

## 1.12.2 Pregnant woman sentenced to death

The PDF quotes the older CrPC framework for postponement/commutation where a pregnant woman is sentenced to death.

> **Current-law note:** Use the current BNSS provision rather than memorizing the old CrPC section number. The existence of a special statutory safeguard for pregnancy should be remembered, but the exact present section number should be checked from the current BNSS when answering a current-law question.

# 1.13 Juvenile Justice Board and children in conflict with law

The PDF teaches:

- A **child** for the juvenile justice framework is a person below 18 years.
- Juvenile Justice Board consists of 3 members, including a Principal Magistrate and two social workers; at least one member should be female.
- The PDF describes offences as "heinous" and gives examples such as murder and rape.
- It teaches a distinction for children aged 16-18.

### Current correction: "heinous offence" and 16-18-year-olds

The current **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015** does not provide a blanket rule that every 16-18-year-old accused of a heinous offence is simply "treated as an adult" and can receive every adult punishment.

For a child aged **16-18 years** alleged to have committed a **heinous offence**, the Board undertakes a **preliminary assessment** under section 15 concerning, among other matters:

- Mental and physical capacity to commit the offence.
- Ability to understand the consequences of the alleged offence.
- The circumstances in which the alleged offence was committed.

The Board may take assistance from experienced psychologists or other experts as provided by the Act. The subsequent statutory pathway depends on the Board's order and the Children’s Court framework.

### Heinous offence - current terminology

The current statutory definition is based on offences for which the **minimum punishment is seven years or more**.

> **Correction to the PDF:** The source says punishment ">7 years". The statutory concept is tied to a **minimum punishment of 7 years or more**, not strictly "more than 7 years".

# 2. Evidence, Witnesses and Court Procedure

# 2.1 Evidence

The PDF defines evidence as a statement/document given to prove a fact or truth.

## 2.1.1 Direct vs circumstantial evidence

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Direct evidence** | Directly proves a fact, e.g. eyewitness observation; the source also gives CCTV as an example of direct proof |
| **Circumstantial evidence** | Proves facts/circumstances from which another fact may be inferred |
| **Indirect evidence** | Source uses this as a broad category for evidence that does not directly prove the fact |

> **Terminology caution:** Circumstantial evidence is a structured form of indirect proof, not an "inferior" category. Its legal weight depends on the facts and whether the inferential chain is complete.

## 2.1.2 Hearsay evidence

The source states that hearsay is evidence heard from somebody else and teaches that hearsay is admissible in examples such as:

- **Dying declaration**
- **Res gestae**

### Current correction

The source's statement that hearsay is valid **only** in dying declarations and res gestae is an exam shorthand, not a complete statement of modern evidence law. The **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** contains several statutory rules dealing with statements of persons who are dead, unavailable, or otherwise within specified exceptions, as well as other forms of relevant evidence.

---

# 2.2 Oral and documentary evidence

The source classifies evidence according to how it is given in court:

### Oral evidence

- Given through a witness.
- The source calls this "superior" in the sense that the witness can ordinarily be cross-examined.

### Documentary evidence

The source specifically highlights documents generated by doctors, including:

1. **Dying declaration** when recorded as a document.
2. **Medical certificates** at the patient's request, e.g.:
   - Sickness certificate
   - Fitness certificate
   - Leave certificate
3. **Medicolegal reports** requested in an investigation, such as:
   - Autopsy/post-mortem report
   - Wound/injury certificate
   - Examination of a victim in a sexual-offence case
   - Examination of an accused in a sexual-offence case

# 2.3 Witness

**Witness:** a person who gives evidence/statement to prove a fact.

## Types of witness in the PDF

1. Common / fact / occurrence / lay witness
2. Expert witness

---

## 2.3.1 Common / fact witness

The source teaches:

- May be a person who actually observed the fact.
- First-hand knowledge is the normal rule.
- The witness can testify about **facts**, rather than giving expert opinions merely because they are present in the case.
- The PDF notes that an eye witness and some hearsay witnesses can fall within this broad category.
- It teaches that there is no absolute age cut-off merely because a person is a witness.

### Special situations mentioned in the source

The PDF also lists:

- Insane person
- Dumb witness

as historical teaching examples of persons who may still be capable of giving evidence depending on their ability to communicate and understand the proceedings.

> **Current terminology note:** Do not use "dumb witness" as contemporary clinical language. For examination of the source, understand it as the historical phrase used in the notes for a person unable to communicate by ordinary speech. Modern evidence law focuses on competency and the ability to communicate evidence.

---

## 2.3.2 Expert witness

An expert witness is a person with special:

- Skill
- Knowledge
- Experience
- Training

in a particular field, allowing them to express an opinion on matters requiring expertise.

### Examples from the PDF

- Fingerprint expert
- Ballistics expert

### Doctor as common + expert witness

The source emphasizes an important forensic point:

> **The same doctor can be both a fact witness and an expert witness.**

For an autopsy doctor:

- **Recorded observations/findings** -> fact/ordinary witness component.
- **Interpretation/opinion based on those findings** -> expert witness component.

> **Exam pearl:** The doctor should distinguish clearly between **what was observed** and **what is inferred from the observations**.

- Issued by the presiding court authority.

- Means, in substance, **bring/produce the document or thing**.

**Conduct money** = money paid to a witness to cover travelling/attendance expenses associated with the summons.

The PDF states:

- **Criminal case:** the source notes that expert witnesses are eligible for conduct money after giving evidence.

# 2.7 Deposition and situations where a witness need not physically attend

**Deposition** = giving evidence in a judicial proceeding, including evidence of an expert witness.

The PDF lists situations where a person/document may be used without requiring the witness to attend in the ordinary way, including:

1. Dying declaration.
2. Expert opinions contained in standard textbooks.
3. A medical witness whose opinion was already given in a lower court with cross-examination, in the source's teaching example.
4. Certain public records, e.g. birth/death records.
5. Certain hospital records, e.g. vital signs and input/output charts.
6. Certain government officers and scientific authorities, including the Chemical Examiner and fingerprint authorities.
7. A witness who is incapable of coming to court or whose attendance would cause unreasonable delay, subject to the applicable law/order.

> **Current correction:** Whether a particular document, expert report, or previous testimony may be admitted without oral testimony depends on the BSA, BNSS, court order and the specific evidentiary rule. The PDF's list should not be treated as an automatic exemption list in every case.

# 2.8 Dying declaration

## Definition

The PDF describes a **dying declaration** as a statement made by a person concerning an unlawful act when the person later dies, which is recorded and presented as evidence.

The statement may be:

- Oral
- Written
- By gestures/signs where reliably interpretable

The source highlights that once written down, it may appear in documentary form while still reflecting the declarant's statement.

## Historical statutory reference in the PDF

The source associates dying declarations with **section 32 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872**.

## Current statutory framework

The current **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, section 26** contains the relevant rule on statements of a person who is dead, cannot be found, becomes incapable of giving evidence, or whose attendance cannot be procured without unreasonable delay or expense, including statements relevant to the cause of death or the circumstances of the transaction resulting in death.

### Important correction to the PDF

The PDF suggests that the declarant must have made the statement expecting death and must eventually die for it to operate as a dying declaration.

The current statutory rule is broader: **a statement about the cause of death or circumstances of the transaction resulting in death can be relevant even without proof that the declarant expected death at the time of making the statement.**

The practical requirement remains that the evidentiary foundation must satisfy the statutory rule and the court must assess reliability.

---

## 2.8.1 Who can record a dying declaration?

The PDF states that it may be recorded by:

- Magistrate - ideally
- Doctor
- Police officer
- Any member of the public

The key issue is not the job title of the recorder alone but the **reliability, voluntariness, mental capacity, and proper recording** of the statement.

### Role of the doctor

The PDF strongly emphasizes documentation of **compos mentis** status.

The doctor should document whether the person is:

- Conscious
- Oriented/mentally capable of understanding and answering
- Able to communicate
- Free from obvious impairment that would make the statement unreliable

> **Exam pearl:** In a dying declaration, the doctor is primarily a medical assessor/certifier of the declarant's condition and should not prompt, tutor, or lead the patient.

---

## 2.8.2 Conditions and principles taught in the PDF

The source lists:

- No force or undue influence.
- Statement should be complete enough for the court to assess it.
- No prompting.
- No alteration of words.
- No leading questions in the ordinary recording process.
- Verbatim recording where possible.
- Question-and-answer format can be used, recording both question and answer.
- Use language understood by the declarant.
- Signatures/thumb impression should be obtained where possible.
- Forward the completed document to the appropriate authority without unnecessary delay.

### What is *not* required as a general rule

- **Oath is not required** for a dying declaration.

---

## 2.8.3 Dying declaration vs dying deposition

| Feature | Dying declaration | Dying deposition |
|---|---|---|
| Source terminology | Statement of dying/dead person | Bedside judicial deposition described in the PDF |
| Recorder | Can be Magistrate, doctor, police or another competent person depending circumstances | Source says Magistrate only |
| Oath | No | Source says yes |
| Accused/defence present | Not ordinarily required | Source says yes |
| Cross-examination | No ordinary contemporaneous cross-examination | Source says cross-examination permitted |
| Leading questions | Not ordinarily used | Source says permitted |
| India | Commonly recognized concept | PDF says not practised in India |

The PDF calls dying deposition **superior**, but this should be understood as a feature-comparison teaching point rather than a universal judicial ranking.

---

## 2.8.4 If the person survives

The source says:

- If the person survives, the statement does not operate as a dying declaration merely because it was initially recorded as one.
- The person can ordinarily be called to give oral evidence if competent.
- The earlier document may have other evidentiary relevance depending on the facts and applicable law.

This is a more reliable way to remember the teaching point than simply saying that every such document becomes "invalid" for all purposes.

The PDF teaches that the witness takes an oath or affirmation before giving evidence.

Traditional form given in the source:

> "I do swear in the name of God, whatever I say shall be the truth, the whole truth and nothing but the truth."

For a person who does not wish to swear religiously, the PDF notes that a **solemn affirmation** may be used.

# 2.11 Examination of witnesses

The PDF uses the following terminology:

1. **Examination-in-chief** / chief examination / direct examination
2. **Cross-examination**
3. **Re-examination** / re-direct examination

## Current BSA structure

The current BSA section 142 defines:

- Examination-in-chief -> by the party who calls the witness.
- Cross-examination -> by the adverse party.
- Re-examination -> after cross-examination by the party who called the witness.

BSA section 143 sets out the order:

```text
Examination-in-chief
        ↓
Cross-examination
        ↓
Re-examination
```

If new matter is introduced in re-examination with the court's permission, the adverse party may further cross-examine on that new matter.

---

## 2.11.1 Examination-in-chief

**Conducted by:** same-side / calling party's lawyer.

### Objectives taught in the PDF

- Place before the court the facts relevant to the case.
- If the witness is an expert, obtain interpretation/opinion based on those facts.

### Leading questions

Generally not used to suggest the desired answer, subject to the current BSA rules and court permission for introductory, undisputed, or sufficiently established matters.

---

## 2.11.2 Cross-examination

**Conducted by:** opposing/adverse lawyer.

### Purposes taught in the PDF

- Test accuracy.
- Test credibility/veracity.
- Bring out facts favourable to the cross-examining side.
- Discredit the witness where legally permissible.

### Leading questions

**Permitted in cross-examination** under current BSA section 146.

---

## 2.11.3 Re-examination

**Conducted by:** the party who called the witness.

### Purpose

- Clarify matters raised in cross-examination.
- Remove ambiguity created during cross-examination.

### New facts

The source says no new facts should be added. The current BSA is more precise: the re-examination is directed to explaining matters referred to in cross-examination; if genuinely new matter is introduced with the court's permission, the adverse side may further cross-examine on it.

---

# 2.12 Leading question

**Definition:** a question that suggests the answer the person asking it wishes or expects to receive.

### Example

> "You were present at the scene, weren't you?"

This question suggests an affirmative answer.

### Current BSA rule - section 146

- Leading questions should not, if objected to by the adverse party, be asked in examination-in-chief or re-examination **except with the court's permission**.
- The court permits leading questions for introductory/undisputed matters and matters it considers sufficiently proved.
- Leading questions **may be asked in cross-examination**.

> **Correction to the PDF:** The source's absolute phrasing that leading questions are "only" for the opposition lawyer is too broad. The current law expressly gives the court discretion in chief/re-examination in specified situations.

---

# 2.13 Prosecution witness vs defence witness

The PDF illustrates the witness-examination pattern:

| Witness | Chief | Cross | Re-direct |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prosecution witness | Public prosecutor | Defence lawyer | Public prosecutor |
| Defence witness | Defence lawyer | Public prosecutor | Defence lawyer |

An expert doctor may appear for either side depending on how the witness is called and the issue on which evidence is sought.

# 2.14 Questions by the judge

The source teaches that the judge may ask questions at any stage of the trial and may inquire about facts/evidence needed to arrive at a just decision, subject to the law.

> **Current principle:** Judicial questioning powers are governed by the BSA and procedural law. The source's old Indian Evidence Act section number should be treated as historical.

# 2.15 Recall / re-examination of a witness

The source states that the prosecution can ask the court to recall or re-examine a witness where necessary, referring to the old CrPC provision.

Again, this is a **historical section reference** in the PDF. The power and procedure now arise under the **BNSS** and the court's procedural authority.

# 2.16 Hostile witness

The PDF describes a hostile/unfavourable witness as one who, for example:

- Becomes adverse to the side that called them.
- Contradicts a previous statement.
- Conceals or departs from expected evidence.

The source teaches that once declared hostile, the same-side lawyer may ask leading questions, with the court controlling the process.

> **Current-law note:** The fact that a witness is treated as hostile does not make the entire testimony automatically useless. The admissibility, reliability and effect of particular portions are matters for the court.

# 3.8 Professional secrecy / confidentiality

The PDF teaches:

# 3.9 Privileged communication

# 3.12 Euthanasia and end-of-life care

The PDF defines euthanasia as:

- "Good death"
- Mercy killing
- Traditionally described as ending the life of a patient with terminal incurable illness and severe suffering.

## 3.12.1 Source classification

| Type | Source description |
|---|---|
| **Active** | Commission; source gives IV opioids such as morphine as an example; says illegal in India |
| **Passive** | Omission/withholding; source uses DNR/letting die as examples; says permitted in India |

### Other classifications in the source

- **Voluntary** - according to the patient's will.
- **Involuntary** - against the patient's will.
- **Non-voluntary** - patient cannot decide, e.g. coma.
- **Battlefield euthanasia** - terminally/fatally wounded soldiers (source term).
- **Paediatric euthanasia** - terminal incurable illness in infants (source term).
- **Geriatric euthanasia** - elderly person with terminal incurable illness (source term).

### Current legal/ethical update - important

The modern Indian legal position is more precise than the old "active vs passive euthanasia" shorthand.

The Supreme Court has recognized that:

- A competent patient has a right to refuse medical treatment, including life-sustaining treatment.
- Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment can be lawful in specified circumstances and subject to safeguards.
- **Active euthanasia remains impermissible.**

In **2026**, the Supreme Court's *Harish Rana v. Union of India* decision further clarified the terminology: the Court stated that "passive euthanasia" is an obsolete/confusing label and preferred **"withholding or withdrawing of medical treatment"**. The Court reaffirmed the legal framework of safeguards, including the best-interests approach for patients lacking decision-making capacity.

> **Correction to the source:** Do not equate "DNR" automatically with euthanasia. A DNR order and the legally regulated withholding/withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment are conceptually related but not identical. The current legal framework is centred on the patient's rights, clinical circumstances, safeguards, and end-of-life care rather than a simple active/passive binary.

# 4. Medical Negligence and Medicolegal Proceedings

# 4.1 Medical negligence / professional malpractice

The PDF defines professional negligence/malpractice as damage to the patient resulting from:

- **Omission of the right thing**, or
- **Commission of a wrong thing**,

associated with absence of reasonable skill/care or wilful negligence by the doctor, resulting in patient harm.

### Core chain

```text
Duty of care
    ↓
Breach / dereliction
    ↓
Causation
    ↓
Damage
```

---

# 4.2 Bolam's test

The PDF teaches:

> A doctor is not liable when standard/accepted clinical practice was followed.

### Current legal refinement

The **Bolam principle** is a major negligence concept, but it should not be reduced to "any accepted practice = no liability." Courts examine whether the professional acted with the skill and care reasonably expected in the circumstances and whether the clinical approach is defensible under the applicable law.

Also:

> **Mere error of judgment or an unsuccessful outcome is not automatically medical negligence.**

Criminal medical negligence has a substantially higher threshold than ordinary civil negligence.

---

# 4.3 Four D's of medical negligence

The PDF's mnemonic is **4 D's**.

## 1. Duty of care

The source states that the duty begins when the doctor accepts the patient.

## 2. Dereliction of duty

A breach/negligent failure in the duty of care.

Source examples include:

- Accepting a patient without adequate competence/resources.
- Knowingly selecting an inappropriate treatment decision.
- Administering a medicine or procedure incorrectly.

## 3. Damage

The source includes:

- Monetary loss
- Pain
- Suffering
- Worsening of disease/condition
- Disability
- Death

## 4. Direct causation

The negligent act should be the **direct/proximate cause** of the claimed damage.

### Exam recall

```text
Duty → Dereliction → Damage → Direct causation
```

> **Important:** In actual litigation, terminology such as duty, breach, causation, damage, foreseeability and standard of care is applied with much more detail than the mnemonic suggests.

---

# 4.4 Civil negligence vs criminal negligence

| Feature | Civil negligence | Criminal negligence |
|---|---|---|
| Source definition | Simple lack of skill/care leading to damage | Gross/wilful/utter carelessness in circumstances amounting to a crime |
| Source examples | Wrong dose, wrong prescription | Wrong-side surgery; criminal abortion (source examples) |
| Forum | Civil/consumer forum depending cause of action | Criminal court |
| Objective | Compensation/remedy | Punishment |
| Parties | Patient/claimant vs doctor/service provider | Prosecution vs accused doctor |
| Standard described | Probability/preponderance | Beyond reasonable doubt |
| Burden | Usually claimant/plaintiff | Prosecution |

### Current criminal negligence law

The PDF cites older IPC sections:

- **IPC 336** - negligent act endangering life/personal safety.
- **IPC 337** - negligent act causing hurt.
- **IPC 338** - negligent act causing grievous hurt.
- **IPC 304A** - causing death by negligence.

These are **historical references** after 1 July 2024.

### Current BNS provisions

- **BNS section 125** - act endangering life or personal safety of others, including graded punishment where hurt/grievous hurt results.
- **BNS section 106** - causing death by negligence.
  - General provision: imprisonment up to 5 years and fine.
  - Where the act is committed by a **registered medical practitioner while performing a medical procedure**, the statute provides imprisonment up to **2 years** and fine.

> **Very high-yield current update:** For present-day criminal medical negligence questions, remember **BNS 106** rather than IPC 304A. For a registered medical practitioner performing a medical procedure, the BNS contains a specific lower maximum imprisonment provision.

---

# 4.5 Consent and criminal negligence

The PDF states that a procedure without proper consent may amount to assault and cites an old IPC section.

> **Correction:** The source's citation to **IPC section 352** as the specific rule for lack of consent is not a reliable current statutory mapping. Section 352 IPC dealt with punishment for assault/criminal force otherwise than on grave provocation; it was not a general "medical procedure without consent" section.

Current consent-related criminal-law exceptions are better mapped to the **BNS consent provisions, especially sections 25-30**, along with other applicable laws and the common/statutory law of consent.

---

# 4.6 Contributory negligence

**Definition in source:** the doctor is negligent, but the patient also contributes to the damage.

### Examples from the PDF

- Incorrect history supplied by the patient.
- Improper follow-up by the patient.

The PDF treats it primarily as a civil-negligence concept and as a **partial defence**.

> **Legal nuance:** Contributory negligence does not automatically erase the doctor's negligence. Depending on the forum and legal doctrine, the patient's contribution may affect causation, apportionment, damages or liability.

---

# 4.7 Comparative negligence

The PDF states:

> The court determines the relative contribution of the parties to the damage and adjusts liability accordingly.

This is the core idea of **comparative negligence/apportionment**.

---

# 4.8 Last clear chance doctrine

The source teaches:

- Both parties are at fault.
- One party had the last clear chance to avoid the damage.
- Liability may attach to the party who failed to use that final opportunity.

> **Current doctrine note:** This is mainly of historical/common-law teaching value and should not be applied as a free-standing modern Indian rule without checking the current cause of action and case law.

---

# 4.9 Avoidable consequence rule

The PDF states:

- Doctor may have been negligent initially.
- Patient has a duty to avoid further damage where reasonably possible.

The concept concerns mitigation of loss rather than converting every later consequence into a new instance of the doctor's original negligence.

---

# 4.10 Res ipsa loquitur

**Latin:** "The thing speaks for itself."

### Source teaching

Applies where an obviously gross event itself strongly indicates negligence, with examples such as:

- Wrong-side surgery.
- Failure to give tetanus prophylaxis in the source's example.

### Important legal refinement

**Res ipsa loquitur is an evidentiary doctrine, not a universal rule that the doctor must prove innocence.** It may permit an inference of negligence where the circumstances strongly speak for themselves and the necessary conditions are satisfied.

The burden of proof does not universally reverse merely because the phrase "res ipsa" is used.

---

# 4.11 Doctrine of common knowledge

The source states that expert evidence may not be necessary where the issue does not involve specialized knowledge and is common knowledge.

### Source example

- IV fluids may be expected in a case of dehydration.

This is the conceptual contrast with cases requiring expert medical evidence because the alleged breach depends on specialized clinical knowledge.

---

# 4.12 Doctrine of calculated risk

The PDF says:

- Every treatment carries some unavoidable risk of damage.
- The doctor should communicate the risk to the patient before the procedure.
- A known inherent risk does not automatically create liability.

### Current refinement

Disclosure of a known risk is important, but **consent does not excuse negligent performance**. A complication can be a recognized risk despite appropriate care, while negligent performance can still result in liability even if the patient signed a consent form.

---

# 4.13 Novus actus interveniens

**Meaning:** a new, unrelated intervening act breaks the causal chain.

### Source flowchart

```text
Initial negligent act by doctor
(e.g. wrong diagnosis)
        ↓
New unrelated intervening act
(e.g. RTA during transport)
        ↓
Damage/death
        ↓
Causal chain may be broken
        ↓
Potential liability may shift depending on facts
```

### Source examples

#### Example 1 - road traffic accident

- Initial negligent diagnosis.
- Patient transported.
- RTA occurs during ambulance transport.
- Patient dies from injuries of the RTA.
- Source attributes primary liability to the person responsible for the RTA if the intervening event is sufficiently independent.

#### Example 2 - transfusion mismatch

- Patient requires transfusion.
- Wrong blood is transfused.
- Patient dies.
- The source keeps the doctor/hospital in the causal chain because the transfusion mismatch may itself be the negligent act.

### Exam pearl

Ask:

> **Did the later event merely contribute to the original chain, or did it constitute a truly new and independent cause?**

---

# 4.14 Vicarious liability

The PDF teaches:

> The superior/employer may be liable for the negligent act of a person working under them.

### Respondeat superior

Core concept:

> **The master/employer may be responsible for negligent acts of the employee committed within the relevant scope of employment.**

The source also states that if two doctors practise together as equals, liability may attach to both according to the facts and their respective acts.

### Civil vs criminal

The PDF teaches vicarious liability mainly as a **civil liability** concept and notes that criminal liability is generally personal and depends on the accused's own acts/omissions and the applicable statute.

---

# 4.15 Borrowed servant doctrine

Where an employee/doctor is temporarily placed under another employer's control for a particular act, the PDF teaches that the **temporary employer** may be liable for negligence occurring in the relevant service relationship.

This depends on actual control/supervision and the legal facts; it is not automatic merely because one institution's staff is physically working in another institution.

---

# 4.16 Captain of the ship doctrine

The PDF describes this as a variant of the borrowed-servant doctrine for the operating room:

> If a team member makes a mistake, the surgeon/captain is responsible.

### Current caution

This is a **historical forensic-medicine teaching doctrine**. Modern hospital liability does not mechanically make a surgeon legally responsible for every act by every team member. Current liability depends on the surgeon's own duty, supervision, delegation, standard of care, employment relationship and the applicable institutional law.

---

# 4.17 Defences in medicolegal proceedings

The PDF lists several concepts.

## 4.17.1 Denial defence

### Denial of duty

Doctor argues that no duty of care existed toward the claimant in the circumstances.

### Denial of dereliction

Doctor argues that:

- Appropriate standards were followed.
- The adverse event was an error of judgment/known complication rather than negligence.

---

## 4.17.2 Double jeopardy / res judicata

The source uses the phrase "double jeopardy / res judicata" together.

> **Important distinction:** These are related but not identical legal concepts. **Double jeopardy** concerns being prosecuted/punished twice for the same offence in the circumstances protected by criminal law/constitutional safeguards. **Res judicata** is a civil-procedure doctrine concerning final adjudication of the same matter. Do not treat the terms as literal synonyms.

---

## 4.17.3 Accidental / unintentional act

The source describes an unexpected diagnostic, therapeutic, or research-related mishap that occurs despite an absence of negligent intent.

The key issue remains whether the standard of care was actually breached; **lack of intent by itself does not determine civil negligence**.

---

## 4.17.4 Maloccurrence / bad outcome despite appropriate care

The PDF's term is **maloccurrence**:

> The doctor has taken the best attention/care, yet the patient has a poor outcome or fails to improve.

The source teaches that this does not by itself make the doctor liable.

### High-yield distinction

```text
Poor outcome ≠ negligence automatically
Negligence = breach + causation + legally recognized damage
```

---

## 4.17.5 Volenti non fit injuria

**Meaning:** to a willing person, no legal wrong is ordinarily committed with respect to an accepted risk.

The source says the patient is aware of the risks.

### Current refinement

A patient's acceptance of risk is **not a blanket waiver of negligence**. Consent to a known inherent risk cannot ordinarily validate negligent treatment itself.

---

## 4.17.6 Limitation period / source's "res indicata"

The PDF appears to use **"res indicata"** for the limitation-period concept and then gives an unreadable/blank value on page 24.

> **Source value:** `[not reliably extractable]`

### Current verification

There is **no single universal limitation period for every medical-negligence claim in India**. It depends on the forum and cause of action.

A major current rule is:

- **Consumer Protection Act, 2019 section 69:** consumer complaints ordinarily must be filed within **2 years** from the date the cause of action arose, subject to the statutory power to entertain a delayed complaint for sufficient cause.
- Limitation under the **Limitation Act, 1963** can produce different periods for other civil causes of action; a 3-year period may be relevant in some tort/compensation contexts, but it should not be presented as the universal rule for all medical negligence litigation.

The source's statement that no such fixed limitation period applies to criminal acts is a useful broad distinction, but criminal prosecution has its own procedural rules and statutory limitation questions depending on the offence.

---

# 4.18 Product liability

The PDF mentions a situation where:

- A medicine is prescribed by the doctor.
- The medicine turns out to be defective because of a manufacturing fault.

The source teaches that liability may fall on the **manufacturer/product chain**, rather than automatically on the prescribing doctor.

### Current refinement

The prescriber can still have liability if the clinician independently acted negligently (wrong drug, wrong dose, failure to recognize contraindication, etc.). A manufacturing defect is a separate causal pathway and should be investigated separately.

# 5. Consent in Medical Practice

# 5.1 Meaning of consent

The PDF states:

> Two people agree on the same thing in the same sense = consent.

It also describes consent as:

- Agreement
- Permission
- Compliance

The source historically links this concept to **section 13 of the Indian Contract Act**.

> **Important:** In medical practice, contractual "consent" is only one part of the legal/ethical picture. Valid treatment consent also depends on capacity, voluntariness, information, and the specific nature of the proposed procedure.

---

# 5.2 Examination or treatment without consent

The PDF states:

> Examination or treatment without consent = assault.

It then incorrectly/too narrowly links the concept to IPC section 352.

### Current clarification

Unconsented touching/procedure can create civil, criminal, professional, or other legal consequences depending on the act and circumstances. There is **no single current BNS section titled "medical procedure without consent."** The criminal-law analysis must consider the specific conduct, whether a statutory exception applies, and the actual harm/intent.

---

# 5.3 Types of consent

## 5.3.1 Implied consent

### Source definition

Consent inferred from:

- Gesture
- Body language
- Patient's conduct

The PDF teaches it mainly in the context of **general physical examination (GPE)**.

### Practical interpretation

Implied consent can be appropriate for ordinary, low-risk clinical examination where the patient's conduct clearly indicates agreement.

It should **not** be stretched to cover invasive or high-risk procedures simply because the patient entered the hospital or examination room.

---

## 5.3.2 Expressed consent

Express consent may be:

- **Oral/verbal**
- **Written**

The source identifies written informed consent as the best form for major procedures and recommends obtaining it under circumstances that permit a third party/witness where appropriate.

### Information to provide before informed consent

The PDF lists:

- Diagnosis
- Prognosis
- Available treatment options
- Benefits and risks of each option
- Proposed treatment
- Benefit of proposed treatment
- Risk of refusing/denying treatment
- Cost of treatment
- Right to refuse consent

### High-yield formula

```text
Information → Understanding → Voluntary choice → Specific agreement
```

> **Clinical-legal point:** A signature alone is not synonymous with informed consent. The information, understanding, voluntariness and scope of the consent matter.

---

## 5.3.3 Blanket / generic / open consent

The PDF describes admission consent such as:

> "Do whatever is needed in the patient's interest"

as **invalid in India** when used as a substitute for specific consent.

### Current practical interpretation

A generic admission form does not replace **procedure-specific informed consent** for an operation, invasive examination, anaesthesia, blood transfusion, research intervention, or other major intervention where specific consent is required.

Consent can also be withdrawn by a competent patient.

---

# 5.4 Substituted consent

The source describes substituted consent where the patient cannot validly consent because of:

- Minority
- Mental incapacity/unsoundness
- Intoxication or inability to understand the decision

and a parent/guardian or other legally authorized person acts for the patient where the law permits.

### Current BNS framework

BNS sections 25-30 contain current consent-related criminal-law exceptions.

In particular:

- **Section 25** - certain acts done to a person above 18 with consent, where death/grievous hurt is not intended/known as specified.
- **Section 26** - acts not intended to cause death, done in good faith for a person's benefit with consent.
- **Section 27** - certain acts done in good faith for a child under 12 or person of unsound mind by/with lawful guardian consent, subject to statutory exclusions.
- **Section 28** - consent given under fear/misconception or by a person unable to understand because of unsoundness of mind/intoxication is not valid consent for the relevant exceptions.
- **Section 29** - a consent exception does not protect an act that is independently an offence.
- **Section 30** - good-faith acts for the benefit of an incapable/unable person without consent in defined circumstances, including certain emergencies.

> **Exam pearl:** Consent is not simply "present/absent". Its legal validity depends on the person's **capacity, voluntariness, information, scope, and statutory context**.

# 5.5 Doctrines of consent

## 5.5.1 Doctrine of full disclosure

Complete relevant information is given to the patient before consent.

Includes, as relevant:

- Diagnosis
- Prognosis
- Alternatives
- Benefits
- Material risks
- Consequences of refusal

---

## 5.5.2 Partial disclosure / therapeutic privilege

The source states:

- Used for the patient's benefit.
- Historically emphasized in psychiatric patients.
- Remaining information may be provided to a guardian.

### Caution

Therapeutic privilege should not become an excuse for routine non-disclosure merely because a doctor thinks the information will upset the patient. The exception should be narrowly justified, consistent with modern autonomy-based practice, capacity assessment, and applicable law.

---

## 5.5.3 Informed refusal

A patient has the right to refuse treatment after receiving appropriate information.

### High-yield distinction

```text
Informed consent = patient accepts after adequate information
Informed refusal = patient declines after adequate information
```

The doctor should document:

- Information given
- Risks explained
- Alternatives explained
- Consequences of refusal
- Patient's decision-making capacity
- The patient's final choice

---

## 5.5.4 Extended consent

The PDF describes:

```text
Patient consents to a specific procedure
        ↓
During procedure, an unforeseen life-saving intervention becomes necessary
        ↓
Additional life-saving intervention may be performed when the legal/clinical emergency criteria are met
```

The source teaches this as an extension of the consent for the specific procedure, especially when the additional intervention is **life-saving**.

### Current refinement

Do not use "extended consent" to authorize arbitrary additional procedures. The ability to proceed without fresh consent depends on the patient's prior authorization, the scope of the original procedure, the urgency, incapacity, and the applicable emergency exception.

---

## 5.5.5 Therapeutic waiver

The source states:

> The patient waives the right to receive the information/consent discussion and transfers that choice to another person.

In modern practice, such a waiver should be **voluntary and documented**, rather than presumed.

---

## 5.5.6 Emergency doctrine

The PDF links emergency treatment to old **IPC section 92** and states:

- Consent may not be available in an emergency.
- Life-saving treatment can proceed without prior consent in the defined emergency situation.

### Current BNS section 30

BNS **section 30** provides a current statutory exception for acts done in good faith for the benefit of a person without consent when the person is incapable/unable to consent and the statutory conditions are met, including situations where there is no guardian from whom consent can be obtained in time.

> **Correction to the source:** "Emergency = no consent needed" is too broad. The exception has **conditions**. A competent adult who understands the situation and gives a valid refusal cannot simply be treated as if unconscious because the doctor believes treatment would be beneficial, subject to specific statutory exceptions in special circumstances.

---

# 5.6 Patient refusal in an emergency

The PDF explicitly states:

> If the patient is unconscious and no relatives are around, emergency treatment may be given; but if a competent patient refuses treatment, the doctor cannot simply proceed because it is an emergency.

This captures the key distinction:

| Patient situation | General principle |
|---|---|
| Unconscious/incapable, emergency, no lawful surrogate available in time | Emergency exception may permit treatment under the statutory conditions |
| Competent adult makes informed refusal | Refusal ordinarily must be respected |
| Surrogate available | Follow applicable surrogate/guardian rules |

---

# 5.7 Doctrine of loco parentis

The PDF's example:

- Child below 18 in a residential school.
- Parents are far away.
- Teacher/person in charge gives consent in the child's immediate interest.

### Current caution

The legal authority of a teacher or institutional caretaker is **not unlimited**. The doctrine is a practical emergency/caregiving concept and must be applied according to the child's age, capacity, the nature/urgency of the intervention and statutory authority.

# 5.8 Rules of consent

# 5.8.1 Medical examination - age of consent

The PDF teaches:

- **General physical examination (GPE):** minimum age for independent consent in the source = **12 years**.
- **Major procedures**, including surgery and per-vaginal/per-rectal examination: **18 years**.

> **Important:** The 12-year figure is a source/PDF examination teaching point and should not be interpreted as a universal modern rule that every 12-year-old can legally consent to every clinical examination. Capacity and the nature of the intervention matter, and specific statutes may set their own ages.

---

# 5.8.2 Autopsy

| Autopsy type | Consent of relatives in source |
|---|---|
| **Medicolegal autopsy** | Not required from relatives |
| **Pathological/clinical autopsy** | Consent from relatives required |

### Core forensic principle

A medicolegal post-mortem is an **investigative/legal process**, not an optional procedure for which the family can veto an authorized autopsy merely by withholding ordinary family consent.

---

# 5.8.3 Medical examination of rape victim and accused

The source uses the old CrPC references:

- **CrPC section 53** -> medical examination of accused in rape case.
- **CrPC section 164A** -> examination of rape victim.

### Current BNSS mapping

#### Rape victim - BNSS section 184

Current BNSS section 184 provides for medical examination of a victim of rape by a registered medical practitioner and requires **the consent of the woman or a competent person who can give consent on her behalf**.

The report includes items such as:

- Age
- DNA material collected
- Injuries, if any
- Mental condition
- Other statutory particulars
- Reasons/conclusions required by the section
- Time of commencement/completion of examination

The report is to be forwarded to the investigating officer within the statutory period.

#### Accused - BNSS section 52

Current BNSS section 52 deals with examination of a person accused of rape by a registered medical practitioner at the request of the police. The provision authorizes reasonable force where necessary and provides for documentation including DNA material and other relevant findings.

> **High-yield contrast:**
>
> - **Victim examination (BNSS 184):** consent is expressly required.
> - **Accused examination (BNSS 52):** the statutory examination is investigative and does not rest on ordinary voluntary consent in the same way; the law provides the authority and procedural safeguards.

---

# 5.8.4 Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)

The PDF states:

- Pregnant woman's consent is mandatory.
- Husband's consent is not required.

### Current MTP position

The **Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971**, as amended, retains the pregnant woman's consent as the central consent requirement, with a special guardian rule where the pregnant person is a **minor or a mentally ill person** as defined by the Act.

The Act's amended framework also permits termination within the statutory gestational limits and categories set by section 3 and related rules/regulations.

> **High-yield:** **Husband's consent is not a substitute for the pregnant woman's own consent.**

---

# 5.8.5 Consent for acts not intended to cause death/grievous hurt

The PDF cites **IPC section 87**:

> A person over 18 may consent to an act not intended/known to cause death or grievous hurt.

### Current equivalent

**BNS section 25** preserves the basic consent exception for a person above 18 years where the statutory conditions are met.

---

# 5.8.6 Consent for a child under 12 / person of unsound mind

The PDF cites **IPC section 89**:

- A guardian may consent for a child under 12 / person of unsound mind for acts done in good faith for the person's benefit, subject to the statutory exclusions.

### Current equivalent

**BNS section 27** contains the current provision, with detailed exclusions including intentional killing and specified acts likely to cause death or grievous hurt except within the statutory therapeutic/preventive purposes.

---

# 5.8.7 Invalid consent

The PDF cites **IPC section 90** and lists:

- Child below 12 years.
- Insane person.
- Consent under fear/threat.
- Consent under intoxication.

### Current BNS section 28

Current BNS section 28 states that consent for the relevant statutory exceptions is not valid when:

- Given under fear of injury or misconception of fact, where the person doing the act knows/reasonably believes this caused the consent; or
- Given by a person who, due to unsoundness of mind or intoxication, cannot understand the nature and consequence of what they are consenting to.

> **Correction:** Do not memorize the PDF as a universal rule that "all consent by every child under 12 is invalid for every medical context." The exact legal question depends on the statute and the nature of the act. The BNS section is an offence-exception provision, not a complete medical-consent statute.

# 5.9 Examination/treatment of undertrial prisoners and convicted prisoners

The PDF teaches:

### Undertrial prisoner

### Convicted prisoner

# 6. Maintenance and Production of Medical Records

# 6.1 NMC medical-record requirement

The source states:

- Medical records of inpatients must be preserved.
- On request by the patient/authorized attendant, a copy should be given promptly.

The source says **3 days**.

### Current NMC 2002 Code

The NMC-hosted **Code of Medical Ethics Regulations, 2002** provides that:

- Indoor patient records should be maintained for **3 years** from commencement of treatment.
- When requested by the patient/authorized attendant or relevant legal authority, the records should be issued within **72 hours**.

Thus, the source's "3 days" is consistent with the current NMC-hosted 2002 Code's **72-hour** requirement.

> **High-yield number:** **3 years** retention + **72 hours** to provide requested records under the NMC 2002 Code.

---

# 6.2 What should be maintained?

At minimum, clinical records should allow reconstruction of:

- Patient identity.
- History and examination.
- Diagnosis/differential diagnosis.
- Investigations.
- Treatment and prescriptions.
- Consent discussions where relevant.
- Operative/procedure records.
- Medication administration.
- Progress notes.
- Discharge summary.
- Referrals/consultations.
- Medicolegal reports/certificates where applicable.

The PDF specifically mentions certificates and medicolegal reports elsewhere in the chapter.

---

# 6.3 Records as evidence

Medical records can become:

- Documentary evidence.
- A source for expert opinion.
- A basis for cross-examination.
- A chronology of patient care.
- A source for determining whether consent and treatment decisions were documented.

> **Exam pearl:** Poor documentation does not automatically prove negligence, but it can make a clinician's position harder to establish when a dispute depends on what was or was not done.

---

# 6.4 Source statement about "D&HS" retention

The PDF states that:

- Outpatient and inpatient records should be preserved for 3 years.
- Afterwards, digitize/store the records indefinitely.
- Medicolegal cases should be kept until trial completion or for 10 years.

However, the exact identity, legal authority, and jurisdictional scope of the source's **"D&HS"** retention rule cannot be reliably established from the supplied pages alone.

> **Source status:** `[not reliably extractable / authority unclear]`

### Safe study rule

For current practice, follow the retention schedule of the **applicable health department, hospital policy, medicolegal record policy, NMC/State Medical Council rules, court directions, and the nature of the claim** rather than treating the PDF's "forever/10 years" statement as a universal national rule.

# 6.5 Patient's avenues after an alleged adverse event

The PDF states that a patient may potentially pursue:

1. **Civil/consumer route** -> compensation.
2. **Criminal route** -> prosecution where the facts satisfy a criminal offence, especially gross criminal negligence where applicable.
3. **Medical regulatory route** -> State Medical Council/NMC disciplinary process.

These pathways can be legally distinct and may have different standards, limitation periods and evidentiary requirements.
