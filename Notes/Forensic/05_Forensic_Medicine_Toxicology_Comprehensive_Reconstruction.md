# 1. General Toxicology

> **Source PDF:** pp. 1–7.

## 1.1 Toxicology

**Toxicology** is the study of poisons/toxic agents, their effects, mechanisms, detection, and management.

The source opens with the teaching point that **acute poisoning is a medical emergency** and that poisoning is an important cause of morbidity and mortality.

### Forensic toxicology

The forensic/medicolegal branch deals with the medicolegal aspects of poisoning, including:

- Medicolegal reporting
- Gastric lavage / stomach-wash material where relevant
- Postmortem findings
- Samples to be preserved
- Dissection in poisoning
- Toxicological analysis

### Occupational toxicology

Study of poisons/toxic exposures in relation to a particular occupation.

### Environmental toxicology

Study of toxic agents in relation to the environment.

## 1.2 Poison — source definition

The PDF defines a **poison** as a substance which, when given in any form or by any route — oral, injectable, inhalational, or contact — causes ill health or death by local or remote action.

### Route and rapidity of onset

The source gives the broad order:

`Inhalation > IV > IM`

This is a simplified teaching comparison; real onset depends on the agent, dose, formulation, tissue distribution, and exposure circumstances.

## 1.3 Poison versus drug

> **“Sola dosis facit venenum” — the dose makes the poison.**

The source attributes this statement to **Paracelsus**.

| Concept in source | Teaching point |
|-|-|
| Drug | Lower/therapeutic dose with a medical/healing intention |
| Poison | Higher/toxic dose and/or use with an intention to cause harm |

The deeper toxicological principle is that **dose and exposure determine toxicity**; a drug can become a poison at a sufficiently high dose, and a toxic substance can sometimes have therapeutic applications at controlled doses.

### Historical personalities

- **Paracelsus** — presented in the source as the “Father of Toxicology.”
- **Mathieu Orfila (Mateo Orfila)** — presented in the source as the “Father of Modern Toxicology.”

 

## 1.4 Toxin, venom, and toxicant

The source distinguishes **toxin** from poison:

- A **toxin** is a toxic biological substance produced by a living organism such as a plant or animal.
- **Toxinology** — study of toxins.
- **Venom** — biologically produced toxic secretion delivered actively, usually by biting/stinging mechanisms.
- **Venomology** — study of venoms (source terminology).

### Practical distinction

- **Poisonous** substances generally cause toxicity when swallowed, inhaled, absorbed, etc.
- **Venomous** animals actively deliver venom by a bite, sting, or similar apparatus.

### Irritants

The source lists:

- **Non-metallic:** phosphorus, iodine, chlorine, bromine etc.
- **Metals/metalloids:** lead, iron, copper, arsenic, thallium etc.
- **Plants:** *Ricinus*, *Abrus*, *Calotropis*, *Croton*, *Capsicum*, *Semecarpus* etc.
- **Animals:** snakes, scorpions, spiders etc.

### Neurotoxic poisons

#### Poisons acting on the brain (cerebral poisons)

- **Somniferous:** opioids/morphine
- **Deliriants:** Datura, cannabis, cocaine (source classification)
- **Inebriants:** alcohol, chloral hydrate etc.
- **Stimulants:** caffeine, cocaine, amphetamines
- **Psychoactive:** cannabis, phencyclidine etc.

#### Poisons acting on spinal cord

- **Excitatory:** strychnine / *Strychnos nux-vomica*
- **Inhibitory:** gelsemium

#### Poisons acting on PNS

- *Conium maculatum* (hemlock)
- Curare

### Cardiotoxic poisons — “CarDONA”

- **Car** — Cardiac poisons
- **D** — Digitalis
- **O** — Oleander
- **N** — Nicotine
- **A** — Aconite

### Asphyxiants

The source gives:

- **Simple/inert:** carbon monoxide and nitrogen are listed together in the notes, although CO is more correctly classified as a chemical asphyxiant.
- **Chemical:** carbon monoxide, cyanide, hydrogen sulphide.
- **Irritant gases:** chlorine, methyl isocyanate, ammonia.

> **Current terminology note:** Carbon monoxide and cyanide are **chemical asphyxiants** because they interfere with oxygen transport/utilization. “Simple asphyxiant” is more appropriate for gases that displace oxygen without directly disrupting cellular oxygen use.

 

## 1.6 Diagnosis of poisoning in the living

### A. History

The source highlights:

- Sudden onset
- Uniformly increasing symptoms
- Symptoms occurring after food intake
- Several members of the same family developing similar illness

These clues increase suspicion but are not diagnostic by themselves.

### B. Clinical examination — toxidromes

A **toxidrome** is a characteristic cluster of signs and symptoms suggesting a class of toxic exposure.

The source highlights:

1. **Cholinergic:** organophosphorus/carbamate poisoning
2. **Anticholinergic:** Datura
3. **Sympathomimetic:** cocaine, amphetamine
4. **Opioid:** morphine/opioids
5. **Sedative-hypnotic:** source lists as a separate toxidrome

### Core toxidrome comparison

| Feature | Cholinergic | Anticholinergic | Opioid | Sympathomimetic |
|-|-|-|-|-|
| Heart rate | ↓ / bradycardia may occur | ↑ | Often ↓ | ↑ |
| Sweating | ↑ | ↓ / dry | Usually not prominent | Often ↑ |
| Temperature | May be ↓/variable | ↑ | ↓ may occur | ↑ |
| Pupils | **Pinpoint/miosis** | **Dilated/mydriasis** | **Pinpoint/miosis** | **Dilated/mydriasis** |
| CNS | Confusion, seizures, coma | Delirium | CNS depression/coma | Agitation, hyperalertness, seizures |
| Secretions | **Markedly ↑** | **Dry** | Usually not increased | Variable |
| Bowel activity | ↑ | ↓ | ↓ | Variable |
| Other clues | Bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, lacrimation | Urinary retention | Respiratory depression | Tremor, severe agitation, hyperthermia |

### Laboratory clues from the source

- **Blood:** routinely useful for many toxicological analyses.
- **Urine:** useful for screening and for many metabolites.
- **Urinary calcium oxalate crystals:** support exposure to **oxalic acid or ethylene glycol**, but they are not alone diagnostic.
- Some poisons/toxic drugs can cause renal tubular injury.

 

## 1.7 “Ideal homicidal” and “ideal suicidal” poison — source table

The source compares theoretical characteristics.

| Feature | “Ideal” homicidal poison — source concept | “Ideal” suicidal poison — source concept |
|-|-|-|
| Colour | Colourless | Less important |
| Odour | Odourless | Less important |
| Taste | Tasteless | Pleasant taste preferred in the source concept |

| Clinical picture | May mimic natural disease | Rapid / predictable |

### Examples in the source

- **Thallium** and **arsenic** are described as “nearly ideal homicidal poisons.”
- The notes emphasize that a truly **ideal homicidal poison does not exist**.

These are historical forensic-teaching concepts, not recommendations.

 

## 1.8 Postmortem examination in suspected poisoning

The source stresses:

- **Smell**
- **Colour of hypostasis**
- **Colour/appearance of stomach mucosa**
- **Chemical analysis**

### Important autopsy principle

> **Never attribute poisoning solely to a single nonspecific autopsy finding.** Toxicological results must be interpreted with the history, scene findings, clinical course, autopsy, histology where appropriate, and laboratory context.

This is an important correction to the source wording that suggests identification of poison in tissues is by itself “absolute proof” of poisoning.

### Smells listed in the source

| Poison / substance | Characteristic odour taught in the source |
|-|-|
| Cyanide | Bitter almonds / “oil of bitter almonds” |
| Hydrogen sulphide | Rotten eggs |
| Phosphorus / some arsenic compounds | Garlic |
| Organophosphorus compounds | Kerosene/garlic-like odour (source wording) |
| Chloral hydrate / paraldehyde | Fruity or solvent-like odour (source table; exact mapping partly degraded) |
| Salicylates | Wintergreen-like odour |
| Cannabis | Burnt/vegetative odour (source table) |

> **Important limitation:** Odour is neither sufficiently sensitive nor specific for diagnosis. In cyanide poisoning, the “bitter almond” odour is especially unreliable because not everyone can detect it and the odour may be absent.

### Hypostasis / skin colour in the source

| Poison / exposure | Source teaching |
|-|-|
| Carbon monoxide | **Cherry red** |
| Cyanide | **Bright/brick red** |
| Phosphorus | Dark brown |
| Bromides / aniline / nitrites | Chocolate-brown / abnormal dark coloration |
| Hydrogen sulphide | Bluish/greenish abnormal coloration in the source table |

These are **supportive forensic clues**, not pathognomonic signs.
### Stomach Mucosa — Postmortem Findings
> **Always preserve the gastric content**

| Poison                                  | Change in the stomach mucosa   |
|               |            |
| Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)                  | Black necrotic                 |
| Nitric acid (HNO₃)                      | Yellow or brown                |
| Carbolic acid (phenol)                  | Leathery, white                |
| Arsenic                                 | Red velvety or white particles |
| Mercury                                 | Slate grey                     |
| Copper sulphate (CuSO₄) & sodium amytal | Bluish                         |
| Phosphorus                              | Yellow / dark brown            |

The PDF repeatedly emphasizes that the **stomach and gastric contents should be preserved** in suspected poisoning when relevant. Sulphuric acid is associated in the notes with **black necrotic gastric mucosa**.

# 2. Duties of a Doctor in a Case of Poisoning

> **Source PDF:** pp. 8–12.

## 2.1 Broad duties


>CASE OF SUSPECTED POISONING
        ↓
>Medical duty — priority
        ↓
>Legal / medicolegal duties


### Medical duty

1. Stabilize the patient
2. Decontaminate when indicated
3. Remove absorbed poison when indicated
4. Give a specific antidote when available
5. Give supportive/symptomatic treatment

### Legal / medicolegal duties in the source

1. Preserve evidence
2. Maintain proper documentation
3. Inform police in reportable/suspicious circumstances
4. Arrange a dying declaration where legally appropriate and the patient is conscious, competent, willing, and near death

### Evidence to preserve
- Food samples
- Stained clothing
- Vomitus
- Gastric lavage material
- Other relevant material from the scene/clinical setting

The principle is **preservation of evidence and chain of custody**, not simply collection.

 

## 2.2 Police intimation and current legal position

The source cites the older **CrPC** and **IPC** framework, including CrPC §39 and IPC §§170/177.

> **Current verification:** Since **1 July 2024**, the criminal-procedure framework has been replaced by the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** and the substantive penal framework by the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)**. Old IPC/CrPC section numbers in the notes should therefore be treated as historical references unless specifically mapped to the current law.

### Suicide and mental healthcare

The source notes that suicide is not to be treated simply as a punishable crime and cites the **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017**.

**Current position:** Section 115 of the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 provides a presumption of severe stress in a person who attempts suicide and directs the appropriate government to provide care, treatment and rehabilitation. The old IPC-based framing in the notes should not be used as the current legal description.

 

## 2.3 Dying declaration

The source describes a patient who is:

- Conscious
- On the verge of death
- Willing to make a statement

The clinical team should prioritize treatment, while following applicable medicolegal procedure and local institutional policy for documentation and recording.

 

## 2.4 Medical management algorithm

```text
Suspected poisoning
        ↓
ABC stabilization
        ↓
Identify likely agent / toxidrome / route / time
        ↓
Decontamination only if indicated and safe
        ↓
Specific antidote when indicated
        ↓
Enhance elimination when evidence-based
        ↓
Support ventilation, circulation, seizures, temperature,
acid–base and electrolyte abnormalities
        ↓
Collect appropriate toxicology specimens
        ↓
Document + preserve chain of custody
        ↓
Disposition + observation for delayed toxicity
```


 

## 2.5 Decontamination by route

| Exposure route | Source teaching |
|-|-|
| Skin | Wash with water |
| Inhalation | Oxygen / remove from exposure |
| Oral | Gastric lavage historically emphasized in the notes |

### Gastric lavage — source versus current practice

The PDF gives details of **Ewald’s tube**, including a long rubber tube, insertion marking, fluids such as water/normal saline/KMnO₄, left lateral positioning, and a preferred time of under 1 hour. It lists contraindications such as corrosives, coma, convulsant poisons, oesophageal varices, volatile hydrocarbons, advanced pregnancy and hypothermia.

> **Current verification:** Gastric lavage **should not be performed routinely**. AACT/EAPCCT position papers state that it should only be considered in exceptional circumstances involving a potentially life-threatening ingestion when it can be performed within about 60 minutes, with a protected airway when airway reflexes are impaired. Corrosives and high-aspiration-risk hydrocarbons are contraindications. Modern poisoning care emphasizes supportive treatment and selective use of safer decontamination strategies rather than routine stomach washing.

> **Source correction:** The PDF's statement that phenol is an exception allowing lavage is not a general contemporary recommendation; phenol poisoning should be managed using current toxicology guidance rather than routine gastric lavage.

See also: [[Corrosive Poisons]]

 

## 2.6 Activated charcoal

### Mechanism

Activated charcoal is a highly adsorptive carbon material. It **binds some toxins on its surface**, reducing gastrointestinal absorption.

### Source teaching

Given through a gastric/Ryle tube when appropriate.

### “CHEMICAL” exclusions in the source

The source gives a mnemonic for agents in which activated charcoal is not useful or should be avoided:

- **C** — Cyanide
- **H** — Hydrocarbons
- **E** — Ethanol
- **M** — Heavy metals
- **I** — Iron
- **C** — Corrosives
- **A** — Compromised/unprotected airway
- **L** — Lithium

The real principle is **chemical adsorption**, so effectiveness varies by toxin.

### Practical limitations

Activated charcoal is generally ineffective or of limited value for substances such as:

- Alcohols
- Many metals and inorganic ions
- Lithium
- Strong corrosives
- Hydrocarbons where aspiration risk dominates

 

## 2.7 Removal of absorbed poison

The source lists:

- Hemodialysis
- Peritoneal dialysis
- Urinary alkalinization
- Urinary acidification (historically, not routinely used)

### Hemodialysis — source mnemonics

**AEIOU** general indications:

- **A** — Acidosis
- **E** — Electrolyte abnormality (e.g. refractory hyperkalaemia)
- **I** — Intoxication with a dialyzable toxin
- **O** — Overload
- **U** — Uraemia

**BLAST** toxicological examples in the notes:

- **B** — Barbiturates
- **L** — Lithium
- **A** — Alcohols
- **S** — Salicylates
- **T** — Theophylline

### Current verification

Modern extracorporeal treatment recommendations are **poison-specific**, not merely mnemonic-based. EXTRIP recommendations support selective dialysis for severe poisonings with agents such as lithium, salicylates, methanol and ethylene glycol according to concentration, acid–base disturbance, clinical severity and antidote availability.

### Urinary alkalinization

The source emphasizes acidic drugs such as:

- Salicylates
- Phenobarbitone

Urinary alkalinization is still a useful principle in selected salicylate poisoning, but it is not a universal detoxification technique.

### Urinary acidification

The source describes this as historically preferred for some alkaloid drugs but explicitly notes that it is **not routinely used**.

 

## 2.8 Antidotes — core table

| Poison | Antidote / specific therapy in source |
|-|-|
| Arsenic | BAL / DMSA (succimer) |
| Copper | D-penicillamine / DMSA |
| Iron | Desferrioxamine |
| Lead | Calcium disodium EDTA; severe CNS cases may require combined strategy |
| Mercury | BAL / DMSA |
| Cocaine / severe sympathomimetic toxicity | Source table lists a beta-blocker; **current practice is supportive treatment and benzodiazepines rather than a blanket “antidote” approach** |
| Carbon monoxide | 100% oxygen; selected cases hyperbaric oxygen |
| Cyanide | Hydroxocobalamin; sodium nitrite + sodium thiosulfate in selected circumstances |
| Digitalis | Digoxin-specific Fab fragments |
| Morphine/opioids | Naloxone |
| Methanol | Fomepizole (or ethanol where appropriate) + folate; dialysis when indicated |
| Organophosphorus poisoning | Atropine + oxime therapy in appropriate cases |
| Carbamates | Atropine; oxime use is toxin-specific and less straightforward |
| Organochlorines | No specific antidote — supportive treatment |
| Pyrethroids | No specific antidote — supportive treatment |

### Chelation — principle

Chelators bind metals to form a complex that is easier to eliminate.

```text
Metal / metalloid
      ↓
Chelating agent
      ↓
Chelated complex
      ↓
More soluble / less biologically active
      ↓
Excretion
```

# 3. Corrosive Poisons

> **Source PDF:** pp. 13–18.

## 3.1 Definition
**Corrosives / caustics** cause major tissue destruction at the site of contact.
## 3.2 Acids versus alkalis

| Feature | Acids | Alkalis |
|-|-|-|
| Primary necrosis | **Coagulative necrosis** | **Liquefactive necrosis** |
| Protein effect | Protein precipitation | Protein dissolution / saponification |
| Eschar | More characteristic | Poorly formed / absent |
| Lateral spread | Relatively limited | More extensive |
| Depth | Often more superficial initially | Often deeper tissue penetration |
| Source teaching on mucosa | Gastric involvement emphasized | Oesophageal involvement emphasized |
| Examples | H₂SO₄, HNO₃ | NaOH, KOH, carbonates, ammonia |

> **Qualification:** The acid-versus-alkali distinction is a useful examination framework, but actual injury patterns depend on concentration, contact time, physical form, volume and tissue exposure.

### Inorganic acids in the source

- Sulphuric acid
- Nitric acid
- Hydrochloric acid

### Organic acids in the source

- Phenol / carbolic acid
- Oxalic acid
- Formic acid
- Acetic acid

### Alkalis in the source

- Sodium hydroxide
- Potassium hydroxide
- Sodium carbonate
- Potassium carbonate
- Ammonia

### Important exception

**Hydrofluoric acid (HF)** behaves differently and can cause **liquefactive tissue injury plus severe systemic fluoride toxicity**.

 

## 3.3 General management of corrosive ingestion

### Source PDF teaching

- Do not induce emesis.
- Do not perform routine gastric lavage because of perforation risk.
- Do not use charcoal because it does not bind the corrosive meaningfully.
- Do not attempt chemical neutralization because an exothermic reaction can worsen injury.
- Keep patient NPO.
- Give IV fluids as required.
- Provide adequate analgesia.
- Manage perforation surgically when necessary.
- Treat infection when clinically indicated.
- Endoscopy is used to assess mucosal injury in appropriately selected patients.

### Current verification

The core “**no emesis / no routine lavage / no neutralization**” principle remains sound. Endoscopy is **case-dependent**, usually performed early in significant corrosive ingestion after stabilization and airway assessment. Routine prophylactic antibiotics and blanket interventions are not appropriate; management depends on injury severity and complications.

 

## 3.4 Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)

### Properties

- Strong inorganic acid.
- **Carbonization** of tissue is emphasized in the source.
- Strongly **hygroscopic** — absorbs water from exposed tissues.

### Clinical features

- Severe pain
- **Odynophagia** — highlighted as a common symptom
- Vomiting, which may be coffee-ground if upper GI bleeding occurs
- Intense thirst
- Abdominal pain, tenderness and distension
- Respiratory distress/stridor/dyspnoea with significant airway exposure

### Autopsy / tissue findings in source

- Black necrotic tongue
- Black/dark necrotic gastric mucosa, described as resembling wet blotting paper
- Gastric perforation may occur
- Chemical peritonitis after perforation

See also: [[Postmortem Examination in Suspected Poisoning]]

 

## 3.5 Nitric acid (HNO₃)

### Properties

**Xanthoproteic reaction:** nitric acid can produce **yellow staining of protein-containing tissues**.

### Clinical features

- Severe pain and odynophagia
- Vomiting
- GI injury
- Respiratory symptoms if fumes are inhaled

### Autopsy findings in source

- Yellow teeth
- Yellow skin staining
- Yellow gastric mucosa
- Brown gastric mucosa may be seen where blood has undergone chemical/digestive alteration

### Examination pearl

**Nitric acid → yellow discoloration via xanthoproteic reaction.**

 

## 3.6 Vitriolage (acid attack)

### Definition in the source

Intentional throwing or application of a corrosive substance with the intention of disfiguring a person.

### Common acids listed

- Sulphuric acid
- Nitric acid

The source also mentions *Semecarpus anacardium* preparations; this is a historical forensic teaching point rather than a contemporary common cause of acid assault.

### Examination findings

- Chemical burns of face and eyes
- Corrosive ulceration
- Singeing of hair
- Usually no classic blistering, except the source notes formic acid as an exception
- Contractures/scarring may develop

### Immediate management

- Immediate copious irrigation with water or normal saline
- Protect airway/eyes as required
- Ophthalmic assessment when the eye is involved
- Subsequent burn/wound care according to severity

> **Source correction:** The notes mention magnesium oxide paste, olive oil, atropine and hydrocortisone in some situations. These are historical notes; current burn/ophthalmic protocols should be followed instead of applying such materials routinely.

### Current legal position in India

The source cites **IPC §§326A/326B** and old **CrPC §357C**.

Current law uses:

- **BNS §124** — voluntarily causing grievous hurt by use of acid; subsection (2) addresses attempts.
- **BNSS §397** — hospitals, public or private, must immediately provide first-aid/medical treatment free of cost to specified victims, including victims covered by BNS §124(1), and inform police.

 

## 3.7 Phenol (carbolic acid)

### Identity

- Phenol = **carbolic acid**.
- Historically used as an antiseptic/disinfectant.
- Related phenolic compounds listed in the notes: cresol, resorcinol, Lysol, Dettol.

### Absorption

Phenol can be absorbed by multiple routes, including **intact skin**.

### Acute poisoning

- Initial burning pain
- Tingling / numbness
- Local anaesthesia may follow
- Vomiting can occur
- CNS depression
- Convulsions
- Pupil constriction in the source

### Metabolism / toxic products in source

Phenol is described as being metabolized into:

- Pyrocatechol (catechol)
- Hydroquinone

### Renal / urinary clue

**Carboluria:** urine may become dark/greenish on exposure to air; renal tubular injury may occur.

### Other source points

- Acute poisoning = **carbolism**.
- Chronic poisoning = **phenolic marasmus** (historical terminology in the source).
- Phenol can delay putrefaction because of its antiseptic effect.
- Clinical clue in the notes: phenolic/hospital-like odour + dark urine.

> **Current verification:** Phenol poisoning is a systemic toxic exposure in addition to a local corrosive exposure. Airway, cardiovascular support and careful skin decontamination are important. Routine gastric lavage is **not** a contemporary default intervention.

 

## 3.8 Hydrofluoric acid (HF)

### Why HF is different

HF is an acid but produces **liquefactive necrosis** and can cause severe systemic fluoride toxicity.

### Mechanism

```text
HF exposure
   ↓
H⁺ + F⁻
generated / absorbed
   ↓
F⁻ binds Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺
   ↓
Hypocalcaemia + hypomagnesaemia
   ↓
Cardiac electrical instability / arrhythmia
   ↓
Possible hyperkalaemia + systemic toxicity
```

### Clinical features

- Excruciating burning pain, sometimes disproportionately severe to the visible lesion
- Deep tissue injury
- Skin, subcutaneous tissue and bone involvement in severe exposure
- Oliguria / renal injury can occur
- Cardiac arrhythmias may be life-threatening

### Treatment — source framework

1. Immediate removal from exposure
2. Copious water decontamination
3. **Calcium gluconate / calcium salts** locally or systemically according to the involved site and systemic findings
4. Correct electrolyte abnormalities
5. Continuous ECG monitoring for significant exposure
6. ICU-level management for serious systemic toxicity

> **Current verification:** In severe systemic toxicity, IV calcium replacement and specialist toxicology care are central. Intra-arterial calcium is **not** a routine general-purpose treatment and should not be copied from old lecture-note wording.

### Occupational clue

HF is classically associated with **glass etching** and other industrial exposures.

 

## 3.9 Oxalic acid

### Identity / source teaching

- Organic acid
- Called “acid of sugar” in the notes
- Can resemble magnesium sulphate
- Used historically in rust/ink-removing solutions
- Plant sources include some vegetables

### Toxic mechanism

```text
Oxalic acid
   ↓
Ca²⁺ binding
   ↓
Calcium oxalate
   ↓
Hypocalcaemia + precipitation
   ↓
Tetany / neuromuscular irritability
   ↓
Tubular obstruction + renal injury
```

### Clinical features

- Local corrosive GI injury
- Systemic absorption
- Hypocalcaemia
- Tetany
- Oxaluria
- Calcium oxalate crystals in urine
- Acute kidney injury / renal failure in severe exposure

### Urinary microscopy

The source describes **needle/envelope-shaped calcium oxalate crystals**.

### Treatment — source

- Calcium gluconate for symptomatic hypocalcaemia
- Hemodialysis when clinically indicated
- The source mentions calcium-lactate gastric lavage

> **Current verification:** Do **not** perform routine gastric lavage for oxalic-acid poisoning. Treatment focuses on stabilization, calcium replacement, supportive care and renal-replacement therapy when indicated.

 

## 3.10 Boric acid

The source gives the classic forensic examination phrase:

> **Boric acid poisoning → “boiled lobster” appearance**

### Skin appearance

- Beef-red / erythematous skin
- Swelling
- Peeling/desquamation

### Causes

### Clinical course

A useful source-based framework is:

```text

   ↓
Stage II

   ↓

   ↓
```

- Kidney failure
- Coagulopathy
- Multiorgan failure

# 5. Animal and Plant Irritants

> **Source PDF:** pp. 31–39.

## 5.1 Snake bites

The source begins with epidemiological material and common myths, but its numerical mortality estimates are older and should not be treated as current national epidemiology.

### Important terminology

- Snakes that actively inject venom are **venomous**.
- “Poisonous” is not the preferred term for an animal that injects venom by bite.

### Common source myths corrected

- Snakes do not normally drink milk as a special biological requirement.
- “Flying snakes” do not pierce human eyes as a routine mechanism.
- Pythons and boa constrictors kill by **constriction causing circulatory/respiratory compromise**, not by routinely breaking every bone.
- Snakes lack external ears; sound reception involves internal structures and vibration conduction.

## 5.13 Tests for snake envenomation

### 20-minute whole blood clotting test (20WBCT)

Source procedure:

1. Place about 5 mL freshly collected venous blood in a **new, clean, dry glass tube**.
2. Leave undisturbed for approximately 20 minutes.
3. Observe for clot formation.

- **No clot** → supports venom-induced coagulopathy, especially viper envenomation.
- **Clot present** → does not exclude all envenomation.

> **Current practice note:** Laboratory PT/INR, fibrinogen and other coagulation parameters are more precise where available. The 20WBCT remains a useful bedside field test in appropriate settings.

### Neuroparalysis bedside clues

- Single-breath count / respiratory reserve
- Forced vital capacity or equivalent respiratory monitoring where available
- Serial cranial-nerve examination
- Look for ptosis and bulbar weakness

 

## 5.14 Snake-bite first aid — source mnemonic

**CARRY NO RIGHT**

- **NO** — no tourniquet; no cutting; no suction; no electrotherapy
- **R** — reassure
- **I** — immobilize as for a fracture
- **Sutherland technique** — pressure immobilization in selected neurotoxic snake bites
- **G/H** — get to hospital immediately
- **T** — tell the doctor what symptoms occurred en route

> **Current qualification:** Pressure-immobilization methods are region- and species-dependent. They are particularly discussed for selected neurotoxic snake bites and should **not** be applied as a tight tourniquet. The source correctly warns against its use for viper bites because of concern regarding local tissue injury.

 

## 5.15 Antisnake venom (ASV)

### Source principles

- Give ASV **when there are signs of clinically significant envenomation**.
- It neutralizes **unbound venom**.
- It can cause anaphylaxis and other reactions.
- Routine test doses are not recommended.
- Use early once indicated.
- The source gives a teaching dose of **10 vials IV** for the initial dose in the typical adult teaching context.
- The source states that this polyvalent ASV covers the Indian “Big Four”.

### Reassessment

The source suggests reassessment after approximately:

- 1 hour
- 6 hours for selected coagulation reassessment

### Additional treatments in source

- Neostigmine + atropine for selected **cobra-induced neuroparalysis**
- Dialysis when renal failure develops
- FFP for clinically significant coagulopathy after appropriate toxin control
- Wound care/debridement for established necrosis

> **Current practice note:** ASV dosing and repeat-dose decisions should follow local Indian snakebite protocols and clinical response. Dose is based on the **severity and species/venom profile**, not simply body weight.

# 7. Neurotoxic Poisons — Inebriants

## 7.1 Alcohol (ethanol)
### Source definition
An inebriant is any substance that causes intoxication.
### Toxicity order in the source
`Ethanol < Methanol < Isopropyl alcohol`
This statement is a broad relative-toxicity teaching mnemonic and should not be interpreted as a universal ranking for every dose/exposure context.
## 7.2 Manufacture and preparations
Ethanol is produced by

### Congeners
The source notes that **congeners** contribute to flavour and hangover effects.
### Types in source

- Rectified spirit: ~95%
- Denatured spirit: source gives ~90% ethanol + methanol, but modern denatured formulations vary and should not be assumed to have one universal composition.
### Common drinks — source ranges
| Beverage | Source alcohol range |
|   -- |       -: |
| Beer     |               ~6–10% |
| Wine     |               ~5–20% |
| Whisky   |              ~40–45% |
| Rum      |              ~45–50% |

## 7.3 Absorption and distribution
### Absorption
Source teaching:
- Approximately **20% from stomach**
- Remainder principally from **small intestine**
### Factors increasing absorption
- Empty stomach
- Carbonated beverages
- Warm fluids
### Factors decreasing absorption
- Food
- High-protein meal
- Cold/ice-containing drink in the source
### Distribution
Alcohol distributes according to body water.
> **Qualification:** The source’s statement that “males tolerate alcohol better than females” is oversimplified. Differences in body water, body composition, enzymes, dose pattern and individual susceptibility all influence BAC and clinical effects.
### Source fluid ratios

- Blood : vitreous ≈ **1 : 1.2**
- Blood : alveolar air ≈ **1 : 0.0021** as represented in the teaching diagram; the exact numerical conversion is instrument/standard dependent.
## 7.4 Legal blood alcohol concentration in India
The source states the legal limit as:
> **30 mg alcohol / 100 mL blood**
Under **Motor Vehicles Act §185**, driving with alcohol exceeding the statutory threshold is punishable when established through the prescribed method of testing.
### Examination pearl
`30 mg/100 mL = 30 mg/dL` is the India motor-vehicle legal blood alcohol threshold.
## 7.5 Alcohol intoxication stages — source ranges
### Stage 1 — excitement
Source range: **50–150 mg/dL**
- Loss of social inhibition
- Reduced judgment
- False sense of confidence
- Talkativeness
- Apparent excitement
- Dilated pupils in the source
### Stage 2 — incoordination
Source range: **150–250 mg/dL**
- Reduced motor coordination
- Slurred speech
- Reduced visual acuity
- Delayed reaction time
- Increased road-traffic risk
- Dilated pupils in the source
### Stage 3 — coma
Source range: **>250 mg/dL**
- Coma
- Respiratory depression can occur
- Pupils may become constricted in severe poisoning
- **McEwan’s sign** is listed in the source
### Alcoholic gaze nystagmus
Source association: approximately **80–100 mg/dL**.
> **Clinical qualification:** BAC–effect relationships vary markedly between individuals, and alcohol concentration does not alone determine clinical severity. Current emergency care is based on the patient’s clinical state.
## 7.6 McEwan’s sign
Source description:
> Pupils constricted → dilate with a stimulus (like slapping or pinching) → constrict again.
The sign is a historical forensic examination concept and should not be overinterpreted outside the complete clinical context.
## 7.7 Laboratory diagnosis of alcohol
### Specimens
- **Blood** — best routine quantitative sample in living patients
- Urine
- Vitreous humour — particularly useful in decomposed bodies
- Alveolar air / breath alcohol
### Tests in source
- Kozelka–Hine test — qualitative, historical
- Cavett/Cavett-type test — qualitative, historical
- **Gas chromatography** — quantitative and described as the best method in the notes
### Current forensic practice
Validated **headspace gas chromatography**, often with flame-ionization detection or mass spectrometric confirmation, remains a standard analytical approach in forensic laboratories.
## 7.8 Legal effects of intoxication — current framework
The source uses historical IPC §§85, 86 and 510 for involuntary intoxication, voluntary intoxication and public misconduct while intoxicated.
Current BNS provisions should be used instead:
- **BNS §23** — acts done by a person incapable of judgment because of intoxication caused without their knowledge or against their will.
- **BNS §24** — effect of voluntary intoxication on liability/knowledge under specified circumstances.
The exact legal application depends on the offence and facts; do not substitute old IPC numbers in a current medicolegal report.
## 7.9 Conditions associated with alcohol use
### Pathological jealousy

A rare state in which marked behavioural disturbance occurs after a relatively small amount of alcohol.
### Alcoholic blackout

Alcohol-associated atrial and other supraventricular arrhythmias in susceptible individuals.

- Anterograde and/or retrograde amnesia

### Names

```text
   ↓
```

# 9. Asphyxiants and Cardiac Poisons
> **Source PDF:** pp. 56–65.
## 9.1 Asphyxiants — classification
| Type         | Examples in source                                 | Principle                                   |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Simple/inert | Nitrogen and similar oxygen-displacing gases       | Environmental hypoxia                       |
| Chemical     | CO, cyanide, H₂S                                   | Interfere with oxygen transport/utilization |
| Irritant     | Chlorine, methyl isocyanate, ammonia, formaldehyde | Direct airway/lung injury                   |
 
## 9.2 Carbon monoxide (CO)
### Formation
Produced by **incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels**.
```text
Incomplete combustion → CO
Complete combustion (adequate oxygen) → mainly CO₂
```
### Sources in source illustration
- Car exhaust in enclosed garage
- Clogged chimney
- Faulty/corroded/disconnected heater vent
- Wood/gas fireplace
- Vehicle exhaust
- Other combustion appliances
### Properties
- Colourless
- Odourless
- Gas
- Density close to air rather than dramatically lighter/heavier
- “Silent killer” is the source’s historical phrase
### CO half-life — source teaching
Approximately **250–300 min on room air**.
Source estimates:
- ~80 min with 100% oxygen
- ~20 min with hyperbaric oxygen
Actual elimination half-life depends on inspired oxygen concentration and other factors.
 
### 9.3 Mechanism of CO toxicity
CO binds haem proteins.
#### Major mechanism — haemoglobin
CO has much greater affinity for haemoglobin than oxygen and forms **carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb)**.
Consequences:
1. Reduced oxygen carriage
2. Leftward shift of the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve
3. Impaired oxygen unloading
4. Tissue hypoxia
#### Other haem proteins
- **Cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV)** — contributes to cellular toxicity
- **Myoglobin** — contributes to tissue toxicity and delayed/rebound phenomena
#### Vulnerable organs
- Brain
- Heart
- Other high-metabolic-demand tissues
#### Brain pathology
The source associates CO with **bilateral lenticular/basal-ganglia necrosis**.
 
### 9.4 CO clinical features
| COHb level — source teaching | Source symptoms                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 10–20%                       | Mild headache; poisoning may be difficult to recognize                                                                       |
| 20–30%                       | Emotional instability / irritability; severe headache                                                                        |
| 30–40%                       | Confusion; ataxia                                                                                                            |
| 40–50%                       | Increasing confusion, incoordination, tremor; may resemble drunkenness; cherry-red skin; amnesia/confabulation in the source |
| 50–60%                       | Arrhythmia, seizures, myocardial injury                                                                                      |
| >60%                         | Coma and death                                                                                                               |
> **Important:** COHb concentration and symptom severity correlate imperfectly. Timing of blood sampling matters because oxygen treatment and time outside the exposure reduce COHb.
#### Other clinical clues
- Headache
- Dizziness
- Weakness
- Nausea/vomiting
- Confusion
- Chest pain
- Dyspnoea
- Loss of consciousness
- Cardiac ischaemia/arrhythmia
 
### 9.5 CO diagnosis
#### Preferred current test
**Blood carboxyhaemoglobin measurement by co-oximetry**.
#### Pulse oximetry
Standard pulse oximetry is **unreliable** because it cannot reliably distinguish oxyhaemoglobin from carboxyhaemoglobin.
#### Historical tests in source
- Kunkel’s test
- Katayama test
- Hoppe–Seyler test
- Spectroscopy
The source describes spectroscopy as the “best” test; modern practice generally uses **multi-wavelength co-oximetry**.
#### Normal COHb
Source notes:
- Normal population: generally <5%
- Smokers: may be >10%
> **Current CDC reference values:** about **2%** or lower in most non-smokers and around **5–9%** in smokers are more contemporary reference points. Clinical diagnosis still depends on exposure history and symptoms.
 
### 9.6 CO treatment
```text
Remove from exposure
      ↓
100% oxygen
      ↓
ABCs + cardiac/neurological monitoring
      ↓
Consider hyperbaric oxygen when severe criteria are met
```
#### Source teaching
- Remove exposure
- **100% oxygen**
- Hyperbaric oxygen for selected severe cases
- Source threshold: COHb >25%
- Source pregnancy threshold: >15%
- Severe signs: coma, convulsions, neurological deficit
#### Current verification
CDC clinical guidance recommends **100% oxygen until the patient is symptom-free** and consideration of hyperbaric oxygen when there is significant neurologic impairment, cardiac involvement, severe acidosis, loss of consciousness, abnormal neuropsychiatric testing, or high COHb. In pregnancy, the threshold for considering HBO is lower and specialist consultation is important.
 
## 9.7 Postmortem findings in CO poisoning
- **Cherry-red hypostasis / skin**
- Cutaneous blisters, especially over flexures, may occur and can also be seen in barbiturate poisoning
- Bilateral symmetrical basal-ganglia/lenticular necrosis
These are suggestive, not pathognomonic.
 
## 9.8 Cyanide
### Names
- Hydrocyanic acid (HCN)
- Prussic acid
- Scheele’s acid (historical terminology)
### Forms
- HCN gas
- Sodium cyanide
- Potassium cyanide
### Source description
- Extremely rapidly acting
- Historical “ideal suicidal poison” classification
- Bitter almond odour association
### Current odour correction
The “bitter almond” smell is **not a reliable diagnostic feature**:
- Not all cyanide exposures produce a detectable odour.
- Some people cannot genetically perceive it.
- Odour should never be used to exclude cyanide poisoning.
### Sources
The source lists:
- Bitter almond and other fruit kernels/pits
- Cherry/apricot pits
- Industrial chemicals
- Fumigants
- Rodenticides/insecticides
- Metal polishing and metallurgy
- Photography-related chemicals
- Sodium nitroprusside as an iatrogenic source
- Smoke/fire exposure
Historical references to Zyklon B and ancient poisonings are retained only as forensic history.
 
## 9.9 Cyanide clinical features
### Mild exposure
- Headache
- Vertigo
- Restlessness
- Palpitations
- Weakness
### More severe exposure
- Throat tightness/constriction
- Confusion
- Visual disturbance
- Loss of consciousness
- Convulsions
- Loss of corneal reflex
- Respiratory and cardiac arrest
### Very high exposure
- Severe collapse
- Bradycardia/asystole may occur

## 9.10 Cyanide mechanism
```text
Cyanide
   ↓
Binds ferric (Fe³⁺) iron in cytochrome c oxidase
   ↓
ETC / Complex IV inhibition
   ↓
Oxidative phosphorylation stops
   ↓
Cells cannot utilize available oxygen
   ↓
Cytotoxic histotoxic anoxia
```
### Important physiological concept
Arterial oxygen may remain present because the primary problem is **oxygen utilization**, not simple oxygen absence.
The source emphasizes the striking idea that venous oxygen can remain abnormally high because tissues cannot extract it normally.
### Target organs
- Brain
- Heart
 
## 9.11 Cyanide treatment
### Older “cyanide kit” concept in source
- Amyl nitrite
- Sodium nitrite
- Sodium thiosulfate
Nitrites create **methaemoglobin**, which competes for cyanide binding.
### Hydroxocobalamin
```text
Hydroxocobalamin + cyanide
          ↓
Cyanocobalamin
          ↓
Excretion
```
### Current preferred antidote
**Hydroxocobalamin** is generally preferred because it binds cyanide without intentionally inducing clinically significant methaemoglobinaemia.
> **Current verification:** CDC/NIOSH specifically warns that **amyl nitrite and sodium nitrite should not be used in smoke-inhalation victims**, because concurrent carbon monoxide exposure can make methemoglobinaemia dangerous. Hydroxocobalamin is especially useful in this setting.
### General management
1. Remove exposure
2. Remove contaminated clothing as required
3. Oxygen/supportive care
4. Immediate antidote for clinically significant poisoning
5. Control seizures
6. Support circulation and ventilation

### Names in source

## 10.1 Organophosphorus compounds (OPCs)
### Importance
The source emphasizes OPC poisoning as particularly important in rural/agricultural settings and notes suicide as a frequent context.
### Examples
The notes include:
- Malathion
- TEPP
- HETP
- OMPA
- Parathion
- Propoxur/carbaryl are also mentioned in the broader agricultural table, although some are carbamate compounds rather than OPCs.
- Nerve agents: **sarin, tabun, soman**
### Mechanism
```text
Organophosphate
      ↓
Phosphorylation of acetylcholinesterase
      ↓
AChE activity falls
      ↓
Acetylcholine accumulates in synapses
      ↓
Excess muscarinic + nicotinic + CNS effects
```
### Irreversibility / ageing
The source explains:
- Initial enzyme binding can be reversible.
- With time, the phosphorylated enzyme undergoes **ageing**.
- After ageing, oximes cannot effectively reactivate the enzyme.
### Practical implication
Give oxime therapy early when indicated, because the window for reactivation is time-sensitive and agent-specific.
 
## 10.2 Cholinergic syndrome
### Muscarinic effects — DUMBELS
- **D** — Diarrhoea
- **U** — Urination
- **M** — Miosis
- **B** — Bradycardia / bronchospasm / bronchorrhoea
- **E** — Emesis
- **L** — Lacrimation
- **S** — Salivation
Also think of:
- Sweating
- Increased GI motility
- Bronchorrhoea
- Secretions
### Nicotinic effects
- Fasciculations
- Muscle weakness
- Autonomic ganglion effects
- Cranial/bulbar weakness
- Respiratory muscle paralysis
### CNS effects
- Anxiety/confusion
- Seizures
- Coma
 
## 10.3 Intermediate syndrome
Occurs after the acute cholinergic phase, typically within **about 1–4 days** in source teaching.
### Features
- Proximal muscle weakness
- Neck-flexor weakness
- Cranial nerve palsies
- Respiratory muscle weakness
The source associates it with inadequate treatment and/or lipophilic OP compounds.
 
## 10.4 Delayed OP neuropathy
The source places delayed neurological toxicity at approximately **2–4 weeks**.
Features:
- Distal sensorimotor polyneuropathy
- Weakness after the acute cholinergic syndrome has resolved
 
## 10.5 Diagnosis of OPC poisoning
### Cholinesterase measurements
| Test | Source teaching |
|-|-|
| RBC / true acetylcholinesterase | More specific; correlates better with clinical effect |
| Plasma pseudocholinesterase | Easier, more sensitive, commonly done |
Clinical diagnosis should remain primary when a convincing toxidrome is present; a normal enzyme result does not always exclude clinically important exposure depending on timing and assay.
 
## 10.6 Treatment of organophosphorus poisoning
### Algorithm
```text
OPC poisoning suspected
        ↓
Protect staff + remove contaminated clothing
        ↓
ABC stabilization
        ↓
Airway suction + ventilation when necessary
        ↓
Atropine
        ↓
Titrate until bronchial secretions/respiratory findings improve
        ↓
Consider pralidoxime / oxime early when appropriate
        ↓
Control seizures + other complications
        ↓
Observe for intermediate and delayed syndromes
```
### Atropine
Source:
- Give until **adequate atropinization**.
- Mechanism: reverses **muscarinic** effects.
Practical endpoint is not simply “maximum pupil dilation” or heart rate normalization. A clinically useful endpoint is **clearer lungs/airway with improved bronchial secretions and adequate oxygenation/perfusion**.
### Oximes
- Cholinesterase reactivators
- Give with atropine when indicated
- More useful before significant enzyme ageing
The source specifically states that oximes should be given as early as possible.
### Current verification
WHO guidance supports early atropinization and gives a clinical endpoint based on respiratory secretions and haemodynamics. Pralidoxime is commonly used in significant organophosphate poisoning, but the strength of evidence and role varies with the specific pesticide, timing and clinical context.
### Important source correction
The note that atropine should not be given while the patient is cyanosed is misleading. In a severely poisoned patient, **oxygenation and ventilation must be corrected, but atropine should not be withheld when clinically indicated because of cyanosis alone.**
 
## 10.7 Carbamate poisoning
### Mechanism
Carbamates cause **reversible carbamylation/inhibition of acetylcholinesterase**.
### Clinical severity
The source describes symptoms as often milder and more transient than classic OPC poisoning.
### Treatment
- Supportive care
- **Atropine** for clinically significant muscarinic toxicity
- Oxime use is controversial/toxin-specific and should not be automatically copied from the OPC protocol
 
## 10.8 Organochlorine poisoning
The source lists:
- DDT
- Endrin
- Other older organochlorine insecticides
### General features
Depending on the agent, toxicity can include:
- CNS excitation
- Tremors

### Treatment
- No single specific antidote
- Supportive care
- Seizure control
- Decontamination where appropriate
 
## 10.9 Pyrethroid poisoning
The source describes pyrethroids as acting on **voltage-gated sodium channels**, causing abnormal nerve transmission.
### Features

# 12. Source Corrections and Current Legal Crosswalk

## 12.1 Criminal law and procedure — historical versus current

| Source PDF reference | Current framework / comment |
|-|-|
| IPC §328 — poisoning/stupefying agent | **BNS §123** addresses causing hurt by poison, stupefying, intoxicating or unwholesome substances with intent to cause hurt or facilitate an offence |
| IPC §§326A / 326B — acid attack | **BNS §124** addresses grievous hurt by acid and attempts |
| CrPC §357C — treatment of victims | **BNSS §397** requires immediate first aid/medical treatment free of cost in specified offences including BNS §124(1), with immediate police information |
| IPC §84 — unsound mind | **BNS §22** — act of a person of unsound mind |
| IPC §85 — involuntary intoxication | **BNS §23** |
| IPC §86 — voluntary intoxication | **BNS §24** |
| Mental Healthcare Act reference to suicide | **Mental Healthcare Act §115** remains relevant: presumption of severe stress after attempted suicide and a care/treatment/rehabilitation framework |
| Motor Vehicles Act §185 | Current statutory alcohol threshold remains **30 mg/100 mL blood** under the section’s framework |
| NDPS Act, 1985 | Remains the principal central narcotics-control statute; definitions in §2 remain important for cannabis and other substances |

### Current legal transition

The **BNS, BNSS and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam** came into force on **1 July 2024** (subject to the commencement provisions of each Act). Therefore, an examination answer or medicolegal report written in 2026 should avoid presenting old IPC/CrPC section numbers as current law without qualification.

 

## 12.2 Particularly important current legal points for this PDF

### Poisoning / stupefaction

**BNS §123** is the modern central provision corresponding to the old IPC teaching about administering poison, stupefying or intoxicating substances with criminal intent.

### Acid attack

**BNS §124** replaces the old IPC acid-attack provisions cited in the source.

### Free emergency treatment

**BNSS §397** imposes an immediate treatment obligation on specified cases in public/private hospitals and specifically includes BNS §124(1) acid-attack victims among covered offences.

### Suicide attempt

**Mental Healthcare Act §115** remains the key provision reflected in the source’s point that attempted suicide should not simply be framed as a punishable act.
