# 1. Autopsy Procedure

**Source pages:** 1–9

## 1.1 Definition and terminology

**Autopsy** = postmortem examination.

Other terms appearing in the PDF:

- **Necropsy** — `nekros` = dead body; `opsy` = study.
- **Thanatopsy**
- **Sectio cadaveris**

The PDF states that an autopsy in India is performed by an MBBS doctor / registered medical practitioner. The exact legal and institutional requirements of a particular autopsy service may additionally depend on applicable state/institutional rules.

## 1.2 Main types of autopsy
### Summary table

| Type | Typical setting in source | Main objective | Authorization / consent in source | Examination in source | Body handed to |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Medicolegal autopsy** | Unnatural / suspicious death | Cause and manner of death, identification, injuries, weapon/poison, fetal status, evidence collection | Investigating officer / magistrate / police inquest requisition; relatives' consent not required | Complete examination of primary cavities | Investigating officer |
| **Clinical / pathological / academic autopsy** | Natural death | Cause/pathology of death | Consent of relatives | Partial examination of suspected pathology | Relatives |
| **Virtual autopsy / virtopsy** | Imaging-based examination | Assess disease/injury without conventional dissection | Case-dependent | CT/MRI/3-D/optical imaging | N/A |
| **Endoscopic / keyhole autopsy** | Limited invasive examination | Internal organ assessment with minimal external alteration | Case-dependent | Endoscope/laparoscope | N/A |
| **Needle autopsy** | Limited tissue sampling | Histopathological / laboratory assessment | Case-dependent | Multiple biopsy samples | N/A |
| **Psychological autopsy** | Uncertain manner of death | Reconstruct mental state before death; source particularly associates it with suspected suicide | Interview-based | Interviews with relatives/family | N/A |
| **Verbal autopsy** | Epidemiology / statistics | Estimate cause of death from structured interviews | Structured interview by trained persons | No conventional postmortem examination | N/A |

### Medicolegal autopsy — source objectives

1. Identification, especially in **decomposed, mutilated, or burnt bodies**.
2. Establish **cause of death**.
3. Establish **manner of death**:
   - Natural
   - Unnatural → accidental / homicidal / suicidal
4. Document injuries and characterize their type.
5. Help identify the responsible **weapon or poison**.
6. In fetal/neonatal cases, assess whether the fetus/infant was live born or dead born and, where applicable, whether viable.
7. Collect, preserve, and forward evidence to the appropriate agencies for investigation and court proceedings.

### Authorization — source position

The PDF states that the investigating officer / magistrate / police inquest sends a requisition letter and that **relatives' consent is not required** for a medicolegal autopsy.

### 1.3 Clinical / pathological / academic autopsy

The source describes this as:

- Usually performed in a **natural death**.
- Objective: identify the cause/pathology of death.
- Requires **consent of relatives**.
- May be **partial**, examining only the cavity or organ relevant to the suspected pathology.
- Body is returned to the **relatives**.


### Virtual autopsy / virtopsy

The PDF describes:

- Complete body imaging through **CT / MRI / 3-D / optical measurement techniques**.
- Useful for assessing disease, injury, and structural changes.
- Limitation stated: mainly **structural lesions** can be identified.

### Endoscopic / keyhole autopsy

- Internal organs are viewed through an **endoscope / laparoscope**.
- The body remains relatively intact externally.

### Needle autopsy

- Multiple tissue samples are obtained through **biopsy needles** and analyzed.

### Psychological autopsy

The source describes it as useful when the **manner of death is uncertain**, especially in suspected suicide.

Method:

- Reconstruct the deceased's pre-death state of mind.
- Interview relatives/family members and other relevant persons.

### Verbal autopsy

- Structured interview of relatives/family by trained persons.
- Data are used to determine a probable cause of death.
- Source states that it is **not highly accurate** and is particularly useful for **statistical/public-health purposes**.

## 1.5 Sequence of a complete postmortem examination

The PDF gives the following progression:

```text
History
  ↓
External examination
  ↓
Internal examination
  ↓
Additional laboratory tests
  ↓
Form opinion regarding cause of death
```

The sequence is a framework rather than a rigid rule; the order of individual procedures can be modified when evidence preservation requires it.

## 1.6 Obscure autopsy and negative autopsy

### Obscure autopsy

**Definition in source:**

- Minimal or nonspecific findings on external and internal examination.
- Additional laboratory tests, history-taking, and interviews are needed to establish the cause of death.

Examples listed:

- Vagal inhibition of heart
- Status epilepticus
- Paroxysmal fibrillation
- Uremia

### Negative autopsy

The PDF states:

- Approximately **2–5%** of autopsies.
- No explanatory finding on external/internal examination.
- No explanatory laboratory result.
- History and interviews also fail to establish the cause.
- Cause of death remains **undetermined**.

> **Source-integrity note:** Numerical percentages and historical labels in this chapter are retained because they are part of the PDF. They should not be treated as universal modern epidemiological constants.

## 1.7 Postmortem external examination

The source associates the external examination with:

- Identification/documentation.
- Documentation of injuries.
- Postmortem changes used in assessment of **time since death (TSD)**.

The police/investigating officer is described as the person responsible for identification in the source workflow.

## 1.8 Internal examination — order of cavity dissection

The PDF states that the **first cavity dissected is the choice of the autopsy surgeon**, but gives classical rules for specific circumstances.

| Situation | Classical rule/source instruction |
|---|---|
| Routine | First cavity depends on autopsy surgeon |
| **Poisoning** | Cranial cavity first in the source, for preservation/assessment of odor |
| **Head injury** | Cranial cavity |
| **Asphyxial death** | Thorax → abdomen → neck, with the neck carefully examined before dissection |
| **Newborn** | Special neonatal sequence may be used |
| **Air embolism** | Thorax is especially important |

### Bloodless dissection in suspected neck compression

The source emphasizes that dissection-related venous congestion/hemorrhage may simulate neck injury.

**Prinsloo's/Gordon artefact** (as named in the PDF):

- Artefactual hemorrhage/congestion in the neck caused by dissection.
- May mimic trauma in hanging/strangulation cases.
- The PDF recommends leaving the neck until later and performing a **bloodless neck dissection**.
- It also describes incising the jugular vein before detailed examination to decompress the venous system.

> **Interpretation:** Neck dissection must be planned deliberately in suspected hanging/strangulation so that surgical artefact does not become confused with antemortem hemorrhage.

## 1.9 Air embolism tests

### Pneumothorax test

Source sequence:

```text
Open skin flap over chest
        ↓
Create a water-filled pocket
        ↓
Puncture an intercostal space / pleura with knife
        ↓
Observe for bubbles in the water
        ↓
Bubbles → supports pneumothorax
```

### Venous air embolism — water test

Source sequence:

```text
Open sternum
  ↓
Incise pericardial membrane
  ↓
Fill pericardial cavity with water
  ↓
Puncture the ventricle
  ↓
Bubbles in water → air in cardiac chambers
```

### Pyrogallol test

The PDF describes:

- A wide-bore syringe/needle containing **pyrogallol + NaOH**.
- Insert into the ventricle and aspirate.
- **Brown colour change** is interpreted in the source as supporting air embolism.

> **Caution:** This is a classical examination-note test. Laboratory confirmation and the overall autopsy findings are more important than any isolated bedside/bench reaction.

### Cranial cavity

In suspected cerebral air embolism, the source says to inspect for **air bubbles along intracranial vessels**.

## 1.10 Diaphragm position in fetus/newborn

The PDF states:

- **Live-born infant with expanded lungs:** diaphragm approximately at the **6th–7th rib**.
- **Dead-born fetus:** diaphragm approximately at the **3rd–4th rib**.

> **Source-integrity note:** These are classical teaching values from the PDF and should not be used in isolation to determine live birth. Assessment of live birth requires integration of lung examination, hydrostatic findings where relevant, histology and case circumstances.

## 1.11 Types of incision

The PDF divides incisions into:

1. **Primary:** on external skin.
2. **Secondary:** on skull vault, ribs, sternum.
3. **Tertiary:** on serous membranes.
4. **Quaternary:** on individual organs.

### Skin-incision patterns

| Incision                       | Description / source use                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Midline vertical incision**  | Chin → pubic symphysis, avoiding umbilicus; opens neck, thorax and abdomen through one line                                             |
| **Y incision**                 | From both acromia below breasts to xiphoid, then vertically to pubic symphysis; cosmetic advantage, especially in females               |
| **Modified Y incision**        | From both mastoid processes to suprasternal notch, then vertically to pubis; useful for detailed neck examination in asphyxial deaths   |
| **T / bucket-handle incision** | Acromion-to-acromion along subclavicular line, with midline extension to pubis                                                          |
| **Inverted Y (newborn)**       | Chin → umbilicus, then bifurcates toward both mid-inguinal points                                                                       |
| **X incision**                 | Posterior body surface; source associates with custodial deaths and assessment of deeper bruising/injury attributed to possible torture |

### Midline incision and umbilicus

The source states that the umbilicus is avoided because scar tissue may make suturing difficult.

> The incision choice should always be adapted to the medicolegal question; the source's named indications are teaching associations, not absolute rules.

## 1.12 Organ-removal techniques

| Technique | Principle | Advantage | Disadvantage / limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Virchow** | Organs removed one by one | Detailed study of each organ | Inter-organ relationships are lost |
| **Letulle / en masse** | Organs removed as one mass from tongue to anus | Preserves relationships; rapid; useful for fetal malformations | Difficult to handle |
| **Ghon / block dissection** | Organs of one cavity removed as a block (e.g. cervicothoracic, abdominal, urogenital) | Easier than en masse while retaining relationships | More limited than whole-body en masse approach |
| **Rokitansky / in situ** | Organs examined/dissected without complete removal | Reduced handling/exposure; useful where infection-control concerns exist | More limited visualization in some contexts |

## 1.13 Head dissection

### Scalp
- **Bimastoid incision** opens the scalp into anterior and posterior flaps.

### Skull vault

- Skull cap is described as opened in a **V-shaped** fashion.
- Electrical saw may be used; the source specifically mentions a **Struker's saw**.

## 1.14 Brain examination and fixation

Source points:

- Brain may be examined **fresh**.
- For delayed examination/histology, the source states fixation in **10% formalin for approximately 4–5 weeks**.
- Cranial cutting method: vertical cuts along the **sagittal plane**.

> **Source-integrity note:** The OCR around the exact fixation duration is imperfect; the rendered PDF is consistent with a several-week fixation interval. The value should be checked against the local laboratory SOP before practical use.

## 1.15 Fetal scalp dissection
### Beneke method
The PDF shows a **butterfly-shaped scalp flap**.

## 1.16 Spinal-cord dissection
Not routinely performed according to the PDF.
### Anterior approach
- Performed after thoracic and abdominal dissection.
- Useful when a lesion is suspected.
- Allows examination of nerve roots and dorsal ganglia.
- Disadvantage: complex dissection.
### Posterior approach — Barr method
- Does not require anterior cavity dissection.
- Easier according to the source.
- Cervical spinal cord is accessible and better visualized.
- Allows assessment of continuity between brain and spinal cord.

## 1.17 Heart dissection
### Inflow–outflow method
The source labels this as the standard/classical method and emphasizes opening along the **direction of blood flow**.

```text
Right atrium
   ↓
Tricuspid valve
   ↓
Right ventricle
   ↓
Pulmonary valve
   ↓
Pulmonary circulation
   ↓
Left atrium
   ↓
Mitral valve
   ↓
Left ventricle
   ↓
Aortic valve
```

### Short-axis / ventricular slicing method
- Multiple cuts **perpendicular to the long axis** of the heart.
- Useful for systematic examination of myocardium.

### Sandwiching / intramural method
- Multiple cuts are made along muscle planes, with horizontal cuts parallel to the heart's axis as represented in the PDF.
- Source use: detection of **myocardial infarction / aortic plaques**.

## 1.18 Stomach dissection
The source recommends:
1. Apply **double ligatures** at the cardiac and pyloric ends to prevent leakage of gastric contents.
2. Cut the stomach **between the ligatures**.
3. Open along the **greater curvature** to examine gastric contents.

### Magenstrasse
The PDF emphasizes the **lesser curvature** as the area of maximum damage in acid ingestion.

## 1.19 Visceral-organ dissection
For organs such as:
- Kidney
- Liver
- Spleen

the PDF describes the **bread-loafing method** — multiple parallel cuts through the organ.

## 1.20 Specimens for DNA and decomposition-stage investigations

| Situation | Source specimen |
|---|---|
| DNA testing | Blood |
| Early decomposition | Muscle / spleen |
| Late decomposition | Bone / teeth / hair roots |

The PDF emphasizes that **bone and teeth are among the last tissues to decompose**.

## 1.21 Exhumation

**Exhumation** = lawful removal of an already buried body for examination.

Also called **disinterment**.

### Source-PDF position

- Authorized by a **magistrate**.
- Source cites **CrPC §176(3)**.
- No maximum time limit is stated in the source.
- Supervised by magistrate, police and doctor in the source workflow.
- Traditionally carried out in the **early morning** in the source notes.
- Source describes collection of surrounding soil samples and a **control soil sample**.

### Soil sampling in the source

The PDF describes six surrounding samples, approximately **500 g each**, from:

- Anterior
- Posterior
- Superior
- Inferior
- Right
- Left

and one **control** soil sample.

The purpose of the control sample is to distinguish genuine poisoning from **postmortem imbibition**.

### Postmortem imbibition

Example used in the PDF:

```text
If arsenic in body > arsenic in soil
→ supports arsenic poisoning

If arsenic in soil > arsenic in body
→ raises possibility of postmortem imbibition from soil
```

> **Current legal update:** The old CrPC framework in the PDF has been replaced by the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)**. BNSS §196(4) provides that a Magistrate may cause an already interred body to be disinterred and examined to discover the cause of death.

# 2. Early Postmortem Changes

**Source pages:** 10–18

## 2.1 Thanatology and taphonomy

### Thanatology

**Study of death in all its aspects.**

### Taphonomy

The PDF's OCR says “postmortem resorption,” which is not a reliable definition.

**Verified/current usage:** Forensic taphonomy concerns the postmortem fate and alteration of human remains, including decomposition and the effects of environmental, biological and physical agents.

### 2.2 Definition of death

The PDF reproduces an old statutory teaching point from **IPC §46** and describes clinical death as permanent cessation of signs of life.

### Bishop's tripod of life

Three essential functions:

1. **Respiration**
2. **Circulation**
3. **Brain function**

The source also calls this the **gateway of life / atria mortis**.

### 2.3 Types of death in the source

#### Somatic / clinical death

Irreversible cessation of:

- Circulation
- Respiration
- Brain function

#### Molecular / cellular death

- Death of individual tissues and cells.
- Timing varies by tissue.
- The source emphasizes that cells may remain viable after clinical death, forming the **supravital period**.
- This is the physiological principle underlying the possibility of organ/tissue retrieval within appropriate medical time windows.

#### Apparent death / suspended animation

- Person appears dead but may recover following resuscitative intervention.
- The source states that cells can continue using dissolved oxygen in blood.
- Important because it can lead to premature declaration of death if a proper assessment is not performed.

### 2.4 Circumstances associated with suspended animation in the PDF

Mnemonic shown in the notes: **I NEW HOTV**.

| Letter/theme | Examples in source |
|---|---|
| I | Insanity / iatrogenic states |
| N | Newborns |
| E | Electrocution |
| W | Wasting diseases — tuberculosis, cachexia, cholera |
| H | Hypothermia / heat stroke |
| O | Drowning |
| T | Typhoid |
| V | Voluntary practice, e.g. yoga practitioners |

These are **classical exam associations** rather than proof that any individual patient is dead or alive.

### 2.5 Historical bedside tests described in the PDF

These tests are best remembered as **historical forensic examination signs**, not modern standards for declaring death.

#### Tests for cessation of circulation

| Test | Source description |
|---|---|
| **Diaphanous test** | Failure to show redness in finger web space when transilluminated from behind |
| **Icard test** | No distal discoloration after injection of fluorescent dye |
| **Magnus test** | No congestion distal to a ligature applied to a finger |

#### Tests for cessation of respiration

| Test | Source description |
|---|---|
| **Winslow test** | No movement of water in a bowl placed on the chest |
| **Feather test** | No feather movement in front of nose |
| **Mirror test** | No fogging of mirror near nose |

> **Current-practice note:** These classical tests are not appropriate substitutes for modern clinical determination of death and, where applicable, accepted neurological/death-by-neurological-criteria protocols.

## 2.6 Classification of postmortem changes

```text
Postmortem changes
├── Immediate — minutes
├── Early — hours to days
└── Late — days to weeks
```

## 2.7 Immediate postmortem changes

The PDF lists:

- Loss of voluntary movements / insensibility — described as the earliest sign.
- Loss of circulation.
- Loss of respiration.

### Autolysis versus putrefaction

| Process | Mechanism | Typical example/source |
|---|---|---|
| **Autolysis** | Endogenous cellular/lysosomal enzymes | Early tissue self-digestion |
| **Putrefaction** | Bacterial enzymatic action | Later decomposition |

## Early Postmortem Changes
### 1. Skin Changes
### 2. Eye changes

#### Retina — Kevorkian sign

- Fragmentation of retinal vessels following cessation of circulation.
- The PDF gives the alternative names **railroad sign** and **cattle-trucking sign**.
- Source places onset within the first few minutes after death and notes that the sign is more readily appreciated when the eyes are open.

#### Sclera — tache noire

- Drying of exposed sclera/cornea region.
- Produces triangular-shaped discoloration/opacities.
- Source gives a sequence **yellow → brown → black**.
- PDF timing: about **3–6 hours** after death.

#### Cornea

Source progression:

```text
Transparent
  ↓
Hazy
  ↓
Opaque
```

#### Intraocular pressure

The PDF depicts a fall from approximately **30 mmHg** to 0 as a postmortem change. Exact timing is diagram-dependent and should not be treated as a precise stopwatch measurement.

#### Vitreous humor

The source emphasizes that:

- Bacteria enter it relatively poorly compared with many other tissues.
- It therefore resists decomposition.
- It is useful for **time-since-death assessment**.
- The PDF specifically highlights **vitreous potassium (K+)** as a useful biochemical indicator.

> **Interpretation:** Vitreous chemistry can support PMI estimation, but PMI is a probabilistic estimate affected by temperature, decomposition, disease and other variables.

### 3. Algor mortis

**Algor mortis** = postmortem cooling of the body.

#### Mechanisms of heat loss in source
- Conduction
- Radiation
- Evaporation

#### Onset
The source states approximately **15 minutes** after death.

#### Temperature-measurement sites
- Common site in source: **rectum**.
- Source's ideal site for core assessment: **subhepatic space**.

#### Instruments
- Thermocouple
- Chemical thermometer / “thermometer” as represented in the PDF.

#### Cooling curve

The source illustrates a **three-phase** fall in body temperature with an approximately **sigmoid / inverted-S** curve.

The PDF gives a rate in the vicinity of **0.1–0.4 °C/hour**, but this is not a universal rate. Cooling is strongly influenced by ambient conditions, body habitus, clothing, immersion and other variables.

#### Henssge nomogram

The PDF uses the **Henssge nomogram** for estimating time since death from rectal temperature and correction factors.

Conceptual structure:

```text
Observed core/rectal temperature
        +
Correction factors for circumstances
        ↓
Henssge nomogram
        ↓
Estimated postmortem interval
```

 > [!note] Postmortem caloricity
Source definition:
>- The body may remain warm or show a transient postmortem temperature rise for approximately **1–2 hours**.
>Associated examples listed:
>- Impaired heat regulation: **heat stroke, pontine hemorrhage**.
>- Increased heat production: **strychnine poisoning, tetanus**.
>- Increased bacterial activity: **septicemia, cholera**.
>
>The PDF additionally states that postmortem caloricity is absent in burns; this is retained as source material but should not be interpreted as a universal exclusion.

### 4. Livor mortis
#### Synonyms in the PDF
- Cadaveric lividity
- Postmortem lividity
- Postmortem staining
- Sugillation
- Coagitation
- Postmortem hypostasis

#### Mechanism

After circulation stops:

```text
Gravity
  ↓
Pooling of blood in dependent capillaries/venules
  ↓
Bluish-purple discoloration from deoxygenated blood
  ↓
Postmortem hypostasis / staining
```

#### Distribution

The source emphasizes that distribution primarily reflects **body position**, not the cause of death.

| Body position | Typical hypostasis described in source |
|---|---|
| Supine | Back of head, posterior chest/abdomen/legs |
| Prone | Front of face, chest and abdomen |
| Vertically suspended | Lower forearms and lower legs — “glove and stocking” distribution |
| Body repeatedly rolling in water | Hypostasis may be poorly formed / absent |

> **Exam pearl:** Glove-and-stocking hypostasis indicates a **vertical position**, not specifically hanging.

#### Contact pallor

Pressure-bearing/contact areas remain pale because blood cannot pool there.

#### Time course in the source

- Onset: approximately **30 min**.
- Visible: approximately **4 h**.
- Fixation: approximately **6–12 h**, with the source emphasizing an average around 8 h.

Before fixation, pressure may cause blanching. After fixation, pressure does not readily eliminate the discoloration.

#### Situations with little/no obvious hypostasis

- Body repeatedly rolling / moving.
- Severe anemia.
- Hemorrhagic / hypovolemic shock.

#### Classical colour associations in the PDF

| Condition | Classical colour association in source |
|---|---|
| Normal hypostasis | Bluish-purple / blue |
| Carbon monoxide poisoning | Cherry red |
| Cyanide poisoning | Cherry red / bright red |
| Hydrogen sulfide | Bluish-green / darkening depending on sulfhemoglobin context |
| Opium | Dark/blackish association in source |
| Hypothermia | Pink / bright coloration in source |
| Methanol | Purple in source |
| Phosphorus / aniline / nitrites / bromides | Dark brown in source |
| Clostridial septic processes | Bronze in source |

> **Caution:** Postmortem colour is **supportive, not pathognomonic**, and can vary with oxygenation, environmental exposure, decomposition and other factors.

### 5. Rigor mortis

**Rigor mortis / cadaveric rigidity** = postmortem stiffening of muscle following death.

#### Sequence of muscle state in the source

```text
Death
 ↓
Primary relaxation
 ↓
ATP depletion
 ↓
Rigor mortis
 ↓
Proteolysis / decomposition
 ↓
Secondary relaxation
```

The PDF depicts:

- ATP ~100% at initial relaxation.
- Stiffness begins when ATP falls to approximately **85%** of normal.
- Maximum rigidity at approximately **15–30%** of normal ATP.

#### Primary relaxation

Source associates passage of:

- Feces
- Urine

with relaxation of sphincters during early primary relaxation.

#### Distribution

The source describes rigor as generalized and says it appears first in involuntary and then voluntary muscles.

**First site of rigor in source:** myocardium.

**First external site:** eyelids.

#### Progression of rigor mortis
##### 2.13 Nysten's rule

The source presents a descending sequence:

```text
Myocardium
  ↓
Neck / jaw / face
  ↓
Thorax
  ↓
Upper limbs
  ↓
Abdomen
  ↓
Lower limbs
  ↓
Fingers and toes
```

The PDF also states that disappearance occurs in the same descending order.

##### 2.14 Shapiro's rule

The PDF presents an opposing/qualifying concept:

- Rigor is a biochemical process occurring **simultaneously** in muscles.
- Differences in apparent onset are influenced by **muscle bulk**.
- Bulky muscles may appear later than smaller muscles.

###### Approximate classical time sequence in source

```text
Onset ~1 h
   ↓
Maximum ~12 h
   ↓
Remains for another ~12 h
   ↓
Gradually disappears over another ~12 h
   ↓
Resolution
```

> **Source-integrity note:** These timings are classical exam values, not precise clinical timestamps.

#### 2.15 Factors influencing rigor mortis

##### Age

The source includes a conflict between texts:

- **Narayan Reddy:** absent below approximately 7 months of intrauterine life.
- **Anil Agarwal:** muscle proteins needed for rigor develop by about 8 weeks of intrauterine life, so rigor would be absent before this.

Because the source itself is contradictory, **do not memorize the disputed lower age threshold as an absolute fact**.

##### Muscle bulk

- More bulky muscle → later apparent onset.
- Less bulky muscle → earlier apparent onset.

##### Early onset associations in the PDF

- Violent death — e.g. firearm injury, cut-throat injury.
- Electrocution.
- Chronic wasting disease — cholera, tuberculosis.
- Tetanus.
- Strychnine poisoning.
- Heat stroke.
- Cyanide poisoning.

##### Late onset associations in the PDF

- Asphyxia.
- Pneumonia.
- Hemorrhage.

#### 2.16 Conditions that mimic rigor mortis
##### Heat stiffening
- Source threshold: **>65 °C**.
- Protein coagulation causes permanent muscle stiffness.
- Associated with burns/high heat exposure.
- Characteristic **pugilistic / boxer attitude** from flexion of joints due to heat-induced protein contraction and differential muscle bulk.

##### Cold stiffening
- Body exposed to freezing conditions.
- Joint fluid freezes → joints become stiff.
- Stiffness disappears after thawing.

##### Gas stiffening / gas rigidity
- Occurs during decomposition.
- Increased decomposition gas production causes marked rigidity/distension.

##### Cadaveric spasm / instantaneous rigor
- Immediate stiffening of a group of voluntary muscles that were active at the time of death.
- No preceding primary relaxation.
- Classically associated with intense emotion/struggle and drowning cases in textbooks.

**Current verification:** The phenomenon remains controversial. Older literature has argued that cadaveric spasm is a myth, while more recent forensic discussion continues to debate whether some instantaneous terminal muscle activity may account for cases traditionally called cadaveric spasm.

> **Exam-safe formulation:** “Cadaveric spasm is a classical forensic concept of instantaneous postmortem rigidity without preceding flaccidity; its pathophysiology and even existence as a distinct phenomenon remain controversial.”

# 3. Late Postmortem Changes

**Source pages:** 19–24

## 3.1 Decomposition

Decomposition = **disintegration / dissolution of the dead body**.

The PDF describes two major processes:

1. **Autolysis**
2. **Putrefaction**

### 3.2 Autolysis
- Self-destruction by **endogenous lysosomal enzymes**.
- Source's external example: **clouding of the cornea**.

### 3.3 Putrefaction
#### Mechanism
- Disintegration by bacterial enzymes.

- Microorganisms listed in the PDF:
	- *E. coli*
	- Streptococci
	- Staphylococci
	- Bacteroides

The gastrointestinal tract is given as the main microbial source.

The PDF labels **Clostridium welchii** as a chief destructive organism and attributes a role to lecithinase.

#### Major changes
1. Colour change.
2. Gas production.
3. Tissue liquefaction / colliquation.

The source places major decomposition changes in a broad **days** range rather than treating any single value as exact.

#### 3.4 Putrefactive colour change
##### Early Internal colour change
- 1st sign of colour change (overall): **reddish-brown discoloration of the aortic intima**. 
- Older teaching once focused on greenish subhepatic discoloration.

##### Early external colour change
- Greenish discoloration first noted in the **right iliac fossa (RIF)** because the caecum contains abundant bacteria.
- Later spreads over the abdominal wall and eventually much of the body.

##### Time in source
- Approximately **12–18 h in summer**.
- Approximately **1–2 days in winter**.

##### Marbling
Bacterial decomposition
  ↓
H₂S production
  ↓
Combination with hemoglobin
  ↓
Sulfhemoglobin / green pigment
  ↓
==Green linear markings along superficial vessels==
  ↓
Marbling

Marbling occurs around: **30–72 h** after death.

##### Medicolegal relevance
- Supports assessment of **postmortem interval**.
- Demonstrates progression of decomposition.

#### 3.6 Gas production
Gases listed:
- CO
- NH₃
- H₂S

The PDF calls **H₂S** the most important decomposition gas.
##### Consequences
1. **Postmortem skin blisters**
2. **Gas rigidity / stiffening**
3. **Postmortem purge**
4. **Foamy / honeycomb liver**

###### 3.7 Antemortem versus postmortem blisters
| Feature        | Antemortem burn blister                                         | Postmortem decomposition blister    |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Content        | Inflammatory fluid, protein/chloride rich in source             | Decomposition gas                   |
| Base           | Reddish in source                                               | Pale in source                      |
| Interpretation | Supports vital injury when integrated with histology/case facts | Postmortem artefact / decomposition |

###### 3.8 Gas rigidity / decomposition stiffening

Source features:

- Body becomes bloated and distended.
- Stiffness may involve the whole body.
- Eyes and tongue may protrude.

###### 3.9 Postmortem purge

- Oozing/discharge of blood or fluid from mouth/nose due to decomposition gas pressure.
- **Must not automatically be attributed to trauma.**

#### 3.10 Order of putrefaction

The PDF gives the following broad order:

##### Earliest

- Larynx
- Trachea

##### Early

- Stomach
- Intestine
- Spleen
- Liver
- Brain
- Heart

Mnemonic in source: **“Sister Lilly's Beautiful HEART.”**

##### Late

- Prostate in males
- Nulliparous uterus in females
- Skin
- Tendon

##### Last

- Bone
- Teeth

##### Special point

The PDF notes that a **gravid uterus** can decompose earlier because it is more vascular.

#### 3.11 Putrefaction and death

The source calls putrefaction a **sure sign of death**, in contrast to early nonspecific signs.

#### 3.12 Poisons said to delay putrefaction

Source list:

- Strychnos / strychnine
- Metallic poisons
- Carbolic acid / phenol

> **Source-integrity note:** These are classical textbook associations; they are not a basis for excluding decomposition when other factors are present.

#### 3.13 Casper's dictum

The PDF gives:

> The rate of putrefaction depends on the surrounding medium.

Classical relative rate in source:

```text
Air : Water : Earth
1   : 2     : 8
```

Thus:

- Fastest in air.
- Intermediate in water.
- Slowest in soil.

This is a teaching approximation rather than a universal quantitative law.

## Methods of preservation
### 3.14 Adipocere
**Adipocere** = wax-like postmortem transformation of body fat.

Other terms in source:
- Grave wax
- “Saponification” (the PDF labels this, but the modern biochemical concept is hydrolysis of triglycerides with formation of fatty acids and other products rather than simple soap formation).

#### Conditions favouring formati
- Moisture.
- Warm environments can facilitate the process.
- Often begins in **subcutaneous fat**.
- Areas rich in fat include breast and gluteal regions.

#### Mechanism 

```text
Body fat
  ↓
Hydrolysis by intrinsic lipases + bacterial processes
  ↓
Fatty acids (e.g. palmitic / stearic acids)
  ↓
Interaction with calcium and minerals
  ↓
Waxy/adipocere material
```

The source emphasizes acidic conditions limiting bacterial multiplication and helping preserve injuries/identifying features.

#### Time course in source
- Earliest reported: approximately **3 days**.
- Average: **3 weeks–3 months**.

Because formation is highly variable, these values are not exact clocks.

#### Medicolegal importance

- Identification of the body.
- Preservation of injuries that may suggest cause of death.
- Assessment of TSD.
- Possible inference about the environment/place of disposal.

### 3.15 Mummification

**Mummification** = drying/dehydration of a body under suitable environmental conditions.

Source scenario:
- Dry, hot environment.
- Free flow of air.
- Dehydration → shrinkage and weight loss.

The source mentions weight loss up to **70%**.
Such a mummified body is odourless.
Time take: **3 months–1 year**.

#### Medicolegal importance

- Identification.
- TSD assessment.
- Possible information on place/environment of disposal.

The PDF states that **antimony and arsenic poisoning** favour mummification.

>[!note] Postmortem photoluminescence
>The source attributes this to organisms that can fluoresce/glow, including:
>
>- *Photobacterium fischeri*
>- *Armillaria* fungus
>
>They may produce visible luminescence in darkness.

### 3.17 Embalming

**Embalming / thanatopraxia** = artificial preservation of a dead body by injecting chemicals.

#### Types
1. **Arterial embalming** — fluid into arteries.
2. **Cavity embalming** — fluid introduced into body cavities.
3. **Surface embalming** — application/injection into tissues/skin.

#### Embalming-fluid composition in source

| Component     | Substance in PDF    |
| ------------- | ------------------- |
| Preservative  | Formalin / methanol |
| Germicide     | Phenol              |
| Wetting agent | Glycerine           |
| Buffer        | Sodium borate       |
| Anticoagulant | Sodium citrate      |
| Dye           | Eosin               |
| Vehicle       | Water               |

The source specifically states that **ethanol is not part of the embalming solution**.

#### Technique points in source

- Best time: within approximately **6 hours**.
- Intermittent/discontinuous injection with drainage.
- Preferred vessels: **carotid or femoral vessels**.
- Technique: **high pressure with low flow**.

#### Skin colour after embalming

- Normal individual: brown-grey.
- Jaundiced patient: green.

#### Disadvantages / medicolegal cautions
- Alters appearance of injuries.
- Produces chemical/permanent rigor.
- May interfere with toxicological evidence.
- May dislodge thrombi/clots.

> **Current legal formulation:** The source's old statement that embalming before autopsy in poisoning is punishable under a particular old IPC section should not be memorized as a current section number. The medicolegal principle is that any intentional destruction, concealment or alteration of evidence can have legal consequences under current law.

**Entomology** = study of insects.

**Forensic entomology** = application of entomological knowledge to forensic/legal investigations.

#### Relevant insects in source

- Housefly — *Musca domestica*
- Blowfly — family **Calliphoridae**
- Flesh fly — family **Sarcophagidae**

#### Insect life cycle

The PDF diagram represents:

```text
Egg
 ↓ 12 hours
Larva / maggot
 ↓ 3-6 days
Pupa
 ↓ 3-6 days
Adult fly

```

#### Medicolegal importance

- Estimation of TSD/PMI in suitable circumstances.
- Assessment of the **place of disposal** or body movement.
- Forensic toxicology through **entomotoxicology**.

#### Preservation of insects

Source methods:

- Absolute alcohol.
- Hot water.

> **Important practical point:** Insect development is temperature-dependent and species-dependent; forensic entomology requires scene-specific sampling and environmental data.

# 4. Asphyxial Deaths — Part 1

**Source pages:** 25–32

## 4.1 Asphyxia

The PDF gives the classical etymological explanation and defines the forensic problem as impaired oxygenation/respiratory function.

### Asphyxial triad — classical

1. **Cyanosis**
2. **Petechial hemorrhages**
3. **Visceral congestion**

The PDF explicitly labels these classical signs as **obsolete/nonspecific signs of asphyxia**.

### Asphyxial quintet — source

The classical triad plus:

4. **Postmortem fluidity of blood**
5. **Right-ventricular enlargement**

> **Verified/current framing:** The triad is nonspecific and cannot by itself establish the mechanism of asphyxia. Current forensic teaching likewise treats cyanosis, congestion and petechiae as supportive rather than diagnostic.

```text
Asphyxial deaths

```

## 4.3 Artefactual neck hemorrhage — Prinsloo's/Gordon artefact

Dissection-related hemorrhage/congestion can mimic antemortem neck injury.

### Prevention in source

1. Open cranial cavity first in suspected neck-compression cases.
2. Keep neck dissection until later.
3. Use **bloodless dissection**.
4. Decompress venous blood by incising the jugular vein before detailed examination.

# 4.4 Hanging

**Definition in source:** Constriction of the neck by a ligature associated with suspension of the body by body weight.

## Classification by knot position

| Type | Knot position |
|---|---|
| **Typical hanging** | Knot in the occipital region |
| **Atypical hanging** | Knot anywhere other than occipital region |

## Classification by suspension

| Type | Source definition | Main constricting force |
|---|---|---|
| **Complete hanging** | Whole body suspended; no body part touches ground | Whole body weight |
| **Partial hanging** | Some body part touches ground; only part of body suspended | Part of body weight |

The source calls complete hanging faster and partial hanging slower, but actual time to death depends on the anatomy and mechanism rather than suspension category alone.

## 4.5 Ligature

Any available material may act as a ligature, e.g.:

- Rope
- Cloth
- Saree/dupatta and similar material

## 4.6 Mechanisms of death in hanging

The source identifies:

- **Asphyxia**.
- Cerebral venous congestion.
- Possible vagal inhibition from neck pressure.
- In judicial hanging, severe cervical injury can be the dominant mechanism.

### Classical sequence of airway/vascular effects

The source emphasizes that veins, arteries, airway and nerves are compressed to different degrees depending on knot position, ligature and force.

> The OCR of the PDF's numerical force thresholds for individual neck structures is unreliable. **Do not memorize numerical kilogram values from this reconstruction.**

## 4.7 External examination in hanging

### Face

Source associations:

- Cerebral venous congestion.
- Cerebral anemia.
- Asphyxial changes.

### Ligature mark

Typical source description:

- Usually **pale**.
- **Parchment-like**.
- May become dark brown later.
- Often **incomplete**.
- Often **oblique**.
- Usually **above the thyroid cartilage**.

### Factors affecting ligature mark

| Factor | Effect in source |
|---|---|
| Thin/narrow ligature | Narrower/deeper mark; may act as a “cheese cutter” |
| Broad/soft ligature | Faint, superficial mark |
| Fixed knot / fixed noose | Incomplete, oblique mark |
| Slip/running knot | May produce more complete/transverse mark |

> These are **classical patterns**, not absolute diagnostic rules.

### Glove-and-stocking hypostasis

Not evidence of hanging by itself; indicates prolonged **vertical positioning**.

### Dribbling of saliva

The source emphasizes saliva dribbling from the mouth as a strong sign supporting **antemortem hanging**, attributed to salivary stimulation during neck compression.

> **Current forensic interpretation:** Saliva dribbling may be supportive in context but should not be treated as pathognomonic or as a standalone proof of vitality.

### Tongue protrusion

The source attributes it to lifting of the pharyngeal skeleton.

### La facie sympathique

Source description:

- Ipsilateral opening of eyelids.
- Ipsilateral pupillary dilatation.
- Attributed to pressure on the cervical sympathetic chain.
- Traditionally described as a sign supporting antemortem hanging.

Again, it is a **classical supportive sign**, not an absolute criterion.

## 4.8 Suspension peak

Classical source diagram:

- Ligature mark rises toward the point of suspension.
- Described as an **inverted V-shaped** peak.

The source uses this to distinguish hanging from strangulation, but modern interpretation should use the entire injury pattern and circumstance rather than one visual sign.

## 4.9 Special low-point suspension

The PDF notes that when the point of suspension is low, e.g. a door/window level, a **transverse appearance** may occur despite hanging.

This is an important exception to the simplistic rule that “hanging always gives an oblique mark.”

## 4.10 Internal findings in hanging

### Hyoid and laryngeal skeleton

The source lists:

- Thyroid cartilage fracture.
- Hyoid bone fracture.
- Cricoid fracture rarely, in very strong compression.

The PDF associates hyoid fractures particularly with older individuals, approximately **>40 years**, because ossification makes fracture more likely.

### Hyoid fracture patterns in source

- **Abduction fracture:** both ends displaced outward.
- **Side-to-side fracture:** one end displaced outward and one inward.
- **Inward compression**: associated with throttling.

### Hangman's fracture

**Hangman's fracture** = fracture through the posterior elements/pars interarticularis of **C2 (axis)**, classically with hyperextension and axial loading.

The source associates it particularly with a **long-drop judicial hanging**.

> **Important correction:** The essential vertebral level is **C2**, not atlas (C1). The source's OCR around the vertebral levels is garbled.

### Amussat sign

- **Transverse intimal tears of the carotid arteries**.
- Classical association with hanging.

### Simon's hemorrhages

- Linear/stripe-like hemorrhages in the intervertebral discs, classically described in the lumbar region.
- The source attributes them to stretching of the spine during suspension.

## 4.11 Manner of death in hanging

The source lists:

1. **Suicidal** — the usual association.
2. **Homicidal** — uncommon but possible.
3. **Accidental** — possible.

Manner must be determined from the complete scene, history, body position, injuries and ancillary evidence.

## 4.12 Fatal period

The PDF gives approximately **3–5 minutes**.

> This is a classical exam value; actual survival time varies significantly with mechanism, degree of vascular/airway compression, rescue, and associated injuries.

## 4.13 Judicial hanging

The PDF states that judicial hanging is practiced in India and may cause severe cervical injury in addition to asphyxial/vascular mechanisms.

### Hangman's knot

Source notes:

- Classical ideal location: **submental**.
- The PDF states an Indian placement **below the ear / angle of mandible**.

## 4.14 Homicidal hanging / lynching

The PDF uses **extrajudicial hanging / lynching** for homicidal hanging by a group of persons in a public place.

# 4.15 Autoerotic / sexual asphyxia

The source describes accidental death in individuals who intentionally create partial neck compression to produce altered sexual sensation.

Terms appearing in the source include:

- Hypoxiphilia
- Asphyxiophilia
- Historical terminology such as “hanging”

### Mechanism as shown in the PDF

```text
Partial neck occlusion
  ↓
Carotid compression
  ↓
Cerebral ischemia / altered consciousness
  ↓
Erotic sensations / orgasm
  ↓
Release of pressure intended
  ↓
Normal state
```

Death occurs when the individual cannot release the constricting mechanism.

# 4.16 Strangulation

**Definition:** Neck compression without suspension of the body.

## Ligature strangulation

### Ligature mark

Source pattern:

- Usually **transverse**.
- Usually **complete**.
- Usually **below thyroid cartilage**.
- May become oblique if the body is dragged.

### Other findings

- Congested face.
- Subconjunctival hemorrhages.
- Bruises/abrasions around ligature mark due to struggle and relative movement of ligature.

## Hanging vs ligature strangulation

| Feature | Hanging | Ligature strangulation |
|---|---|---|
| Suspension | Present | Absent |
| Direction of mark | Usually oblique | Usually transverse |
| Completeness | Often incomplete | Usually complete |

# 4.17 Pseudoligature mark

Skin folds in the neck, especially in **obesity or elderly persons**, can resemble a ligature mark.

The mark must therefore be interpreted in the context of:

- Entire neck examination.
- Internal neck injuries.
- Scene findings.
- Ligature material.
- Histopathology where appropriate.

# 4.18 Throttling / manual strangulation

**Definition:** Neck compression by the **hands**.

The PDF states that it is **almost always homicidal**.

### External findings

- Crescentic / vertical fingernail abrasions.
- Bruises from fingertips.
- “Six-penny bruises” — round/discoid fingertip bruises in the source.

### Internal findings

- Extensive soft-tissue contusion in neck muscles.
- Thyroid cartilage fracture.
- Hyoid fracture.
- Rare cricoid fracture with very severe force.

### Hyoid mechanism in source

- **Inward compression** → adduction-type fracture.
- Source calls throttling the **most common cause of hyoid fracture** in this context.

# 4.19 Palmar strangulation / mugging

Source description:

- One hand covers the mouth/nostrils.
- The other hand produces pressure over the neck.

It therefore combines an element of airway obstruction with neck compression.

## Carotid sleeper hold

- Elbow/forearm compresses **carotid arteries**.
- Trachea is relatively spared.
- Unconsciousness results from reduced cerebral perfusion.

## Choke hold

- Forearm compresses the **trachea/airway**.
- Asphyxial mechanism is emphasized.

## 4.20 Garrotting

- Thin cord or iron cuff used from behind to compress the neck.
- The PDF describes it as a form of ligature constriction.

## 4.21 Spanish windlass technique

- Variant of garrotting.
- The source describes historical use as a method of judicial execution in Spain/Turkey.

## 4.22 Bansdola

- Bamboo sticks used to constrict the neck.
- Presented in the source as a specific form of mechanical neck compression.

# 5. Asphyxial Deaths — Part 2

**Source pages:** 33–42

## 5.1 Suffocation

**Source definition:** Asphyxia without neck compression, due to exclusion/impairment of air reaching the lungs.

Major types in the source:

- Smothering
- Gagging
- Choking
- Traumatic asphyxia
- Positional asphyxia
- Environmental suffocation
- Drowning

---

## 5.2 Smothering

**Definition:** Closure of the mouth and nostrils by hands or another object.

### Accidental examples

- Infant covered by heavy blanket/bedsheet.
- Child playing with plastic bag over face.
- Adult falling into sand/mud or other material covering the face.

### Homicidal examples

- Pillow over mouth/nose.
- Hands over mouth/nose.

### Local injuries from hand smothering

- Perioral abrasions/bruises.
- Lip contusion.
- Frenulum tear/rupture.

Infants and elderly persons are particularly vulnerable.

The source calls suicidal smothering **very unlikely**.

---

## 5.3 Gagging
- Cloth or other material inserted into the mouth.
- Obstructs the pharynx.
- Source associates it with kidnapping/abduction.
- Generally homicidal in medicolegal context.
- Death may be accidental in the course of an attempt to silence the victim.

## 5.4 Choking

**Definition:** Internal mechanical obstruction of the airway by a foreign body.

Example in source: **food bolus**.

### Sequence

```text
Foreign body enters airway
  ↓
Cough / respiratory distress
  ↓
Airway obstruction
  ↓
Hypoxemia / asphyxia
  ↓
Death if obstruction is not relieved
```

The PDF mentions the **Heimlich manoeuvre** as a first-aid concept.

> **Current-practice note:** Actual choking first aid should follow contemporary resuscitation guidance rather than relying on the old one-line “Heimlich manoeuvre” teaching note.

### Cafe coronary syndrome

The source describes a sudden death while eating, particularly in an obese/intoxicated/drugged person.

It emphasizes that this is a **misnomer** and not necessarily due to myocardial infarction.

The PDF attributes the term to **Ronald Haugen**.

---

## 5.5 Traumatic asphyxia

**Definition:** Asphyxia resulting from mechanical restriction of chest expansion.

### Causes listed in source

- Heavy weight on chest.
- Stampede.
- Building collapse.
- Being crushed.
- Burning alive, where smothering and traumatic mechanisms may coexist.

### Mechanism

```text
Mechanical chest compression
  ↓
Reduced chest expansion + obstruction of venous return
  ↓
Marked venous congestion in head/neck
  ↓
Asphyxia
```

### Masque ecchymotique

The body region **above the level of compression** may show:

- Marked cyanosis.
- Petechiae.
- Congestion/ecchymosis.

The part of the chest under pressure may instead be pale and injured; the PDF illustrates rib fractures in the compressed chest.

---

## 5.6 Positional asphyxia

**Definition:** Respiratory impairment from abnormal positioning of the body, restricting lung expansion.

### Examples

- **Jack-knife position:** abdominal organs compress the lungs.
- **Inverted crucifixion:** internal organs can compress the lungs in a head-down configuration.

## Overlaying

- Larger person shares a bed with a small infant.
- Infant becomes trapped beneath/against the larger body.
- Mechanism may combine:
  - Smothering.
  - Traumatic chest restriction.

---

## 5.7 Burking

Historical term from the source.

**Method:**

- Perpetrator sits on the victim's chest.
- Mouth/nostrils are simultaneously closed.

### Mechanism

```text
Mouth/nose occlusion
      +
Chest compression
      ↓
Smothering + traumatic asphyxia
```

The source links the term historically with **William Burke and William Hare**, who murdered people and sold bodies for dissection.

---

## 5.8 Wedging

Source example:

- Infant falls into a narrow gap between bed and wall.
- Body becomes trapped.
- The source classifies this as a form of **traumatic asphyxia**.

---

## 5.9 Environmental suffocation

Occurs when oxygen availability becomes inadequate in a confined/contained space.

Examples in source:

- Sewage tank.
- Unused well.
- Enclosed box / confined space.

Mechanism is fundamentally **environmental hypoxia**, rather than neck constriction.

## 5.10 Drowning

The PDF defines drowning as respiratory impairment due to **submersion/immersion in water** and notes that complete body submersion is not necessary if the airway is submerged.

> **Verified/current definition:** WHO defines drowning as the process of experiencing respiratory impairment from submersion/immersion in liquid, with outcomes that may include death or survival with/without morbidity.

### 5.10.1 Mechanism shown in source

```text
Person falls into water
      ↓
Panic / breath holding
      ↓
Air hunger
      ↓
Involuntary inspiration
      ↓
Water/fluid reaches airway
      ↓
Respiratory impairment
      ↓
Hypoxemia / cerebral hypoxia
      ↓
Death or survival with injury
```

The PDF also illustrates possible laryngeal reflex closure and variable water aspiration.

#### 5.10.2 Freshwater versus saltwater drowning — classical source model

#### Freshwater drowning

Source teaching model:

```text
Hypotonic freshwater enters alveoli
  ↓
Hemodilution
  ↓
RBC swelling → hemolysis
  ↓
Hyperkalemia
  ↓
Hypervolemia
  ↓
Cardiac overload / ventricular fibrillation
```

The source additionally describes:

- Surfactant washout.
- Alveolar collapse.
- V/Q mismatch.
- Cerebral anoxia.

#### Saltwater drowning

Source teaching model:

```text
Hypertonic saltwater in alveoli
  ↓
Fluid movement out of circulation
  ↓
Hemoconcentration
  ↓
Hypovolemia
  ↓
Pulmonary edema + respiratory failure
  ↓
Cardiovascular collapse
```

#### Current correction

The old “freshwater = hemolysis/ hyperkalemia → VF” versus “saltwater = hemoconcentration → hypovolemia” dichotomy is a **classical textbook model** and should not be treated as the dominant real-world mechanism in most drowning cases. Modern literature shows that clinically important electrolyte shifts from aspirated water are usually much less dramatic than the classical model suggests, because the volume of aspirated water is often small relative to blood volume.

> **Exam-safe phrasing:** Know the classical fresh-vs-saltwater diagram because it is historically tested, but clinically the central pathophysiology is **hypoxemia from respiratory impairment**.

### 5.11 Hydrocution / immersion syndrome

Source description:

- Sudden immersion in cold water.
- Cold stimulus, emotional stress or abrupt water contact may trigger intense autonomic/vagal effects.
- The PDF specifically describes water around **5 °C below body temperature** and “duck diving”/feet-first entry.
- Bradycardia and cardiac arrest are attributed to vagal inhibition in the source.

> **Current note:** The term “hydrocution” is historically used and encompasses multiple proposed mechanisms; it should not be treated as a single pathophysiologically proven entity.

---

### 5.12 Delayed complications after drowning
The source uses the older terms:
#### Secondary drowning / Post-immersion syndrome / Near-drowning

It describes delayed death from pulmonary complications and lists:

- Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy.
- Metabolic acidosis.
- Electrolyte abnormalities.
- Aspiration pneumonia.

#### Current terminology

Contemporary sources discourage terms such as **secondary drowning**, **dry drowning**, **wet drowning**, and **near-drowning** and instead use the single diagnosis **drowning** with specification of outcome and complications.

> **Source-PDF translation:** Read “secondary drowning/post-immersion syndrome” as the older description of **delayed respiratory or systemic complications after a nonfatal drowning event**.

### 5.13 Postmortem findings in drowning

The PDF emphasizes:

- Drowning may occur **antemortem or postmortem**.
- Diagnosis is principally a **diagnosis of exclusion**.
- No single autopsy finding is pathognomonic.

#### Floatation of body

Source classical values:

- Summer: approximately **1 day**.
- Winter: approximately **2–3 days**.

Again, this is environmentally variable.

#### 5.13.1 Cadaveric spasm with material in hands
The source describes:
- Grass/mud tightly held in the hands.
- Traditionally interpreted as suggestive of **antemortem drowning**.

> **Caution:** Hand contents are supportive at most; postmortem handling, entrapment and subsequent rigor can produce similar appearances. The phenomenon of cadaveric spasm itself is controversial.

#### 5.13.2 Froth at mouth/nose
Classical source features:
- Fine.
- Tenacious/mucoid.
- Copious.
- Persistent even after wiping.

##### Mechanism in source

```text
Water irritates airway
  ↓
Respiratory struggle
  ↓
Increased mucus
  ↓
Water + mucus + air + surfactant
  ↓
Persistent fine froth
```

The source calls it suggestive of **antemortem drowning**.

#### No froth in source

- “Dry drowning” / laryngospasm pattern.
- Hydrocution.
- Unconscious person without major respiratory struggle.

> **Current terminology note:** “Dry drowning” is an obsolete term; absence of froth does not exclude drowning.

#### 5.13.3 Cutis anserina

- Gooseflesh from erector pili muscle contraction.
- **Nonspecific**.
- May occur after death regardless of drowning.

#### 5.13.4 Washerwoman's hands/feet

Water immersion → maceration/soddening of skin.

Source sequence:

| Change | Classical source timing |
|---|---:|
| Wrinkling | 3–4 h |
| Peeling of cuticle | ~12 h |
| Bleaching | ~18 h |
| Soddening | ~24 h |

Sequence of spread in source:

```text
Fingers → palm → dorsum of hand → sole
```

More pronounced in **warm water** in the source.

> These are approximate environmental teaching values, not reliable individual clocks.

### 5.14 Internal findings in drowning

#### Lungs

Source findings:

- Voluminous.
- Crepitant.
- “Ballooning out” / protruding when sternum is opened.
- Mud/sand may occur in the lower airway in some cases.

##### 1. Paltauf hemorrhages

- Subpleural hemorrhages.
- Source attributes them to rupture of alveolar walls/capillaries during respiratory struggle.
- Predominantly in **lower-lobe subpleural areas**.

They are **not pathognomonic**.

##### 2. Emphysema aquosum

Source definition:

- Bullous overdistension plus Paltauf hemorrhages.
- Classically associated with a **conscious** person struggling to breathe during drowning.

##### 3. Edema aquosum
Source description:
- Heavy lungs.
- Pulmonary edema.
- Little/no characteristic froth.
- Source associates it with an unconscious (**not dead, so antemortem**) person drowning.

##### 4. Hydrostatic lungs
Source uses this term for ==postmortem drowning==, where water enters the lungs after death.

#### Gastrointestinal water

The source states that water in:

- Stomach.
- Intestines.

supports antemortem swallowing, but this must be interpreted in context.

#### Sinuses

- **Sveshnikov's sign** — water in paranasal sinuses, as presented in source.

#### Middle ear

- Middle-ear hemorrhage / **Veno's sign** in source.
- Described as nonspecific.

### 5.15 Tests in drowning

#### Diatom test

##### What are diatoms?

- Unicellular algae.
- Found in many bodies of water.
- Species vary by environment.
- Outer wall contains **silica**.
- Relatively resistant to heat, acid and putrefaction.

##### Classical mechanism

```text
Water with diatoms
  ↓
Respiratory aspiration
  ↓
Alveoli
  ↓
Circulation
  ↓
Potential deposition in marrow / brain / spleen
```

The source gives a size range of roughly **<40–100 μm**, though the exact threshold is context-dependent and the OCR is not completely reliable.

##### Interpretation in source

- Diatoms found in marrow plus matching water-body organisms were said to support antemortem drowning.
- Not expected in dry/hydrocution patterns.
- The source says the test is **not in common use**.

> **Current interpretation:** Diatom testing is limited by contamination, background diatoms and variable sensitivity/specificity. It should be treated as an ancillary test rather than a standalone diagnosis.

#### Gettler test
Comparison of **chloride concentration** between right- and left-sided cardiac blood.

Source classical model:

| Situation | Source prediction |
|---|---|
| Normal | Approximately equal chloride concentration |
| Freshwater drowning | Right > left due hemodilution |
| Saltwater drowning | Left > right due hemoconcentration |

Limitations listed:
- Dry drowning.
- Hydrocution.
- Patent foramen ovale / mixing.
- Limited practical usefulness.

Again, it is a historical/classical forensic test, not a definitive stand-alone diagnosis.

### Typical versus atypical drowning

| Type | Meaning in source |
|---|---|
| **Typical drowning** | Death associated with water entering the airways/lungs |
| **Atypical drowning** | Death in water without the classical “water-entering-lungs” mechanism; source includes dry drowning/hydrocution |

# 9. Medicolegal / Legal Updates to the PDF

> This section deliberately separates **the PDF's 2023-era legal teaching** from the **current statutory framework**. The PDF uses older IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act section numbers on its final pages.

## 9.1 Criminal law: IPC → BNS

### Source PDF

The PDF uses old **Indian Penal Code (IPC)** references, including examples around:

- Definition of death.
- Criminal responsibility of children.
- Kidnapping.
- Homicide.
- Certain evidence-related/consent points.

### Current position

The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)** is now the operative substantive criminal code. It was enforced from **1 July 2024**.

The official India Code text identifies:

- §20 — act of a child under seven.
- §21 — act of a child above seven and under twelve years of immature understanding.
- §63 — rape.
- §91–94 — specified child/birth-related offences.
- §97 — kidnapping/abducting a child under 10 with intent to steal from the child's person.
- §100–103 — culpable homicide/murder and punishment.
- §137 — kidnapping.
- §141 — importation of girl or boy from foreign country.
- §358 — repeal/savings framework relating to prior penal law.

## 9.2 Criminal procedure: CrPC → BNSS

The **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** replaced the CrPC framework and was enforced from **1 July 2024**. India Code lists it as Act 46 of 2023.

### Exhumation

Current **BNSS §196(4)** provides that where the Magistrate considers examination of an already interred body necessary to discover the cause of death, the Magistrate may cause the body to be **disinterred and examined**.

Therefore:

> **PDF/source text:** CrPC §176(3) for exhumation.
>
> **Current verification:** Refer to **BNSS §196(4)** for the corresponding current procedural provision.

## 9.3 Evidence law: Indian Evidence Act → BSA

The **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** replaced the Indian Evidence Act framework from 1 July 2024.

### Child witnesses

The PDF says a child can give evidence but suggests a particular age threshold for oath-taking.

**Current legal principle:** BSA §124 frames competency functionally: persons are competent unless the court considers that tender years or another cause prevents them from understanding questions or giving rational answers. There is **no simple universal “under 12 cannot testify / over 12 must take oath” rule** equivalent to the PDF's old mnemonic-style formulation.

> **PDF/source text:** Age-based oath teaching on page 71.
>
> **Current verification:** Apply the current BSA competency framework rather than memorizing a rigid 12-year cutoff.

## 9.4 Age of majority

The **Majority Act, 1875** currently provides that a person domiciled in India attains majority on completing **18 years**.

> Majority is not identical to every other age threshold in Indian law; marriage, sexual consent, child-protection law, employment, driving, voting and medical-consent questions can have different statutory rules.

## 9.5 Marriage age

The **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006** defines a “child” as:

- Male: **under 21 years**.
- Female: **under 18 years**.

Therefore the source's mnemonic association of “18 = marriage” is incomplete for Indian marriage law.

## 9.6 POCSO / child threshold

For child sexual-protection law, the relevant legal framework treats a **child as below 18 years**.

The BNS rape provision also treats sexual intercourse with a person **under 18** within the statutory offence framework regardless of consent.

## 9.7 Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)

### Source PDF

The last pages contain a compressed old-style MTP age/doctor-count mnemonic.

### Current statutory framework

Under the **MTP Act as amended in 2021**:

- Up to **20 weeks**: opinion of **one registered medical practitioner** under the statutory conditions.
- **20–24 weeks**: opinion of **two registered medical practitioners** for specified categories covered by the Rules.
- Beyond 24 weeks in specified circumstances involving substantial fetal abnormalities: the **Medical Board** provisions become relevant.

The 2021 Rules identify categories including survivors of rape/sexual assault, minors, certain changes in marital status, women with disabilities/mental illness, substantial fetal abnormalities, and other prescribed circumstances. 

> **PDF/source text:** one-doctor/two-doctor/Medical Board mnemonic.
>
> **Current verification:** Use the **MTP Act, 1971 as amended in 2021 and MTP Rules, 2021**, rather than the old shorthand table.
