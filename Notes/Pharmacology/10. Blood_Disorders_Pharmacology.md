# 1. ANTIPLATELET DRUGS

## 1.1 Clinical indications in the PDF

**[PDF]** Antiplatelet drugs are presented as drugs used when there is excessive platelet deposition in blood vessels.

| Location | Disease/example in PDF |
|---|---|
| Coronary artery | MI (myocardial infarction) |
| Brain/cerebral artery | Ischemic stroke |
| Limb arteries/leg arteries | PVD (peripheral vascular disease), including Buerger disease in the handout |

**Clinical distinction:** Platelet-rich **arterial** thrombosis is the central pharmacologic setting for antiplatelet therapy. Venous thromboembolism is primarily a coagulation/fibrin-driven process and is treated with anticoagulation.

---

## 1.2 Physiology of platelet attachment, recruitment and aggregation

The first page contains a platelet diagram linking COX-1/TXA2, ADP/P2Y12, cAMP and GP receptors. The logical sequence is:

```text
Platelet adhesion/attachment
        |
        | COX-1 in platelets -> thromboxane A2 (TXA2)
        v
Platelet activation / recruitment
        |
        | ADP -> P2Y12 receptor
        | cAMP opposes platelet activation/recruitment
        v
Platelet aggregation
        |
        | GP IIb/IIIa activated
        | fibrinogen cross-links adjacent platelets
        v
Stable platelet aggregate / primary hemostatic plug
```

### The three steps presented in the PDF

### Step 1 - Platelet attachment

**[PDF]**
- COX-1 in platelets produces **TXA2**.
- TXA2 promotes platelet activation/attachment.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The earliest adhesion of circulating platelets to exposed subendothelial matrix is classically mediated by **GP Ib-IX-V binding von Willebrand factor (vWF)**. TXA2 is an important platelet agonist/amplifier but is not the sole molecular mechanism of initial adhesion.

### Step 2 - Platelet recruitment

**[PDF]**
- Activated platelets release **ADP**.
- ADP acts on platelet **P2Y12 receptors**.
- This recruits/activates additional platelets.
- **cAMP inhibits platelet activation/recruitment** in the simplified schema.

### Step 3 - Platelet aggregation

**[PDF]**
- Recruited platelets are cross-linked by a fibrin-like "cement".
- The PDF labels the relevant receptor as **GP 2B3A**.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The standard terminology is **glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GP IIb/IIIa; integrin alphaIIb beta3)**. It binds **fibrinogen** (and also vWF under some conditions), allowing platelet-to-platelet cross-linking.

## 1.3 Classification of antiplatelet drugs

| Class | Key action | Drugs in the PDF |
|---|---|---|
| Irreversible COX-1 inhibitor | ↓ TXA2 synthesis | **Aspirin** |
| P2Y12 (ADP receptor) blockers | ↓ platelet recruitment/activation | Ticlopidine, clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, cangrelor |
| PDE-3 inhibitors | ↑ intracellular cAMP; inhibits platelet activation | Pentoxifylline, dipyridamole, cilostazol |
| GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors | Inhibit platelet aggregation | Abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban |
| Other/newer agents | Other platelet activation pathways | Vorapaxar; dazoxiben is mentioned as investigational |

## 1.4 Aspirin

### Mechanism

**[PDF]**
- Irreversible inhibitor of **cyclooxygenase (COX)**.
- Described as a **"suicide inhibitor" / "hit-and-run" drug**.
- ↓ platelet TXA2 production -> ↓ platelet activation/attachment.
- Because the platelet cannot synthesize new COX-1, the effect persists for the platelet lifespan.

### Clinical use

**[PDF]**
- Presented as an overall drug of choice for MI, stroke and Buerger disease/PVD.
- Antiplatelet dose range in the handout: **42-325 mg**.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Current antiplatelet use depends on the clinical syndrome and treatment phase. Aspirin remains a foundational antiplatelet agent in acute coronary syndromes and in secondary prevention, but modern ACS practice commonly uses **dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)** with aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor when indicated. Modern stroke care also uses short-course DAPT in selected minor stroke/TIA scenarios rather than aspirin alone in every patient. See the current ACS and stroke references in Appendix C.

### Dosing table preserved from the PDF

| Setting | PDF dose | PDF duration |
|---|---:|---|
| Acute MI / acute stroke | **325 mg orally** | Single dose |
| Secondary prophylaxis | **75 mg**, may be increased up to 100 mg | Lifelong |

**[Exam note]** These doses should not be read as universal dosing instructions for every contemporary stroke/ACS scenario; clinical dosing depends on the specific indication.

### Adverse effects

**[PDF]**
- Increased bleeding time/bleeding risk.
- Peptic ulcer/gastrointestinal injury.

### Aspirin-associated peptic ulcer management

**[PDF]**
- Drug of choice listed: **proton-pump inhibitor (PPI)** such as omeprazole or pantoprazole.
- Alternative listed: **misoprostol**.

### Surgery

**[PDF]**
- "Stop aspirin 7 days before major surgery."
- Rationale given: platelet lifespan about 7 days and irreversible COX-1 inhibition.

**[Verified/Corrected]** A fixed 7-day stop rule is not appropriate for every patient. Perioperative aspirin decisions depend on the indication for aspirin, bleeding risk of the procedure, cardiovascular risk of interruption, and whether aspirin is being used for secondary prevention or another reason. Platelet function recovery follows new platelet production over several days, but whether aspirin should be stopped is a clinical risk-benefit decision.

### Breastfeeding / Reye syndrome - correction of a PDF error

**[PDF]** The source states that aspirin is "absolutely contraindicated" during lactation because of risk of liver damage/Reye syndrome in the neonate.

**[Verified/Corrected]** This is too absolute and conflates two different issues. Current LactMed information states that **low-dose aspirin (75-325 mg/day) produces low salicylate levels in breast milk**, although monitoring for bruising/bleeding is advised if it is used. Continuous high-dose maternal aspirin is less desirable. Reye syndrome is associated with **aspirin administration to infants/children with viral infections**; the risk from low-dose salicylate exposure through breast milk is unknown rather than established as a neonatal Reye syndrome mechanism.

## 1.5 P2Y12 (ADP receptor) blockers

### Mechanism

**[PDF]** Inhibit platelet recruitment by blocking the platelet **P2Y12 ADP receptor**.

### Classification

| Type | Drugs | PDF wording/notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Irreversible** | Ticlopidine, clopidogrel, prasugrel | Prodrug/inactive until metabolized (for these thienopyridines) |
| **Reversible** | Ticagrelor, cangrelor | Active drugs; not prodrugs |

### Indications and special points in the PDF

| Drug | PDF use | Important PDF note |
|---|---|---|
| **Clopidogrel** | MI + stroke | Add-on to aspirin in DAPT; synergistic/additive effect |
| **Ticlopidine** | MI + stroke | Older P2Y12 drug |
| **Prasugrel** | "Only MI" | PDF states contraindicated in stroke due to bleeding/ICH risk |
| **Ticagrelor** | "Only MI" | Reversible P2Y12 blocker |
| **Cangrelor** | IV only | Used around stent placement/PCI |

**[Verified/Corrected]** Contemporary indications are broader and depend on ACS, PCI, stroke/TIA subtype and bleeding risk. Prasugrel is specifically used in selected ACS patients undergoing PCI and is **contraindicated in patients with a prior stroke or TIA**. Ticagrelor has indications beyond MI alone, including other ACS settings and selected stroke/TIA strategies.

### Clopidogrel as a prodrug

**[PDF]**
- Clopidogrel is a **prodrug**.
- Requires conversion to its active metabolite via **CYP2C19**.

### PPI interaction

**[PDF]**
- Omeprazole is presented as the strongest CYP inhibitor and therefore an undesirable combination with clopidogrel.
- Rabeprazole is given as preferred; pantoprazole as second choice.
- The PDF mnemonic is essentially: "Clopidogrel -> avoid omeprazole."

**[Verified/Corrected]** The clinically important point is that **omeprazole and esomeprazole can inhibit CYP2C19 and reduce formation of clopidogrel's active metabolite**. When gastroprotection is necessary, a PPI with less CYP2C19 inhibition is commonly preferred. Do not interpret the PDF's rabeprazole/pantoprazole ordering as a universal guideline hierarchy.

### Adverse effects

**[PDF]**
- Common: diarrhea.
- Serious: immune-mediated thrombocytopenia/TTP described in the handout.
- **Ticlopidine:** bone-marrow suppression/agranulocytosis.

**Clinical note:** Ticlopidine is now rarely used because of hematologic toxicity and safer alternatives.

## 1.6 Phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE-3) inhibitors

### Mechanism

**[PDF]**
- Inhibit platelet **PDE-3**.
- This increases **cAMP**.
- cAMP inhibits platelet activation/recruitment.

### Drugs / PDF mnemonic

> **PDE-3 memory:**
> - **P** = Pentoxifylline
> - **D** = Dipyridamole
> - **E-3** = Cilostazol

### PDF clinical framing

- Described as add-on drugs to aspirin.
- **Cilostazol:** specifically emphasized for Buerger disease/intermittent claudication.
- **Pentoxifylline:** described as a rheological agent intended to improve blood flow in limb arteries.
- Headache is listed as the most common adverse effect due to vasodilation.
- The handout says these agents are avoided in MI because PDE-3/cAMP is also relevant to cardiac rate and oxygen demand.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The source's generalized rule "only stroke/Buerger" and its blanket "absolute contraindication in MI" should not be applied as a universal clinical rule. **Cilostazol** is primarily used for intermittent claudication and is **contraindicated in heart failure** in US labeling because of increased mortality in patients with class III-IV heart failure. **Dipyridamole** has a separate role in stroke prevention, commonly in combination with aspirin. **Pentoxifylline** has limited contemporary evidence and is not a routine first-line drug for peripheral artery disease.

## 1.7 GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors

### Mechanism

**[PDF]**
- Also called anti-aggregating or anti-integrin drugs.
- Inhibit platelet aggregation by blocking **GP IIb/IIIa**.

### Drugs - PDF mnemonic

> **E-A-T**
> - **E** = Eptifibatide
> - **A** = Abciximab
> - **T** = Tirofiban

### Use

**[PDF]**
- All three are IV drugs.
- Given during stent placement/PCI (PTCA in the handout).

**[Verified/Corrected]** These drugs are highly selected adjuncts in PCI rather than routine therapy for every patient undergoing stent placement. Abciximab is a monoclonal antibody Fab fragment against GP IIb/IIIa; eptifibatide and tirofiban are smaller molecules.

### Abciximab special property in the PDF

**[PDF]**
- Called a "renal safe" drug because it is a protein/peptide and not expected to be cleared by glomerular filtration in the same way as small molecules.
- Handout also describes immunosuppressive effects through effects on leukocyte integrins.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Renal function still matters when choosing and dosing antiplatelet therapy, and the simple rule "all monoclonal antibodies are renal safe" is not a clinically reliable general pharmacology rule.

## 1.8 Newer antiplatelet drugs

### Vorapaxar

**[PDF]**
- **PAR-1 antagonist**.
- PAR = protease-activated receptor.
- Platelet PAR-1 activation promotes aggregation.
- Presented as an add-on to aspirin and described as approved for MI.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Vorapaxar is a **PAR-1 antagonist used to reduce thrombotic cardiovascular events in selected patients with a history of MI or peripheral arterial disease**. It is not an acute-MI treatment drug in the sense implied by the handout, and its use is limited by major bleeding risk, including intracranial hemorrhage; it is contraindicated in patients with a history of stroke/TIA/ICH in US labeling.

### Dazoxiben

**[PDF]**
- Thromboxane A2 synthase inhibitor.
- Presented as investigational/not currently approved.

**[Status]** Retained as source-derived historical pharmacology; not a routine current clinical drug.

# 2. ANTICOAGULANT DRUGS

## 2.1 Why anticoagulation is used

**[PDF]** Anticoagulants are defined as drugs that prevent **fibrin deposition**.

The handout discusses:
- **DVT** -> excessive fibrin deposition in leg veins.
- A detached venous thrombus can cause **pulmonary embolism (PE)**.
- Common provoking settings listed include immobility, ICU/unconscious state and recent knee/hip surgery.
- **Atrial fibrillation (AF) + mitral stenosis** -> left-atrial thrombus -> embolization -> ischemic stroke/TIA.
- **DIC** -> systemic activation of coagulation and fibrin deposition.

### DVT -> PE flow

```text
Venous stasis / provoking factor
        -> venous thrombus (fibrin-rich)
        -> thrombus embolizes
        -> pulmonary artery obstruction
        -> pulmonary embolism
```

### AF + mitral stenosis -> embolic stroke flow

```text
AF +/- significant mitral stenosis
        -> ineffective atrial contraction / blood stasis
        -> left atrial thrombus
        -> embolization
        -> cerebral arterial occlusion
        -> ischemic stroke or TIA
```

**[PDF]** The source states that TIA represents reversible neuronal dysfunction/damage whereas stroke produces permanent neuronal injury.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Modern definitions are based on tissue injury and transient symptoms, not simply a blanket reversible-versus-permanent rule.

**[PDF]** It advises ECG/echo evaluation in stroke patients to look for AF/mitral stenosis.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Cardiac rhythm evaluation is important when cardioembolic stroke is suspected, but the work-up is individualized and often includes prolonged rhythm monitoring; echocardiography is used when indicated by the clinical scenario.

---

## 2.2 Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

**[PDF]**
- Fibrin deposition occurs throughout the circulation.
- Triggers include activation of the coagulation pathway by systemic disease.
- Causes listed: sepsis (most common in the handout), abruptio placentae, malignancy and mechanical/artificial valves.
- Source says DIC from sepsis/abruption/cancer is an emergency and gives IV heparin as the drug.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The central treatment principle in DIC is **rapid treatment of the underlying cause plus supportive blood-component therapy when bleeding is significant**. Therapeutic-dose heparin is **not routinely given to every patient with DIC**; it may be considered in selected patients when thrombosis predominates or when there is a specific thrombotic indication.

### Mechanical heart valve

**[PDF]**
- Lifelong oral anticoagulation.
- Drug named: **warfarin**.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Mechanical prosthetic valves require **lifelong vitamin K antagonist anticoagulation** unless an individualized specialist plan dictates otherwise. Direct oral anticoagulants should not replace warfarin for mechanical valves.

## 2.3 Clotting pathways - the PDF's exam map

```text
EXTRINSIC PATHWAY                    INTRINSIC PATHWAY
Factor VII                           Factor XII
       \                                 /
        \                               /
         +---------- Factor X ----------+
                        |
                        | Xa
                        v
                 Prothrombin (II)
                        |
                        | Thrombin (IIa)
                        v
                      Fibrin
```

| Pathway | PDF start factor | Main laboratory test |
|---|---|---|
| Extrinsic | **Factor VII** | **PT** |
| Intrinsic | **Factor XII** | **aPTT** |
| Common | Converges at **Factor X** | Both pathways feed the common pathway |

**[PDF]** The handout emphasizes factor Xa as the key step converting prothrombin (factor II) to thrombin (IIa), followed by fibrin formation.

---

## 2.4 Classification of anticoagulants

### Oral anticoagulants

1. **Vitamin K antagonist (VKA):** warfarin, acenocoumarol, dicoumarol/bishydroxycoumarin.
2. **Direct factor Xa inhibitors:** rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban (and other drugs in this class exist).
3. **Direct thrombin inhibitor:** dabigatran.

### Parenteral anticoagulants

1. **Indirect thrombin inhibition via antithrombin:** heparins.
2. **Direct thrombin inhibitors:** argatroban, bivalirudin and other historical/less commonly used agents.
3. **Indirect factor Xa inhibitor:** fondaparinux.

## 3.2 Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)

**[PDF]** The handout calls them "NOACs" and describes them as newer and generally safer than warfarin for many patients.

Common indications presented:
- DVT
- PE
- AF to prevent ischemic stroke/systemic embolism

### Situations where the PDF says warfarin remains the drug of choice

| Situation | PDF choice | Current interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical heart valve | Warfarin | **Warfarin remains required**; DOACs are not recommended as substitutes. |

# 4. WARFARIN

## 4.1 Basic identity

**[PDF]**
- Warfarin is a **coumarin derivative**.
- Historical mnemonic: **WAR-F** = Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation.
- "-arin" suggests a coumarin-derived drug.
- Historically developed from compounds related to rodenticide use.

---

## 4.2 Mechanism of action

**[PDF]**
- Acts in the liver.
- Inhibits **VKORC1** (vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1).
- This reduces regeneration of active vitamin K.
- Production of vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors falls.

```text
Warfarin
   |
   v
Inhibits VKORC1
   |
   v
Less reduced/active vitamin K
   |
   v
Reduced gamma-carboxylation / functional synthesis of
Factors II, VII, IX, X + Protein C + Protein S
   |
   v
Anticoagulant effect
```

### Factor effects

**[PDF]**
- Clotting factors reduced: **II, VII, IX, X**.
- Anticoagulant proteins reduced: **Protein C and Protein S**.

---

## 4.3 Sequential reduction and the paradoxical early phase

**[PDF]**
1. Factor VII falls first.
2. Protein C falls next.
3. Factor II (thrombin) falls last.
4. Full anticoagulant effect takes about **5 days**.

This underlies the classic early **hypercoagulable/paradoxical phase**.

```text
Start warfarin
    |
    +--> Factor VII falls quickly -> PT/INR changes
    |
    +--> Protein C falls -> temporary loss of anticoagulant protection
    |
    +--> Factors II/other clotting factors decline more slowly
    |
    +--> Full therapeutic anticoagulation develops over several days
```

### Complication described in the PDF

- **Dermal vascular necrosis / limb necrosis**.
- **Purple toe syndrome** is also mentioned.

### Prevention / bridging - correction of oversimplification

**[PDF]**
- Combine warfarin with heparin for the initial 5 days.
- Stop heparin after 5 days.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Heparin overlap is classically used when initiating warfarin for **acute VTE**, but routine heparin bridging is **not required for every patient starting warfarin for every indication**. Bridging decisions are indication- and thrombosis-risk-specific.

---

## 4.4 Pharmacokinetics

### Protein binding / volume of distribution

**[PDF]**
- Very low volume of distribution.
- Approximately **99% albumin bound**.
- Extensive protein binding contributes to poor removal by dialysis.

### Enantiomers

**[PDF]**
- Warfarin is a racemic mixture of R- and S-warfarin.
- S-warfarin is described as the active form and R-warfarin as inactive.

**[Verified/Corrected]** **Both R- and S-warfarin are pharmacologically active**, but **S-warfarin is more potent** and is primarily metabolized by CYP2C9.

### Metabolism

**[PDF]**
- Warfarin metabolism is associated with **CYP2C9**.
- The handout links variability to CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genetics.

**[Verified/Corrected]** CYP2C9 genotype and VKORC1 genotype can influence warfarin dose requirements, but routine pre-treatment genotyping is not universally required in clinical practice.

---

## 4.5 Monitoring warfarin

### Test

**[PDF]**
- **PT with INR**.
- PT = prothrombin time.
- INR = international normalized ratio.

Simplified formula in the handout:

```text
INR ~ Patient PT / Control PT
```

**[Verified/Corrected]** Actual INR incorporates the reagent's **ISI (international sensitivity index)**; the simple ratio is a teaching approximation, not the exact formula used by laboratories.

### INR values in the PDF

| Situation | INR |
|---|---:|
| Normal, no anticoagulant | ~1.0 |
| PDF normal range | 0.8-1.1 |
| Common therapeutic target | **2.0-3.0** |

**[Verified/Corrected]** INR targets vary by indication. 2.0-3.0 is common for AF and VTE, but some mechanical valves require a higher target depending on valve type and patient factors.

## 4.6 Warfarin interactions

### PDF table - preserved

| Factor | PDF examples | PDF effect on INR | Better current interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| CYP inhibitors | Ciprofloxacin, ketoconazole | Increase INR | Many CYP inhibitors can increase warfarin exposure; effect is drug-specific. |
| CYP inducers | Rifampicin | Decrease INR | Correct direction: rifampicin strongly induces metabolism and can reduce effect. |
| Vitamin K deficiency | Antibiotics, aspirin | Increase INR | Antibiotics can increase INR through several mechanisms; aspirin primarily increases **bleeding risk** rather than predictably raising INR. |
| Liver disease | - | Increase INR/toxicity | Can reduce clotting-factor synthesis and alter warfarin handling; clinical context matters. |
| Estrogens/OCPs | - | PDF says lower INR | Estrogen-containing therapy can alter thrombotic/bleeding balance; INR direction is not a universal rule. |
| Kidney disease | Albumin loss | PDF says lower INR | Hypoalbuminemia can change free warfarin and bleeding risk; INR response is not reliably predictable from albumin loss alone. |

### High-yield warfarin interaction principles

- **Rifampicin -> decreased warfarin effect.**
- Many CYP inhibitors and interacting antibiotics -> **increased INR**.
- Changes in vitamin K intake can alter INR.
- Antiplatelet drugs/NSAIDs may increase bleeding **without needing to raise INR**.
- Always interpret interaction in the clinical context rather than memorizing a one-direction table.

## 4.7 Management of raised INR / warfarin overdose

### Scenario 1 - raised INR without bleeding

**[PDF]**

| INR | PDF action |
|---|---|
| **3-10** | Stop warfarin; no other treatment in the handout |
| **>10** | Oral vitamin K **2.5-5 mg** |

**[Verified/Corrected]** Exact management depends on the actual INR, bleeding status, thrombotic risk and dose history. The handout's simplified thresholds are useful for exam revision but should not replace a contemporary reversal protocol.

### Scenario 2 - raised INR with clinically significant bleeding

**[PDF]**
- IV vitamin K.
- Immediate replacement of clotting factors:
  - **4-factor PCC** = preferred in the handout.
  - **FFP** if PCC unavailable.

### Why 4-factor PCC is important

It contains the vitamin-K-dependent coagulation factors:

- II
- VII
- IX
- X

**Key principle:** Vitamin K restores the body's own factor synthesis but **does not act instantly**; PCC provides immediate factor replacement.

# 6. PARENTERAL ANTICOAGULANTS

## 6.1 Direct thrombin inhibitors

### PDF classification

**[PDF]** Drugs ending in **"-rudin"**:
- Hirudin
- Lepirudin
- Desirudin
- Bivalirudin
- Argatroban (does not follow the suffix mnemonic)

### Administration/monitoring in the PDF

- IV administration.
- aPTT monitoring for the older parenteral direct thrombin inhibitor framework.
- Indication emphasized: use when **heparin cannot be given**, especially heparin allergy/HIT.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Current HIT management recommends discontinuing heparin and using a **non-heparin anticoagulant**. Options include argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, danaparoid and selected DOACs. Argatroban and bivalirudin are particularly useful when a short-acting parenteral agent is desired.

### HIT

> **HIT = heparin-induced thrombocytopenia**

HIT is a **prothrombotic** immune-mediated adverse drug reaction, usually involving antibodies to PF4-heparin complexes.

**[PDF]** The handout names hirudin/lepirudin/argatroban as drugs of choice for HIT and says there is no antidote for hirudin/lepirudin.

**[Verified/Corrected]** In current practice, the specific agent depends on availability, organ function, clinical stability and bleeding/procedure risk. **Argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux and selected DOACs** are accepted options in contemporary ASH guidance.

# 7. HEPARINS

## 7.1 Core mechanism

**[PDF]**
- Heparin activates **antithrombin (AT)**.
- Antithrombin inhibits thrombin and other activated clotting factors.
- Therefore heparin is described as an **indirect thrombin inhibitor**.

### Types of heparin

| Feature | UFH / high-MW heparin | LMWH | Synthetic pentasaccharide/ULMW group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | Unfractionated heparin | Enoxaparin, dalteparin, tinzaparin | Fondaparinux; idraparinux is historical/investigational context |
| Predominant activity | Anti-Xa + Anti-IIa | Anti-Xa > Anti-IIa | Anti-Xa only |
| Typical route | IV (also SC in some settings) | SC | SC |
| PDF source | Animal-derived heparin preparations | Animal-derived LMWHs | Synthetic |
| Monitoring | aPTT commonly used for therapeutic UFH | Routine aPTT generally not useful | No routine aPTT |
| HIT risk | Yes | Yes, lower than UFH | Very low/none compared with heparins |
| Clearance | RES plus other pathways | Predominantly renal | Renal |
| Antidote | Protamine | Protamine gives **partial** reversal | No fully established specific antidote |

**Terminology note:** Fondaparinux is a **synthetic selective indirect factor Xa inhibitor**, not simply a chemically smaller heparin molecule.

## 7.2 UFH vs LMWH - exam table, corrected

| Feature | UFH | LMWH |
|---|---|---|
| aPTT monitoring | **Required for therapeutic IV UFH** in the traditional protocol | **Not routinely required** |
| HIT | Risk present | Risk present, generally lower |
| Renal impairment | Often preferred when rapid reversibility/independence from renal clearance is important | Dose adjustment/caution required in renal impairment |

| Typical route | IV for therapeutic inpatient anticoagulation | SC |

### "Which heparin is not excreted in urine?"

**[PDF exam answer]** UFH/high-MW heparin.

### "Which heparin is safest in severe renal failure?"

**[PDF exam answer]** UFH/high-MW heparin.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The practical principle is sound: **UFH is often preferred when renal clearance is severely impaired** because it is less dependent on renal elimination and is rapidly reversible. This does not make it universally "renal safe" in every circumstance.

# 8. ANTICOAGULATION IN PREGNANCY

## 8.1 General principle

**[PDF]** Heparins are presented as the safest anticoagulants in pregnancy, with a preference for LMWH/ULMW preparations.

**[Verified/Corrected]** For **acute VTE in pregnancy, LMWH is preferred over UFH** by ASH guidance. DOACs are generally avoided in pregnancy because of inadequate safety data and concerns about fetal exposure.

## 8.2 Warfarin and fetal effects

**[PDF]**
- Warfarin crosses the placenta.
- Vitamin K is important in fetal bone/cartilage formation.
- First-trimester exposure can cause **fetal warfarin syndrome / chondrodysplasia punctata**.
- Features listed:
  - Saddle nose.
  - Limb hypoplasia.
  - Cranial defects/microcephaly.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Warfarin embryopathy is particularly associated with exposure during the critical early gestational period, but fetal/neonatal bleeding and central nervous system abnormalities can occur with exposure later in pregnancy as well. Management of pregnancy in a patient who requires chronic anticoagulation - especially a patient with a **mechanical valve** - is highly individualized and cannot be reduced to a single universal week-by-week schedule.

## 8.3 Pregnancy protocol shown in the PDF

| Time period | PDF drug | PDF notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0-12 weeks | Heparin injection only | Warfarin contraindicated |
| After 12 weeks | Warfarin can start/continue; heparin also listed | "Either option acceptable" |
| After 36 weeks | Stop warfarin; give heparin | Preparing for delivery |
| 1 day before delivery | Stop all anticoagulants | Prevent postpartum hemorrhage |
| Postpartum | Heparin | Heparin as drug of choice postpartum |

**[Verified/Corrected]** This table is a **source-derived exam algorithm, not a universal modern pregnancy protocol**. Actual anticoagulant selection in pregnancy depends heavily on the indication. Acute VTE is generally treated with LMWH. Mechanical-valve anticoagulation requires a separate specialist strategy because maternal valve thrombosis risk is substantial. Timing of interruption around delivery is individualized according to dose, renal function, neuraxial anesthesia plans and obstetric factors.

# 13. STATINS

## 13.1 Mechanism

**[PDF]**
- Statins competitively inhibit **HMG-CoA reductase** in the liver.
- HMG-CoA -> mevalonate is the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis.
- Reduced hepatic cholesterol synthesis -> increased hepatic LDL receptor expression -> increased LDL uptake from plasma -> lower blood LDL-C.

```text
HMG-CoA
   |
   | X  HMG-CoA reductase inhibited by statin
   v
Mevalonate
   |
   v
Hepatic cholesterol synthesis
   |
   v
Hepatic LDL receptors increase
   |
   v
More LDL cleared from plasma
   |
   v
Lower LDL-C
```

## 13.2 PDF indications/examples

The handout calls statins the drug of choice for hypercholesterolemia associated with:
- Metabolic syndrome/lifestyle-associated dyslipidemia.
- Nephrotic syndrome.
- Certain drugs.
- Alcohol use.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The contemporary indication is based on ASCVD risk, LDL-C level and specific conditions such as clinical ASCVD or familial hypercholesterolemia rather than a simple etiologic list.

## 13.3 Pleiotropic effects listed in the PDF

- Anti-atherosclerotic effect.
- Anti-inflammatory effects.
- Lowering of uric acid is mentioned as beneficial for gout.

**Clinical note:** These effects are secondary to the central LDL-lowering action; they should not be used to choose a statin over established lipid-management criteria.

### CYP metabolism

**[PDF]**
- Most statins: CYP3A4.
- Exceptions highlighted:
  - Pravastatin: not metabolized by CYP.
  - Rosuvastatin: not metabolized by CYP.
  - Fluvastatin: CYP2C9.
- Rosuvastatin + pravastatin are highlighted as having fewer CYP-mediated interactions.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The commonly tested broad pattern is:
- **CYP3A4:** simvastatin, lovastatin, atorvastatin.
- **CYP2C9:** fluvastatin.
- **Minimal CYP metabolism:** pravastatin.
- **Rosuvastatin:** limited CYP metabolism, including CYP2C9/2C19 contributions, but substantially different from the CYP3A4 statins.

## 13.5 Adverse effects

**[PDF]**
- Hepatotoxicity/transaminase elevation.
- **Myopathy**, which may progress to **rhabdomyolysis**.
- Rhabdomyolysis clues:
  - Increased **CK/CPK**.
  - Myoglobin release.
  - **Myoglobinuria -> cola-colored urine**.

### Pregnancy

**[PDF]** "Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy."

**[Verified/Corrected]** The FDA removed the former blanket pregnancy contraindication in 2021, but **most pregnant patients should still discontinue statins**, with continued use reserved for selected very-high-risk situations after individualized specialist assessment. The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline also generally recommends avoiding statin/nonstatin lipid-lowering therapy during pregnancy/lactation except when the clinical situation justifies an exception.

### Pediatrics

**[PDF]** "Children <12 years" are listed as absolutely contraindicated.

**[Verified/Corrected]** This is too broad. Pediatric statin use is **agent-, age-, diagnosis- and risk-specific**, with established indications in selected children/adolescents with familial hypercholesterolemia. There is no universal rule that all statins are absolutely contraindicated in every child under 12 years.

**OCR/visual correction:** The PDF OCR renders the macrophage receptor as **"FYC"**. The biologically correct terminology is **Fc gamma receptor (Fc-gamma receptor; FcgammaR)**.

## 25.2 Treatment concepts

### PDF treatment hierarchy

1. **Corticosteroids** - reduce immune-mediated platelet destruction/autoantibody production.
2. **Rituximab** - used for steroid-resistant disease.
3. **Fostamatinib** - inhibits a tyrosine kinase pathway in splenic macrophages.
4. **Romiplostim / eltrombopag** - increase platelet production.
5. **IVIG** - listed as the "best and most effective" treatment for refractory ITP.
6. **Plasmapheresis** - severe cases in the handout.
7. **Splenectomy** - when medical therapy fails.

### Current 2026 ITP update

**[Verified/Corrected]** The ASH 2026 focused guideline for adults with primary ITP updates the older steroid-only hierarchy. It conditionally recommends **initial corticosteroids combined with either rituximab or a thrombopoietic agent** rather than corticosteroids alone in relevant adults, and after failure of first-line steroids it strongly recommends a **thrombopoietic agent** as one preferred second-line option and conditionally recommends rituximab as another.

### IVIG

**[Verified/Corrected]** IVIG can produce a **rapid temporary increase in platelet count** and is important when a rapid response is needed, but it should not be memorized as a universal "most effective" treatment for every ITP patient.

### Plasmapheresis

**[Verified/Corrected]** Plasma exchange is **not routine treatment for typical primary ITP**. Consider it only in unusual life-threatening refractory scenarios under specialist management.

### Splenectomy

**[Verified/Corrected]** Splenectomy remains an option for selected chronic/refractory ITP but is generally a later-line strategy after medical therapies, with timing individualized.

**[Verified/Corrected]** The target is **spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK)**. Fostamatinib inhibits SYK signaling involved in Fc-receptor-mediated platelet phagocytosis.

# 26. CHELATORS FOR HEAVY-METAL POISONING

## 26.1 General principle

Chelators bind metal ions to form complexes that can be eliminated from the body. **Chelation is not interchangeable across metals**; the correct agent depends on the toxic metal, severity, organ function and timing.

---

## 26.2 Dimercaprol (BAL; British Anti-Lewisite)

### PDF mnemonic: "BAM BAM"

**[PDF]**
- **B** = Bismuth
- **A** = Arsenic
- **M** = Mercury

### PDF contraindications

- Cadmium poisoning.
- Iron poisoning.
- Reason: toxic complexes can form.

**[Verified/Corrected]** Dimercaprol is a recognized chelator for **acute arsenic poisoning** and has uses in selected heavy-metal intoxications. The ATSDR specifically discusses dimercaprol in acute arsenic exposure and notes the need for specialist toxicology input. It should not be used indiscriminately for all metals.

**Important:** Iron poisoning is not treated with dimercaprol.

---

## 26.3 Calcium disodium EDTA (CaNa2EDTA)

**[PDF]** Mnemonic "MLC":
- M = manganese
- L = lead
- C = cadmium

**[Verified/Corrected]** The key clinical indication to remember is **lead poisoning**, where calcium disodium EDTA may be used in appropriate severe cases. The PDF's blanket manganese/cadmium "drug of choice" wording is not reliable and should not be used as a current clinical rule.

**Critical nomenclature point:** **Calcium disodium EDTA (CaNa2EDTA)** is the appropriate EDTA formulation for lead poisoning. Disodium EDTA is not interchangeable with it and can cause dangerous hypocalcemia.

---

## 26.4 Penicillamine

**[PDF]**
- Chelator for **copper**.
- Classic association: **Wilson disease**.

This is a useful exam association, although modern Wilson disease treatment can also involve zinc and other chelation strategies depending on the clinical situation.

---

## 26.5 Iron chelators

**[PDF]**
- **Deferoxamine**
- **Deferiprone**
- Both contain "FE" in their names.
- Used for iron poisoning/iron overload, with thalassemia and repeated transfusions mentioned.

**[Verified/Corrected]**
- **Deferoxamine** is a major chelator for **acute iron poisoning** and for chronic iron overload in selected patients.
- **Deferiprone** is an oral iron chelator primarily used for **chronic transfusional iron overload**, not the routine first-line antidote for acute iron poisoning.
- **Deferasirox** is another important oral chelator for chronic iron overload.

### Thalassemia

Repeated transfusions can cause **secondary iron overload**, which is an important indication for chronic chelation therapy.

**British Society for Haematology / ISTH contemporary DIC guidance and reviews.** Used for the principle of treating the underlying cause and reserving therapeutic heparin for selected thrombotic-predominant situations.

**2026 Guideline for the Early Management of Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke.** American Heart Association/American Stroke Association, Stroke, 2026.
Key point used: eligible patients within 4.5 hours may receive alteplase 0.9 mg/kg or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg (max 25 mg), with selected extended-window strategies.

**2026 ACC/AHA/AACVPR/ABC/ACPM/ADA/AGS/APhA/ASPC/NLA/PCNA Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia.** Circulation, 2026.
Key points used: statins remain foundational; nonstatins include ezetimibe, bempedoic acid and PCSK9-directed therapy; severe hypertriglyceridemia may warrant fibrates/prescription omega-3; niacin is generally avoided for routine use.

**FDA Drug Safety Communication (2021): removal of strongest warning against statin use during pregnancy.**

Key points used: ferrous sulfate is preferred for cost, once-daily oral dosing at most, every-other-day dosing may be better tolerated, add vitamin C, use IV iron when oral therapy is not tolerated/absorbed/effective, and true anaphylaxis is very rare.
