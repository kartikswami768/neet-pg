# 2. Important Verification Summary

The original PDF is a high-yield exam document, but several statements are deliberately simplified or are no longer fully aligned with contemporary clinical guidance. The following points were specifically checked against current major guidelines and drug labels:

| PDF statement/theme | Handling in this reconstruction |
|---|---|
| Mannitol as a treatment of lithium toxicity | **Corrected:** mannitol is not standard first-line treatment for lithium poisoning; severe poisoning may require hemodialysis |
| Loop diuretic as generic “DOC for hypertensive crisis” | **Qualified:** loop diuretics are primarily for volume overload; IV loop diuresis may be part of management when congestion is present |
| “Only metolazone is safe” at low GFR | **Corrected:** thiazide-like agents retain clinical utility at reduced GFR; no blanket “only safe thiazide” rule |
| Thiazides/loops contraindicated in diabetes, gout, dyslipidemia | **Corrected:** these are adverse-effect/caution issues, not blanket contraindications |
| ACEI/ARB always first drug in every CKD patient | **Corrected:** use depends on CKD etiology/albuminuria and comorbidities; SGLT2 inhibitors and nonsteroidal MRAs have important roles |
| Losartan has an antiplatelet effect by blocking thromboxane A2 receptors | **Not retained as a verified pharmacologic property** |
| “MAD enzyme” exists only in veins | **Corrected:** ALDH2 is involved in nitrate bioactivation; venodilation predominates at lower nitrate doses |
| 8-hour nitrate-free interval as universal rule | **Qualified:** transdermal nitroglycerin labeling commonly uses a nitrate-free interval of about 10-12 hours |
| “Hypertensive urgency” with routine rapid lowering | **Updated:** contemporary guidelines distinguish severe hypertension without acute target-organ injury from hypertensive emergency and do not recommend reflex rapid IV lowering without acute injury |
| MONAS as complete ACS treatment | **Updated:** retained as historical PDF mnemonic, but current ACS care emphasizes aspirin + P2Y12 inhibition + anticoagulation when indicated + high-intensity statin + reperfusion strategy; oxygen/morphine are selective rather than routine |
| “Four cardioprotective beta blockers” in HFrEF | **Corrected:** the strongest guideline-supported mortality data are for carvedilol, sustained-release metoprolol succinate, and bisoprolol |
| Nesiritide as routine HF therapy | **Qualified:** not a core modern guideline-directed therapy |
| Renal failure as absolute digoxin contraindication | **Corrected:** renal impairment requires lower dosing and monitoring; it is not an absolute contraindication |
| Digoxin target 0.6-2.0 ng/mL as a universal therapeutic range | **Updated:** lower serum concentrations are generally preferred; toxicity rises above about 2 ng/mL and may occur even below that threshold |
| DC shock “absolutely contraindicated” in digoxin-associated VF | **Corrected:** life-threatening unstable ventricular arrhythmias still require appropriate electrical therapy; digoxin-specific Fab is critical when indicated |
| Class IB “opens K+ channels” | **Corrected:** class IB drugs are Na+ channel blockers; they generally shorten or have little effect on ventricular APD |
| Sotalol as exclusively class III | **Qualified:** sotalol has both beta-blocking (class II) and potassium-channel-blocking (class III) actions |
| Vernakalant as a ventricular antiarrhythmic/VF drug | **Corrected:** it is principally an atrial-selective agent used for pharmacological cardioversion of recent AF in jurisdictions where approved |
| Adenosine/atropine listed without modern acute algorithm | **Updated** using current resuscitation guidance |
| Magnesium for all polymorphic VT | **Qualified:** magnesium is most relevant to recurrent torsades associated with prolonged QT; sustained polymorphic VT requires immediate unsynchronized shock |

# 3. RENAL: DIURETICS

## 3.1 General Definition

**[PDF]**

Diuretics are drugs that increase excretion of sodium and/or water in urine.

The PDF organizes the major diuretics by nephron site:

1. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
2. Osmotic diuretics
3. Loop diuretics
4. Thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics
5. Potassium-sparing diuretics

---

## 3.2 Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors

**PDF location:** page 1 (printed p. 94)

### Mechanism of action

**[PDF]**

- Act at the **proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)**.
- Inhibit **carbonic anhydrase**.
- Reduce bicarbonate reabsorption.
- Increase urinary loss of:
  - Na+
  - HCO3-
  - water
- The PDF describes these agents as **noncompetitive and reversible inhibitors** of carbonic anhydrase.
- They are **sulfonamide derivatives**.

### Drugs

- **Acetazolamide**
- **Methazolamide**
- **Dichlorphenamide**

### Clinical uses

**[PDF mnemonic: GAME Plan]**

#### G - Glaucoma

- Reduce aqueous humor production.
- Lower intraocular pressure.

#### A - Alkalinization of urine

- Increased urinary bicarbonate causes urine alkalinization.
- PDF teaching point:
  - Useful for increasing urinary excretion of some weak acids.
  - Mentioned with **uric acid and cystine stones**.
- The PDF also notes an increased risk of **calcium phosphate stones**.

#### M - Metabolic acidosis

- Bicarbonate loss can produce a metabolic acidosis.
- The PDF connects increased H+ concentration with respiratory-center stimulation.

#### E - Epilepsy

- Acetazolamide can be used as an adjunct in selected seizure disorders.
- **PDF mnemonic continuation:** “Plan: Periodic muscle paralysis.”
- The PDF states that acetazolamide may be used in **hyperkalemic or hypokalemic periodic paralysis**.

#### Mountain sickness

- **Acetazolamide** is a standard preventive treatment for acute mountain sickness.
- It promotes renal bicarbonate loss and produces a mild metabolic acidosis that stimulates ventilation.

### Adverse effects

**[PDF mnemonic: BASE]**

- **B - Bone marrow suppression**
  - Rare but important sulfonamide-related hematologic toxicity.
- **A - Ammonia elevation**
  - The PDF specifically warns about use in liver disease because impaired ammonia handling may aggravate encephalopathy.
- **S - Stones**
  - Calcium phosphate nephrolithiasis.
- **E - Electrolyte/metabolic effects**
  - Hypokalemia
  - Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis

### Verification note

**[Verified correction/update]**

- The key clinical use in altitude illness is well established.
- Acetazolamide can worsen metabolic acidosis and may contribute to nephrolithiasis.
- The wording “contraindicated in liver disease” is too absolute for every clinical context; severe hepatic disease with risk of encephalopathy is a major caution because acetazolamide can reduce urinary acid excretion and may alter ammonia handling.
- “Bone marrow suppression” is uncommon, not the typical adverse effect.

## 3.3 Osmotic Diuretic: Mannitol

**PDF location:** page 2 (printed p. 95)

### Drug

- **Mannitol**
- PDF presentation: IV **20% osmolar solution**.

### Mechanism

**[PDF]**

- Raises plasma osmolality.
- Pulls water from tissues into the intravascular space.
- Reduces:
  - CSF production -> lowers ICP
  - Aqueous humor production -> lowers IOP

### Clinical uses

#### Cerebral edema / raised intracranial pressure

- **[PDF] Drug of choice** in the teaching note.
- Decreases CSF volume and intracranial pressure.

#### Acute glaucoma

- Reduces intraocular pressure by osmotic dehydration of the eye.

#### Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome

- The PDF describes maintenance of plasma osmolality to reduce the risk of cerebral edema.

### Contraindications / major cautions

**[PDF]**

- Heart failure
  - Increased intravascular volume can worsen preload/congestion.
- Renal failure/anuria
  - Inability to excrete the osmotic load can worsen volume status.

### PDF-specific statement requiring correction

The PDF states:

- “Mannitol excretes metals”
- “Lithium toxicity (treatment of choice)”
- “Cisplatin toxicity”

**[Verified correction/update]**

Do **not** use this as a current lithium-poisoning algorithm.

- Mannitol is not standard first-line treatment for lithium toxicity.
- Initial management generally involves stopping lithium and appropriate fluid/electrolyte management.
- **Hemodialysis** is the definitive extracorporeal therapy for severe lithium poisoning when clinical criteria are met.
- “Forced diuresis” has no routine modern role comparable to hemodialysis.
- Mannitol is not a general routine treatment for cisplatin nephrotoxicity.

### Nephron localization correction

**[Verified correction/update]**

The PDF labels osmotic diuretics as acting at the loop of Henle. The pharmacology is more accurately described as osmotic diuresis occurring predominantly in the **proximal tubule and thin descending limb**, with effects dependent on the filtered osmole and water permeability.

# 3.4 Loop Diuretics

**PDF location:** page 2 (printed p. 95)

### Site of action

**[PDF]**

- **Thick ascending limb (TAL) of the loop of Henle**

### Mechanism

**[PDF]**

1. Inhibit the **Na+-K+-2Cl- (NKCC2) cotransporter** in the TAL.
2. Increase urinary NaCl and water excretion.
3. The PDF additionally emphasizes increased prostaglandin synthesis and renal vasodilation/blood flow.

### Major characteristics

- **Most effective / high-ceiling diuretics.**

### Drugs

#### Mercury-containing

Historical only:

- **Mersalyl**
  - Very ototoxic.
  - PDF notes permanent deafness.

#### Sulfonamide-containing

- **Furosemide**
- **Torsemide**
- **Bumetanide**

PDF pearls:

- Torsemide: longer acting than furosemide.
- Bumetanide: more potent per mg.
- Ototoxicity can occur.
- Sulfonamide allergy is a consideration.

#### Non-mercury, non-sulfonamide

- **Ethacrynic acid**

This is the main loop option classically used when a non-sulfonamide loop is desired.

### Clinical uses

**[PDF]**

- Pulmonary edema - PDF calls this **drug of choice**.
- Severe fluid overload.
- Hypertensive crisis - PDF lists this, but the modern qualification is important.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Loop diuretics are especially useful when hypertension coexists with significant volume overload or renal dysfunction, but they are **not a generic first-line drug for all hypertensive emergencies**. Blood-pressure control in emergency depends on the specific acute target-organ injury.

### NSAID interaction

**[PDF]**

**Aspirin + loop diuretic**

- Aspirin is an NSAID.
- NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis.
- Reduced renal prostaglandins can reduce renal blood flow and blunt the natriuretic response.

**[Verified correction/update]**

This interaction is pharmacologically plausible and clinically relevant, especially with high-dose NSAIDs and in patients at risk of kidney injury or sodium retention.

# 3.5 Thiazide and Thiazide-like Diuretics

**PDF location:** page 3 (printed p. 96)

### Site

**[PDF]**

- **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)**

### Mechanism

- Inhibit the **Na+-Cl- symporter (NCC)**.

### Drugs

#### Simple thiazides

- **Chlorothiazide**
- **Hydrochlorothiazide**

#### Thiazide-like drugs

- **Chlorthalidone**
  - PDF: “most potent thiazide-like drug.”
- **Metolazone**
  - PDF: highlighted for reduced renal function.
- **Indapamide**

### Clinical uses - PDF mnemonic HORD

#### H - Hypertension

- The PDF calls thiazides **DOC** for hypertension.
- The note contrasts this with loop diuretics for hypertensive crisis.

#### O - Osteoporosis

- Reduced urinary Ca2+ loss may help preserve bone mineral density.

#### R - Renal stones

- Particularly **calcium stones** associated with hypercalciuria.

#### D - Diabetes insipidus

- Thiazides paradoxically reduce urine volume in **nephrogenic DI**.

### Low-GFR statement

The PDF says:

> “Only thiazide safe in renal failure, when GFR <45” for metolazone.

**[Verified correction/update]**

This wording should **not** be memorized as an absolute rule.

- Thiazide-like agents, especially **metolazone and chlorthalidone**, can retain antihypertensive and/or diuretic effects at reduced GFR.
- The choice depends on the clinical objective, kidney function, volume status, and electrolyte risk.
- There is no contemporary universal statement that metolazone is the “only thiazide safe” below a specific GFR.

# 3.6 Adverse Effects of Loop + Thiazide Diuretics

**PDF location:** page 3 (printed p. 96)

The PDF lists:

- Hyponatremia
- Hypotension
- Hypokalemia
  - Increased risk of **digoxin toxicity**
- Hypomagnesemia
- Hyperglycemia
- Hyperlipidemia
- Hyperuricemia

### PDF contraindication statement

The PDF says loop/thiazide diuretics are contraindicated in:

- Diabetes mellitus
- Gout
- Dyslipidemia

**[Verified correction/update]**

These should **not** be treated as blanket contraindications.

They are clinically relevant adverse-effect/caution domains:

- Thiazides can worsen glycemia.
- Thiazides can raise uric acid and precipitate gout in susceptible patients.
- Some diuretics can worsen metabolic lipid parameters.
- The decision is individualized rather than an automatic exclusion.

**PDF location:** page 4 (printed p. 97)

| Blood Ca2+ | Can lower serum calcium | Can increase serum calcium |
| Classic clinical use | PDF: **DOC for hypercalcemia** | PDF: **DOC for hypercalciuria** |
| Renal stone point | Less useful for prevention of calcium stones | Useful in recurrent calcium stones with hypercalciuria |

**PDF note:**
- The PDF warns about calcium phosphate stone risk with calcium-retaining effects, but the clinical context is more nuanced.

# 3.8 Potassium-Sparing Diuretics

**PDF location:** pages 4-5 (printed pp. 97-98)

### Site

- **Collecting duct**

### Basic mechanism

**[PDF]**

- Reduce Na+ reabsorption in the collecting duct.
- Reduce K+ secretion.
- Therefore they are **potassium-sparing**.

### Main types

| Type | Drugs | Site/target |
|---|---|---|
| Aldosterone receptor antagonists / MRAs | Spironolactone, Eplerenone, Finerenone | Mineralocorticoid receptor |
| ENaC inhibitors | Amiloride, Triamterene | Epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) |

### Important exam distinction

**[PDF]**

> Potassium-sparing diuretics are the major diuretic class associated with **hyperkalemia**.

This remains a useful exam pearl.

---

## 3.8.1 Aldosterone physiology

**[PDF]**

Aldosterone binds its intracellular receptor in principal cells of the collecting duct and promotes:

- ENaC expression/activity
- Na+ reabsorption
- K+ secretion

Therefore, an aldosterone antagonist causes:

- Less Na+ reabsorption
- Less K+ secretion
- More K+ retention

### Topology correction

**[Verified correction/update]**

It is more precise to describe the **mineralocorticoid receptor** as an intracellular receptor rather than saying the drug acts literally “from the basolateral side.” ENaC is the apical/luminal channel that is directly targeted by amiloride/triamterene.

---

## 3.8.2 Spironolactone

### Pharmacology

- Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist.
- Active metabolite: **canrenone**.

### Adverse effects / special features

- **Gynecomastia**
- Sexual adverse effects
- Hyperkalemia
- Antiandrogenic effects

### Clinical uses

**[PDF]**

- Chronic heart failure
- Cirrhotic ascites
- Hyperaldosteronism
- Hirsutism/PCOS in selected patients

### Verification

Spironolactone is a guideline-supported MRA in appropriate HFrEF patients and is also widely used in resistant hypertension and hyperaldosteronism.

---

## 3.8.3 Eplerenone

- More selective mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist.
- Less antiandrogenic effect than spironolactone.
- Therefore:
  - **Lower risk of gynecomastia**
  - Sexual adverse effects may be less prominent.

**[PDF]**

The PDF emphasizes lower gynecomastia risk and lower hyperkalemia risk than spironolactone.

**[Verified correction/update]**

The hyperkalemia risk is **not absent** and is still clinically important with eplerenone.

---

## 3.8.4 Finerenone

**[PDF]**

- Newer MRA.
- Cardioprotective.
- Nephroprotective.
- Used in diabetic nephropathy.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Finerenone is a **nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist** with important evidence in **type 2 diabetes + CKD**, especially in patients with albuminuria, to reduce cardiorenal outcomes when used appropriately. It is not simply another high-volume “diuretic.”

Current CKD guidance places finerenone among important kidney/cardiovascular risk-reducing therapies in appropriately selected patients.

---

## 3.8.5 ENaC inhibitors

### Amiloride

**[PDF]**

- Acts from the **luminal side**.
- Blocks ENaC.

### Uses

#### Liddle syndrome

- Genetic gain-of-function/excess ENaC activity.
- **Amiloride** blocks ENaC.

#### Lithium-induced nephrogenic DI

- Amiloride can reduce lithium entry into collecting-duct principal cells through ENaC.
- This can improve lithium-induced nephrogenic DI.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Amiloride is particularly useful in lithium-induced nephrogenic DI because of this mechanism and is often favored over simply escalating thiazide therapy.

### Triamterene

- ENaC inhibitor.
- Potassium-sparing.

## 4.1 Vasopressin Agonists

### 4.1.1 Natural arginine vasopressin

**[PDF]**

- “Presses” blood vessels.
- Administration: injection.
- Major outcome: vasoconstriction and increased blood pressure.
- PDF indication:
  - Resistant shock, including shock resistant to epinephrine/norepinephrine.

**[Verified correction/update]**

## 4.1.2 Terlipressin

**[PDF]**

- V1 agonist.
- Injection.
- Vasoconstriction.
- Listed uses:
  - Bleeding esophageal varices
  - Hepatorenal syndrome

**[Verified correction/update]**

Terlipressin is a vasopressin analogue with strong V1a-mediated vasoconstrictor activity and has established use in selected patients with variceal bleeding and **hepatorenal syndrome / HRS-AKI**, depending on jurisdiction and protocol.

## 4.1.3 Desmopressin (DDAVP)

**[PDF]**

- Primarily **V2 agonist**.
- Oral or intranasal administration.
- Major uses:
  - Neurogenic/central DI
  - Nocturnal enuresis
  - Hemophilia A in selected patients
- V2 stimulation increases release of **factor VIII**.

### Additional verified point

Desmopressin also increases **von Willebrand factor** and is used in selected patients with type 1 von Willebrand disease and other appropriate bleeding disorders.

# 4.2 Vasopressin Antagonists - Vaptans

**PDF location:** page 6 (printed p. 99)

### Mechanism

- Block ADH/vasopressin receptors.
- Promote free-water excretion (**aquaresis**).

### Drugs

- **Conivaptan**
  - IV
  - V1a/V2 antagonist
- **Tolvaptan**
  - Oral
  - Selective V2 antagonist

### Main indication in the PDF

- **SIADH**

### SIADH pathophysiology

Excess ADH -> increased water reabsorption -> dilutional hyponatremia.

PDF-listed causes:

- Head injury
- Brain tumors
- Heart failure
- ADPKD

**[Verified correction/update]**

The list is not exhaustive. SIADH can occur with pulmonary disease, malignancy, drugs, CNS disease, postoperative states, and many other conditions.

### Demeclocycline

**[PDF]**

- Tetracycline antibiotic.
- Used in SIADH because it reduces renal responsiveness to ADH.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Demeclocycline is no longer a routine first-line SIADH treatment in many modern practices because of nephrotoxicity and availability/preferences for other strategies. It is best remembered as an older pharmacologic option.

# 4.3 Nocturnal Enuresis

**PDF location:** page 6 (printed p. 99)

### Age

**[PDF]**

- Treatment generally considered when the child is **>6 years** and bedwetting remains clinically significant.

### First-line non-drug management

- Education/training.
- Restrict excessive fluid intake immediately before sleep.
- **Alarm therapy**
  - Alarm triggers when urine is passed.
  - Trains arousal to bladder filling.

### Pharmacotherapy

#### Desmopressin

- PDF: **DOC**
- Decreases nocturnal urine production.
- Nasal route mentioned for small children.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Intranasal desmopressin is not generally preferred for routine enuresis because of safety concerns; oral formulations are more commonly used, with careful fluid restriction to reduce hyponatremia risk.

#### Oxybutynin

- Anticholinergic.
- Relaxes bladder detrusor.
- Usually considered in selected children with bladder dysfunction and not as the universal first pharmacologic choice.

#### Imipramine

- Tricyclic antidepressant.
- Anticholinergic effect.
- Can relax the bladder.
- Its adverse-effect profile and overdose toxicity limit routine use.

# 4.4 Diabetes Insipidus (DI)

**PDF location:** page 6 (printed p. 99)

### Basic pathophysiology

Deficient ADH effect -> reduced water reabsorption -> increased free-water loss.

Clinical pattern:

- Polyuria
- Dilute/low-osmolality urine

---

## 4.4.1 Central (Neurogenic) DI

### Cause

- Deficient ADH production/release from hypothalamic-neurohypophyseal pathways.

### Drug of choice

- **Desmopressin**

### Other PDF-listed drugs

- Carbamazepine
- Chlorpropamide

These may increase endogenous ADH effect/secretion in selected patients, but are not first-line modern replacements for desmopressin.

---

## 4.4.2 Nephrogenic DI

### Cause

- Kidney is resistant to ADH.

### PDF treatment

- Thiazide diuretics
- NSAIDs (e.g., indomethacin)
- Amiloride

### Mechanistic idea

Thiazide-induced mild extracellular volume contraction -> increased proximal sodium/water reabsorption -> less fluid reaches the collecting duct -> lower final urine volume.

### Lithium-induced DI

**[PDF]**

The note says thiazides are contraindicated because of lithium toxicity.

**[Verified correction/update]**

The issue is more nuanced:

- Thiazides can **increase lithium serum concentration** and therefore require substantial caution, lithium dose adjustment, and close monitoring.
- **Amiloride** is particularly useful in lithium-induced nephrogenic DI because it limits lithium entry through ENaC.
- Thiazides are not simply an absolute contraindication in every patient; they are a medication-interaction problem requiring careful management.

## 5.1 RAAS Pathway

**[PDF]**

Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system:

```text
Angiotensinogen
      |
      | renin (kidney JG cells)
      v
Angiotensin I
      |
      | ACE
      v
Angiotensin II
      |
      | AT1 receptor
      v
Vasoconstriction
+
Aldosterone release
+
Na+ retention
+
K+ loss
      |
      v
Increased BP
```

### Drug classes

1. **Direct renin inhibitor**
   - Aliskiren
2. **ACE inhibitors**
   - names end in **-pril**
3. **Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)**
   - names end in **-sartan**

### Pharmacologic effect of RAAS inhibition

- Arterial vasodilation.
- Reduced aldosterone.
- Reduced sodium retention.
- Potassium retention.
- Lower blood pressure.

---

## 5.2 ACEI vs ARB: Adverse Effects and Contraindications

### Pregnancy

**[PDF]**

- ACEIs and ARBs are contraindicated in pregnancy because of fetal renal toxicity and related adverse fetal outcomes.

**[Verified correction/update]**

This remains a major contraindication. Current hypertension-in-pregnancy guidance also excludes other RAAS-modifying drugs in pregnancy.

### Bilateral renal artery stenosis

**[PDF]**

- GFR may already depend on angiotensin II-mediated efferent arteriolar constriction.
- RAAS blockade can sharply reduce intraglomerular pressure.
- AKI/oliguria can result.

**[Verified correction/update]**

This is a classic high-risk situation, not an instruction to label every renal artery stenosis as an absolute contraindication. The degree of stenosis, kidney function, indication for therapy, and monitoring matter.

### Hyperkalemia

- ACEI/ARB can increase serum K+.
- Risk is higher with:
  - CKD
  - MRA therapy
  - potassium supplements
  - other potassium-raising drugs.

### ACEI-specific adverse effects

ACE inhibition reduces bradykinin breakdown.

Therefore:

- **Dry cough** - common
- **Angioedema** - uncommon but potentially life-threatening

Management of significant ACEI-associated angioedema:

- Stop ACE inhibitor.
- Treat the airway/emergency syndrome appropriately.
- ARB use requires individualized consideration.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Do not list **aliskiren** as the routine automatic replacement for ACEI angioedema.

# 5.3 Nephroprotective Drugs in CKD

**PDF location:** page 8 (printed p. 101)

### PDF mnemonic: SAF

- **S - SGLT2 inhibitors**
- **A - ACE inhibitor / ARB**

# 6. CALCIUM CHANNEL BLOCKERS (CCBs)

**PDF location:** page 8 onward (printed pp. 101-102)

## Mechanism

Block **L-type calcium channels** in vascular smooth muscle and/or myocardium.

---

## 6.1 Classification

| Class | Examples | Major action |
|---|---|---|
| Dihydropyridines (DHP) | Amlodipine, Nifedipine, Nicardipine, Clevidipine, Nimodipine | Predominantly vascular |
| Non-DHP | Verapamil, Diltiazem | Cardiac conduction + vascular smooth muscle |

### DHPs

- Primarily arterial dilators.
- Lower systemic vascular resistance.
- Lower BP.

### Non-DHPs

- Slow SA-node automaticity.
- Slow AV-node conduction.
- Reduce heart rate.
- Also produce some vasodilation.

---

## 6.2 Clinical Applications

### Verapamil

**[PDF]**

- Used for supraventricular tachycardias.

**[Verified correction/update]**

For an acute **regular narrow-complex tachycardia**, vagal maneuvers and adenosine are commonly first steps; IV diltiazem/verapamil are options in selected stable patients.

### Diltiazem

**[PDF]**

- Angina
- SVT

The PDF emphasizes reduced heart rate and oxygen demand.

### DHP CCBs

- Common first-line BP-lowering agents.
- Amlodipine has a long oral duration.

### Nicardipine

**[PDF]**

- IV drug for severe hypertension.
- PDF calls it DOC for hypertensive emergency.

**[Verified correction/update]**

IV nicardipine is a widely used titratable agent for hypertensive emergency, but the choice should match the organ injury (e.g., aortic dissection, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary edema, stroke) and local protocol.

### Nimodipine

**[PDF]**

- Crosses the BBB.
- Used in subarachnoid hemorrhage.
- PDF says it prevents cerebral vasospasm.

**[Verified correction/update]**

In aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, nimodipine is used to reduce the risk of **delayed cerebral ischemia and poor neurologic outcome**. It is not best described simply as a generic “vasospasm-prevention” drug.

### Non-DHP

- Bradycardia
- AV block
- Negative inotropy

### DHP

- Reflex tachycardia (more prominent with short-acting agents)
- Peripheral edema, especially with amlodipine
- Flushing
- Headache

**[PDF]**

**Reason:**

- Excessive AV-nodal suppression

- AV block

## 9.1 Essential Hypertension

The PDF lists first-line classes:

This broad class structure remains consistent with contemporary major hypertension guidance.

### Combination therapy

Current practice often uses combination therapy when BP is substantially above goal, frequently with:

- ACEI/ARB + thiazide-type diuretic
- triple therapy with all three classes when necessary

## 9.2 Resistant Hypertension

**[PDF]**

- BP not controlled on 3 drugs in combination.
- DOC:
  - Spironolactone or eplerenone.
- Newer drug:
  - Aprocitentan.

**[Verified correction/update]**

The **2025 AHA/ACC hypertension guideline** defines resistant hypertension as BP above goal despite **3 complementary antihypertensive agents at maximally tolerated doses, one being a diuretic**, or BP controlled only with **4 or more drugs**.

- Spironolactone is a preferred fourth-line option when appropriate.
- Eplerenone is an alternative MRA.
- Aprocitentan, a dual endothelin-receptor antagonist, is a newer option for resistant hypertension.

Important safety issues for MRAs include:

- Hyperkalemia
- Reduced kidney function

### Important modern qualifications

#### BPH

**[Verified correction/update]**

Alpha-1 blockers can improve BPH symptoms, but **prazosin is not a standard universal first-line antihypertensive drug**. Hypertension should usually be treated according to cardiovascular/renal indications rather than selecting an alpha blocker solely because BPH is present.

#### Beta blockers

Beta blockers are not universal first-line antihypertensives. They are especially useful when there is another compelling indication.

#### Asthma

- Nonselective beta blockers can worsen bronchospasm.
- Cardioselective agents may sometimes be used cautiously when strongly indicated, but the PDF's exam rule is to avoid beta blockade in asthma when alternatives exist.

#### Pregnancy

**[Verified correction/update]**

ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibition are contraindicated.

Commonly used pregnancy-compatible agents include:

- Labetalol
- Nifedipine
- Methyldopa

Hydralazine is used in selected acute severe-hypertension settings.

# 9.4 Hypertensive Crisis

The PDF uses:

- BP >180/120 mmHg

### “Urgency” vs emergency

**[PDF]**

- Urgency: no organ damage.
- Emergency: end-organ damage such as:
  - hemorrhage
  - stroke
  - aortic dissection

**[Verified correction/update]**

Contemporary guidance increasingly avoids treating “hypertensive urgency” as a mandate for rapid pharmacologic BP lowering. The critical distinction is:

- **Severe hypertension without acute target-organ injury**
- **Hypertensive emergency with acute target-organ injury**

In hypertensive emergency, IV titratable therapy and controlled reduction are used according to the injured organ.

**[Verified correction/update]**

**[Verified correction/update]**

- **Metoprolol succinate (extended release)**
- **Bisoprolol**

Nebivolol has supportive evidence in selected older patients in some populations, but it should not be substituted for the three core HFrEF evidence-based agents in a high-yield “three drugs” question.

# 16. DIGOXIN

**PDF location:** pages 15-16 (printed pp. 108-109)

## 16.1 Source and class

**[PDF]**

- Source: **Digitalis** plant / foxglove.
- Cardiac glycoside.
- Digoxin is the clinically used member emphasized in the PDF.
- Other historical plant glycosides:
  - Digitoxin
  - Ouabain

---

## 16.2 Mechanism

**Primary mechanism**

- Inhibits **Na+/K+-ATPase** in cardiac myocytes.
- Increased intracellular Na+ reduces Na+/Ca2+ exchanger-mediated Ca2+ extrusion.
- Intracellular Ca2+ rises.
- Increased SR Ca2+ availability -> **positive inotropy**.

### AV-node effect

- Increased vagal tone.
- Slows:
  - SA-node rate
  - AV-node conduction

Therefore:

- **Negative chronotropy**
- **Negative dromotropy**

---

## 16.3 Clinical uses

**[PDF]**

- Chronic heart failure
- Atrial flutter/fibrillation / SVT

**[Verified correction/update]**

Digoxin is not a generic first-line drug for every SVT.

Contemporary roles include:

- Selected patients with **HFrEF** who remain symptomatic despite guideline-directed therapy.
- **Rate control in atrial fibrillation**, particularly when other rate-control agents are unsuitable or when HF limits choices.
- It does not replace catheter ablation or other rhythm-control strategies when those are indicated.

---

# 16.4 Digoxin Adverse Effects

### Gastrointestinal

- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Appetite loss

### Visual

- Yellow vision / **xanthopsia**
- Other color-vision disturbances

PDF comparison:

- Sildenafil -> blue-green color disturbance [not a routine toxicity pearl]
- Ethambutol -> red-green color disturbance

### Arrhythmias

Digoxin toxicity may produce almost any arrhythmia.

PDF examples:

- Ventricular bigeminy
- Ventricular fibrillation

### Gynecomastia / hormonal effects

The PDF associates gynecomastia with several drugs, including:

- Digoxin
- Isoniazid
- Spironolactone
- Cimetidine
- Ketoconazole
- Omeprazole

**[Verified correction/update]**

Gynecomastia is classically associated with **spironolactone**. It is not a defining major toxicity of digoxin.

# 16.5 Digoxin Pharmacokinetics

### Narrow therapeutic window

**[PDF]**

- Requires therapeutic drug monitoring.

**PDF range:** 0.6-2.0 ng/mL.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Do **not** memorize 0.6-2.0 ng/mL as a universal desired range.

Current labeling and guideline-era practice emphasize:

- Concentrations >2 ng/mL are associated with substantially greater toxicity without clear benefit.
- Lower concentrations are often targeted in HF, commonly around **0.5-0.9 ng/mL** depending on context.
- A level must be interpreted with timing from the last dose, symptoms, renal function, potassium, magnesium, and indication.

### Volume of distribution

- High Vd.
- Distributed into tissues.
- Therefore **hemodialysis is not effective** for significant digoxin poisoning.

### Renal elimination

- Important renal clearance.
- P-glycoprotein transport is relevant.

### Drug interactions

**[PDF]**

- Rifampicin -> P-gp induction -> lower digoxin exposure.
- Erythromycin/clarithromycin -> P-gp inhibition -> higher digoxin exposure.

### Renal impairment

**[PDF]**

- Marked concern.

**[Verified correction/update]**

Renal failure is **not an absolute contraindication**.

Instead:

- Reduce dose.
- Monitor renal function.
- Monitor serum digoxin concentration when clinically indicated.
- Monitor K+ and Mg2+.

# 16.6 Electrolytes and Digoxin Toxicity

The PDF highlights:

- **Hypokalemia** -> increased digoxin toxicity.
- Thiazide/loop diuretics can therefore indirectly raise digoxin toxicity risk.

Also clinically important:

- Hypomagnesemia increases arrhythmia susceptibility.
- Hypercalcemia may increase toxicity risk in certain settings.

# 16.7 Antidote for Digoxin Poisoning

**[PDF]**

- **Digibind**

Modern terminology:

- **Digoxin-specific antibody fragments (digoxin immune Fab)**

Examples include Digibind/DigiFab products.

Indications for Fab depend on the severity and manifestations of toxicity, including life-threatening arrhythmias, significant hyperkalemia in acute poisoning, and major exposure in selected situations.

# 16.8 Digoxin-induced Ventricular Arrhythmia

The PDF says:

> DC shock/defibrillation is absolutely contraindicated in digoxin-induced VF.

**[Verified correction/update - important safety correction]**

This is too absolute and should **not** be used as a bedside rule.

- A patient with unstable ventricular fibrillation requires immediate life-saving electrical therapy when indicated.
- Digoxin-specific Fab is central treatment for severe digoxin toxicity.
- Lidocaine or other selected antiarrhythmics may be used for ventricular arrhythmias depending on the clinical situation and toxicology guidance.

# 17. WOLFF-PARKINSON-WHITE (WPW)

**PDF location:** page 16 (printed p. 109)

### Mechanism

Accessory AV pathway (classically the **Bundle of Kent**) bypasses the AV node.

ECG:

- Short PR interval
- Delta wave
- Wide QRS complex in classic pre-excitation

### Digoxin

**[PDF]**

- Avoid in WPW.

**[Verified correction/update]**

The particularly dangerous situation is **atrial fibrillation with pre-excitation**:

- AV nodal blockade can favor conduction down the accessory pathway.
- Drugs such as digoxin, verapamil/diltiazem, and beta blockers are therefore avoided in acute pre-excited AF.
- **Procainamide** is a recognized option for stable pre-excited AF in appropriate patients.
- Definitive treatment for a symptomatic accessory pathway is often **catheter ablation**.

### Flecainide

The PDF proposes:

- Procainamide if structural heart disease is present
- Flecainide if no heart disease

**[Verified correction/update]**

This is a teaching simplification; selection depends on the exact arrhythmia, structural heart disease, ventricular function, and local guideline.

# 18. HYPERTROPHIC OBSTRUCTIVE CARDIOMYOPATHY (HOCM)

**[PDF]**

**[Verified correction/update]**

## 29.1 Adenosine

### Mechanism

# 31. EXAM-ORIENTED CROSS-TOPIC PEARLS

## Diuretics

- **Loop:** TAL, NKCC2, lose Ca2+, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia.
- **Thiazide:** DCT, NCC, retain Ca2+, useful in hypercalciuria.
- **MRA/ENaC blockers:** potassium sparing -> hyperkalemia.
- **Acetazolamide:** PCT, bicarbonate loss, metabolic acidosis.
- **Mannitol:** osmotic diuresis, raised ICP/IOP.

## Vasopressin

- **V1** -> vasoconstriction.
- **V2** -> renal water reabsorption.
- **Desmopressin** -> central DI, enuresis, factor VIII/vWF.
- **Vaptans** -> aquaresis.

## RAAS

- ACEI -> ↓ angiotensin II + ↓ aldosterone + ↑ bradykinin.
- ACEI cough/angioedema -> bradykinin.
- ARBs block AT1 without increasing bradykinin.
- Both can produce hyperkalemia and reduce GFR in susceptible states.
- Pregnancy -> avoid ACEI/ARB/direct renin inhibition.

## CCBs

- DHP -> vessels.
- Verapamil/diltiazem -> AV node + heart.
- DHP -> edema/flushing/headache.
- Verapamil -> constipation, bradycardia.
- Avoid non-DHP + beta blocker combination.

## Nitrates

- Predominantly venous dilation at lower doses.
- Reduce preload.
- Nitrate + PDE5 inhibitor -> severe hypotension.
- Continuous nitrate exposure -> tolerance.
- Nitrate-free interval is required.

## ACS

- Aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor is foundational antiplatelet therapy.
- High-intensity statin.
- Oxygen is selective, not routine in normoxemia.
- Primary PCI preferred for eligible STEMI when timely.
- Fibrinolysis is for appropriate STEMI when timely PCI is unavailable; not for NSTEMI.

## HFrEF

Modern four pillars:

1. ARNI / ACEI / ARB strategy
2. Evidence-based beta blocker
3. MRA
4. SGLT2 inhibitor

## Digoxin

- Na+/K+-ATPase inhibition -> positive inotropy.
- Vagal effect -> AV nodal slowing.
- Hypokalemia -> more toxicity.
- Renal impairment -> dose reduction/monitoring.
- Digoxin immune Fab -> severe toxicity.
- Avoid AV-node blockers in acute pre-excited AF/WPW.

## Antiarrhythmics

- IA -> Na block + K block -> prolonged QT.
- IB -> ventricular Na blockade, shortens/minimally changes APD.
- IC -> strong Na blockade, conduction slowing; avoid in prior MI/major structural disease.
- II -> beta blockade.
- III -> repolarization prolongation.
- IV -> verapamil/diltiazem.
