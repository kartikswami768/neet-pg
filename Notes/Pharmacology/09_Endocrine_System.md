# 1. Diabetes Mellitus

<!-- PDF source: page 1 -->

## 1.1 Core pathophysiology in the PDF

The PDF describes diabetes mellitus in terms of defective insulin action leading to:

- Increased catabolism.
- Hyperglycemia.
- Increased fat breakdown.
- Ketone production, especially in marked insulin deficiency.

### Type 1 diabetes mellitus

**PDF description**

- Autoimmune disease causing pancreatic β-cell destruction.
- Insulin deficiency is the central pharmacological problem.

**PDF-listed drugs**

- Insulin
- Acarbose
- Pramlintide

### Current verification/correction

For type 1 diabetes, **insulin is essential replacement therapy**. Acarbose and pramlintide are not substitutes for insulin. Pramlintide can be used as an adjunct to mealtime insulin in selected patients; acarbose is an oral glucose-lowering drug that does not replace insulin.

---

## 1.2 Type 2 diabetes mellitus

**PDF description**

- Peripheral insulin resistance.
- β-cell response to insulin becomes inadequate.
- PDF lists **metformin + other oral hypoglycemic agents (OHA)**.
- PDF states: **HbA1c >10% → start insulin.**

### Current verification/correction

The 2026 ADA Standards state that insulin should be considered in adults with type 2 diabetes when there are symptoms of hyperglycemia or very high glycemia, specifically **A1C >10% or blood glucose ≥300 mg/dL**, or when hyperglycemic crisis is present. In patients without severe hyperglycemia or crisis, GLP-1-based therapy is generally preferred over insulin for initial/add-on therapy. Therapy selection should also account for cardiovascular disease, heart failure, CKD, weight goals, and hypoglycemia risk.

**High-yield 2026 point:** in type 2 diabetes with established/high-risk ASCVD, heart failure, or CKD, an SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 receptor agonist with demonstrated outcome benefit may be appropriate **irrespective of A1C and with or without metformin**.

---

## 1.3 Gestational diabetes mellitus

**PDF**

- Diabetes in pregnancy.
- Lists:
  - Insulin
  - Metformin
  - Glyburide

### Current verification/correction

The ADA 2026 pregnancy standard states:

- **Insulin is the preferred agent for GDM.**
- Metformin and glyburide **should not be used as first-line agents** for GDM.
- Other noninsulin injectable glucose-lowering drugs lack sufficient long-term pregnancy safety data.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists should be discontinued before pregnancy; semaglutide labeling recommends discontinuation well before planned conception because of its long half-life.

# 2. Insulin

<!-- PDF source: pages 1–2 -->

## 2.1 Classification

| Type | Drugs in PDF | Approximate duration in PDF |
|---|---|---:|
| Rapid / ultra-short acting | Lispro, Aspart, Glulisine | ~3 h |
| Short acting | Regular insulin | ~6 h |
| Intermediate acting | NPH, Lente | ~12–15 h |
| Long acting | Detemir, Glargine, Degludec | ~18 / 24 / 42 h |

> **Note:** Duration varies substantially with preparation, dose, route, injection site, and patient factors. The PDF values are useful as exam-level approximations rather than exact pharmacokinetic constants.

### Basal–bolus concept in the PDF

**Bolus insulin**

- Rapid/ultra-short or short acting.
- Given around meals.
- Controls postprandial glucose.

**Basal insulin**

- Intermediate/long acting.
- Suppresses hepatic glucose production and limits fasting/intermeal hyperglycemia.
- Prevents catabolic effects of insulin deficiency.

### Current verification/correction

The ADA 2026 describes basal insulin as a convenient initial injectable option when insulin is needed in type 2 diabetes. Common starting estimates are individualized; the principal basal action is suppression of hepatic glucose production.

## 2.2 Insulin preparations — exam distinctions

### Rapid-acting analogues

- Insulin lispro
- Insulin aspart
- Insulin glulisine

**Use:** prandial / mealtime insulin.

### Short-acting

- Regular insulin

**Important routes/uses**

- Subcutaneous use.
- **IV regular insulin** is classically used in:
  - Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).
  - Hyperkalemia as part of a potassium-shifting regimen.

### Intermediate-acting

- NPH insulin.
- Lente is included in the PDF classification.

### Long-acting

- Insulin glargine.
- Insulin detemir.
- Insulin degludec.

### Peakless / relatively flat basal insulin in the PDF

- Glargine
- Degludec

The conceptual exam point is that long-acting basal analogues have a much flatter profile than intermediate NPH insulin.

## 2.3 Afrezza — inhaled insulin

<!-- PDF source: page 2 -->

**PDF**

- Calls Afrezza “nasal insulin” and “dry powder inhaled.”
- Describes it as ultra-short acting and used before meals.
- Notes it should be combined with long-acting injectable basal insulin.
- Lists smokers and COPD as contraindications.
- Mentions a possible lung-cancer risk.

### Current verification/correction

Afrezza is **inhaled human insulin powder**, delivered by **oral inhalation**, not nasal insulin.

Current prescribing information:

- It is a rapid-acting inhaled insulin for glycemic control.
- It is contraindicated in patients with **chronic lung disease such as asthma or COPD** because of the risk of acute bronchospasm.
- Baseline lung assessment/spirometry is required before initiation.
- It is **not recommended in patients who smoke or recently stopped smoking** because safety/effectiveness are not established.
- Current labeling does **not support describing lung cancer as an established adverse effect**. The prominent pulmonary warning is acute bronchospasm.

# 4. Insulin Secretagogues

<!-- PDF source: page 3 -->

## 4.1 Mechanism

Drug acts on pancreatic β-cell:

1. Closes ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels.
2. β-cell membrane depolarizes.
3. Voltage-dependent Ca²⁺ channels open.
4. Intracellular Ca²⁺ rises.
5. Insulin exocytosis increases.

---

## 4.2 Sulfonylureas

### First generation

- Chlorpropamide
- Tolbutamide

### Second generation

More potent according to the PDF:

- Glimepiride
- Glipizide
- Gliclazide

### Major adverse effects

- Hypoglycemia.
- Weight gain.

### Chlorpropamide — classic exam associations

- **SIADH / hyponatremia**.
- **Disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol**.

### Adverse effects

- Hypoglycemia.
- Weight gain.

# 5. Incretin Physiology

<!-- PDF source: pages 3–4 -->

After food enters the gastrointestinal tract:

- Nutrient exposure and intestinal signaling lead to incretin release.
- Incretins enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion.
- Incretin signaling also suppresses glucagon.
- GLP-1 signaling in the CNS reduces appetite.

## Two important drug strategies

### GLP-1 receptor agonists

Mimic GLP-1 action.

### DPP-4 inhibitors

Prevent enzymatic degradation of endogenous incretins and increase GLP-1 activity.

# 6. GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

<!-- PDF source: pages 3–4; current update -->

## Drugs in the PDF

- Exenatide
- Dulaglutide
- Liraglutide
- Semaglutide

### Core actions

- ↑ glucose-dependent insulin secretion.
- ↓ glucagon.
- ↓ appetite.
- Delay gastric emptying to varying degrees.
- Weight loss is a prominent clinical effect.

### Semaglutide

**PDF**

- Available in oral form.
- Described as longest acting.
- Notes weight loss and pancreatitis.
- Notes a possible pancreatic/thyroid cancer risk.

### Current verification/correction

- Semaglutide is available in both oral and injectable forms.
- It has a long duration of action and is commonly used as a weekly injectable GLP-1 receptor agonist.
- GI adverse effects and weight loss are well established.
- Pancreatitis is a labeled warning/precaution; it is more accurate to describe this as a **potential risk requiring clinical monitoring**, not as an inevitable adverse effect.
- Claims that GLP-1 receptor agonists cause thyroid cancer in humans should not be presented as established clinical causation. Rodent C-cell tumor findings underlie labeling warnings, while human causation remains unproven.

### Important 2026 treatment point

ADA 2026 recommends GLP-1-based therapy preferentially over insulin in many adults with type 2 diabetes who do not have severe hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis.

### Combination rule

**Do not combine a GLP-1 receptor agonist with a DPP-4 inhibitor** merely to obtain additive glucose lowering; ADA 2026 states that concurrent use is not recommended because there is no meaningful additional benefit.

# 7. DPP-4 Inhibitors

<!-- PDF source: pages 3–4 -->

## Mechanism

- Inhibit DPP-4-mediated degradation of incretins.
- ↑ endogenous GLP-1 activity.
- Result:
  - ↑ glucose-dependent insulin.
  - ↓ glucagon.
  - Reduced appetite may occur through the incretin pathway.

## Drugs

- Linagliptin
- Sitagliptin
- Vildagliptin
- Saxagliptin

### Renal point

**Linagliptin**

- Renal dose adjustment is generally **not required**.

### Saxagliptin

The PDF text says “inhibited by CYP3A4.”

**Correction:** saxagliptin is metabolized mainly through **CYP3A4/5** and exposure can be affected by CYP3A4/5 inhibitors/inducers. It is not best described as simply “inhibited by CYP3A4.”

### Adverse effects listed in the PDF

- Inflammation.
- Arthritis.
- Nasopharyngitis.

### Additional high-yield caution

Some DPP-4 inhibitors have specific heart-failure safety considerations; ADA 2026 specifically notes that saxagliptin and alogliptin require caution in patients who develop heart failure.

# 8. Insulin Sensitizers

## 8.1 Metformin

<!-- PDF source: page 4 -->

### Mechanism

- Insulin sensitizer.
- Major action in the liver.
- Activates **AMPK-related pathways**.
- ↓ hepatic gluconeogenesis.
- ↓ hepatic glucose output.

### Clinical uses in the PDF

- DOC for type 2 diabetes mellitus.
- DOC for type 2 diabetes with PCOS.
- PDF maximum dose: approximately **2550 mg/day**.

### Adverse effects

- **Most common:** diarrhea / gastrointestinal symptoms.
- Vitamin B12 deficiency with long-term use.
- Rare but serious: lactic acidosis.

### Contraindication

- **Severe renal impairment; eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²**.

### Current verification

ADA 2026 confirms:

- Metformin is contraindicated at eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m².
- GI adverse effects can be reduced by slow titration and/or extended-release formulations.
- Vitamin B12 deficiency can occur and should be monitored/repleted when appropriate.

---

## 8.2 Pioglitazone

<!-- PDF source: page 4 -->

### Mechanism

- Insulin sensitizer.
- Acts predominantly through adipose tissue and other insulin-sensitive tissues.
- **PPAR-γ agonist**.
- Promotes adipogenesis / alters adipocyte insulin sensitivity.

### PDF use

- Add-on with metformin.

### Important adverse effects / cautions

The PDF lists:

- Urinary bladder cancer.
- Heart failure from a fluid-retaining effect.

### Current verification/correction

- Pioglitazone can cause **fluid retention, edema, and weight gain** and can precipitate or worsen heart failure.
- “Anti-diuretic effect” is not the best description; **fluid retention** is the clinically relevant concept.
- Bladder cancer remains a labeling concern, although the strength and interpretation of epidemiologic evidence have varied across studies.
- Use is avoided in patients with clinically significant symptomatic heart failure, and product-specific labeling should be followed.

# 10. α-Glucosidase Inhibitors

<!-- PDF source: page 5 -->

## Drugs

- Acarbose
- Voglibose

## Mechanism

Inhibit intestinal α-glucosidase enzymes responsible for breakdown of complex carbohydrates into absorbable monosaccharides.

Result:

- Delayed carbohydrate digestion.
- ↓ intestinal glucose absorption rate.
- ↓ postprandial hyperglycemia.

## Timing

- Taken **before meals**.

## Weight

- Generally weight neutral.

## Hypoglycemia pearl

When hypoglycemia occurs in a patient taking an α-glucosidase inhibitor, **give glucose/dextrose rather than sucrose**, because sucrose breakdown is inhibited.

> **Exam pearl:** “Acarbose → treat hypoglycemia with glucose, not sucrose.”

# 11. Pramlintide

<!-- PDF source: page 5 -->

## Pharmacology

- Synthetic **amylin analogue**.
- Amylin is normally co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic β-cells.
- Given subcutaneously.

## Actions

- ↓ glucagon.
- ↓ gastric emptying.
- ↑ satiety.
- Promotes weight loss.

### Clinical role

Adjunct to mealtime insulin in selected patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes; it does **not replace insulin** in type 1 diabetes.

# 12. SGLT2 Inhibitors

<!-- PDF source: pages 5–6 -->

## Mechanism

Act in the **proximal convoluted tubule**.

They inhibit sodium-glucose cotransport and increase urinary glucose excretion.

### Transporters in the PDF

- **SGLT2:** ~90% of filtered glucose reabsorption.
- **SGLT1:** ~10%.

## Drugs

- Canagliflozin
- Dapagliflozin
- Empagliflozin
- Sotagliflozin — inhibits SGLT1 + SGLT2.

## Effects

- Glycosuria.
- Osmotic diuresis.
- Reduced plasma glucose.

## Uses

- Type 2 diabetes mellitus.
- Chronic heart failure.
- Chronic kidney disease.

## Cardiorenal benefit

The PDF emphasizes:

- Cardioprotective effect.
- Nephroprotective effect.

### Current verification

ADA 2026 supports SGLT2 inhibitor use in appropriate patients with type 2 diabetes and heart failure and/or CKD because of cardiovascular and kidney outcome benefits. These benefits can be relevant independently of A1C.

---

## 12.1 SGLT2 inhibitor adverse-effect mnemonic from the PDF

**GUU**

- **F** — Fournier gangrene.
- **O** — Osteoporosis/fracture concern.
- **D** — Diabetic ketoacidosis.
- **U** — UTI / genital infections.

### More precise current interpretation

Common clinically important adverse effects include:

- Genital mycotic infection.
- Urinary/genitourinary infections.
- Volume depletion.
- Euglycemic or otherwise atypical DKA.
- Rare Fournier gangrene.

Fracture concerns have been strongest with specific agents/populations rather than being a uniform class effect.

### Perioperative/high-acuity point

ADA 2026 advises avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors during severe acute illness, ketonemia/ketonuria, prolonged fasting, and around surgery; scheduled surgery generally warrants holding them in advance according to agent-specific guidance.

# 16. Hyperkalemia — Drug Treatment

<!-- PDF source: page 7; ECG diagram visually reconstructed -->

The PDF illustrates:

- Hyperkalemia.
- ECG changes, including **peaked T waves**.
- Treatment divided conceptually into cardiac protection, intracellular shift, and potassium removal.

## Step 1 — Protect the heart

**Calcium gluconate**

- Stabilizes the cardiac membrane.
- **Does not lower serum potassium.**

## Step 2 — Shift potassium into cells

### Insulin

- **IV regular insulin**.
- Administer with glucose as appropriate to reduce hypoglycemia risk.
- Promotes intracellular K⁺ shift.

### β2-adrenergic stimulation

PDF mentions:

- Salbutamol.
- Epinephrine.

The standard exam/clinical drug is **nebulized salbutamol (albuterol)** as an adjunct to insulin/glucose in appropriate patients.

## Step 3 — Remove potassium from the body

- Loop diuretic such as furosemide, when appropriate and when renal function/volume status permit.
- Potassium-binding resins/binders.

### PDF

- Polystyrene resin.

### Current clinical note

Modern practice also uses newer potassium binders in suitable patients, but the PDF specifically focuses on older resin therapy.

> **Exam pearl:**  
> **Calcium = membrane stabilization**  
> **Insulin + glucose / β2 agonist = intracellular shift**  
> **Diuretics / potassium binders / dialysis = potassium removal**

# 18. Thyroid Peroxidase (TPO) Inhibitors — Thionamides

## Drugs

- Propylthiouracil (PTU).
- Carbimazole.
- Methimazole.

### Mechanism

TPO inhibition decreases:

- Iodide oxidation.
- Organification.
- Coupling reactions.

Therefore thyroid hormone synthesis falls.

### Prodrug relationship

- **Carbimazole → prodrug of methimazole.**
- Methimazole → active thionamide.

## PTU — additional action

PTU also reduces **peripheral conversion of T4 to T3**.

### Major adverse effects

- Rash.
- Agranulocytosis.
- Hepatotoxicity — particularly important with PTU.

The PDF says “monitor WBC”; clinically, patients should be instructed to report fever/sore throat urgently because agranulocytosis can develop abruptly.

# 19. Iodides

<!-- PDF source: page 8 -->

## Drugs

- Sodium iodide.
- Potassium iodide.
- Lugol iodine.

### Lugol iodine in the PDF

- 5% iodine + 10% potassium iodide.

### Mechanism / clinical point

High-dose iodide acutely inhibits thyroid hormone release and organification (Wolff–Chaikoff effect).

**Key exam point:**

- Very rapid onset.
- Useful for short-term control, including thyroid storm.
- Not generally used as chronic standalone treatment because the gland can “escape” from the acute inhibitory effect.

# 21. Hyperthyroidism in Pregnancy

<!-- PDF source: page 8 -->

## PDF

### First trimester

- PTU.

### Second and third trimester

- Carbimazole / methimazole.

### Reasoning

- PTU has lower risk of the characteristic embryopathy associated with first-trimester methimazole exposure but carries hepatotoxicity risk.
- Methimazole is generally preferred after the first trimester because PTU hepatotoxicity is an important concern.

### Current verification

The American Thyroid Association states that when antithyroid drug therapy is required, **PTU is preferred through the early pregnancy period (commonly through week 16)**, after which switching to methimazole is generally recommended.

Methimazole exposure in early pregnancy is associated with characteristic congenital anomalies, including **aplasia cutis and choanal/esophageal abnormalities**.

**Do not memorize the PDF as “PTU is forbidden in 2nd/3rd trimester” in absolute terms:** rather, PTU is usually switched to methimazole after early pregnancy unless a specific clinical reason requires otherwise.

# 23. Thyroid Storm

<!-- PDF source: page 8 -->

The PDF gives the sequence:

1. **Propranolol**
2. **PTU**
3. **Iodides + dexamethasone** in resistant cases.

### Current clinical sequencing principle

A pharmacologic emergency regimen generally includes:

1. **β-blockade** for adrenergic symptoms and tachycardia.
2. **Thionamide** to block new hormone synthesis.
3. **Iodide after thionamide** to block hormone release and organification acutely.
4. **Glucocorticoid** to reduce T4→T3 conversion and treat possible relative adrenal insufficiency.

The crucial exam relationship is:

> **Give iodide after the thionamide, not before it**, because giving iodine first can provide substrate for additional hormone synthesis in some settings.

# 26. Osteoporosis

<!-- PDF source: pages 9–11 -->

## Core concept

The PDF summarizes osteoporosis as:

- ↓ bone mineral density.
- ↑ fracture risk.

The PDF visually organizes osteoporosis drugs into three groups:

### A. Inhibit osteoclasts → ↓ bone resorption

- Bisphosphonates.
- Calcitonin.
- Gallium nitrate.
- Denosumab.

# 28. Teriparatide

<!-- PDF source: page 10 -->

## Pharmacology

- Synthetic recombinant **PTH fragment**.
- Given intermittently/pulsatilely.

### Key concept

- **Intermittent PTH exposure → osteoblast stimulation → bone formation.**
- Chronic sustained PTH excess → bone resorption.

## Use

- Osteoporosis, especially in patients at very high fracture risk.

## Adverse effect / warning in the PDF

- Osteosarcoma.

### Current verification/correction

The osteosarcoma warning historically came from rat toxicology. Human postmarketing/epidemiologic data have not demonstrated a causal increase in osteosarcoma. The drug remains reserved for selected patients and contraindications/cautions remain important.

The PDF says **maximum 2 years**.

Current labeling has relaxed the historical absolute lifetime 2-year restriction in the U.S.; use beyond 2 years may be considered in patients who remain at or return to high fracture risk, under specialist guidance.

### Avoid / caution

- Skeletal malignancy or other conditions associated with increased baseline osteosarcoma risk.
- Hypercalcemia.
- Paget disease is a classic exam caution.

# 34. Hypercalcemia — Pharmacological Treatment

<!-- PDF source: page 11 -->

## Initial treatment in the PDF

### Current refinement

The core initial intervention in significant hypercalcemia is **volume repletion with isotonic saline**.

# 75. High-Yield Exam Pearls

> **Insulin:** hypoglycemia is the most important acute adverse effect.

> **Metformin:** ↓ hepatic gluconeogenesis; GI upset is common; B12 deficiency can occur; lactic acidosis is rare but serious; contraindicated when eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m².

> **Sulfonylureas:** hypoglycemia + weight gain.

> **Chlorpropamide:** SIADH + disulfiram-like reaction.

> **GLP-1 RA:** weight loss; do not routinely combine with DPP-4 inhibitor.

> **SGLT2 inhibitor:** glucosuria + cardiorenal benefit; think genital mycosis, volume depletion and euglycemic DKA.

> **Acarbose:** hypoglycemia must be treated with **glucose/dextrose** rather than sucrose.

> **PTU:** inhibits TPO + peripheral T4→T3 conversion.

> **Methimazole:** preferred after early pregnancy period when an antithyroid drug is required.

> **Iodide:** fastest acute thyroid-inhibiting effect; give **after** a thionamide in thyroid storm.

> **Radioiodine:** never in pregnancy.

> **Levothyroxine:** T4 replacement; oral is standard chronic therapy.

> **Teprotumumab:** anti-IGF-1 receptor therapy for thyroid eye disease.

> **Bisphosphonates:** inhibit osteoclast mevalonate pathway through FPPS.

> **Denosumab:** anti-RANKL.

> **Teriparatide:** intermittent PTH → bone formation.

> **Romosozumab:** anti-sclerostin.

> **Burosumab:** anti-FGF23 → XLH, not routine osteoporosis.

> **Acromegaly:** transsphenoidal surgery is an important first-line approach when the adenoma is surgically resectable; somatostatin analogues and pegvisomant are major pharmacologic options.

> **Prolactinoma:** cabergoline is a major first-line dopamine agonist; bromocriptine has a long-established pregnancy role.

> **GnRH agonist:** continuous administration → initial flare, then suppression.

> **GnRH antagonist:** suppresses without the initial flare.

> **Hydrocortisone:** clinically important combined glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid activity.

> **Dexamethasone/betamethasone:** potent glucocorticoids with minimal mineralocorticoid activity.

> **Mifepristone:** progesterone receptor antagonist **and** glucocorticoid receptor antagonist.

> **Ulipristal:** SPRM; **30 mg** EC dose; up to 5 days.

> **Tamoxifen:** anti-estrogen in breast; estrogenic effects in bone/uterus.

> **Raloxifene:** SERM used in postmenopausal osteoporosis; does not stimulate the endometrium in the same way as tamoxifen.

> **Aromatase inhibitors:** ↓ androgen → estrogen conversion.

> **Finasteride/dutasteride:** inhibit 5α-reductase; they are **not** androgen receptor blockers.
