## Change in body fluid with age (*00:09:55*)

- **Intrauterine life:** TBW is 95% of total body weight and decreases with age.
- **Preterm infants:** TBW is 90% of birth weight.
- **Term infants:** TBW is 75% of birth weight.

### ICF and ECF

- In intrauterine life, initial ICF is less than ECF.
- ICF gradually increases due to organogenesis, while ECF decreases.
- At **3–4 months after birth**, ICF and ECF become equal (ratio **1:1**).
- By **1 year of age**, ICF and ECF approach adult levels.
- At puberty, percentage and distribution of body fluids reach adult proportions.

## Measurement of body water (*00:17:56*)

- To measure the exact amount of body water (TBW), the **indicator-dilution principle**, also called the **volume-of-distribution method**, is used.
- The schematic distinguishes:
  - Water inside the cell: intracellular fluid (ICF).
  - Water outside the cell: interstitial fluid (ISF).
  - Water inside the capillary: plasma.
  - Plasma + ISF: extracellular fluid (ECF).
- A substance such as **Q mg of inulin** is injected into a capillary. It distributes in plasma, penetrates the capillary, and enters the interstitial fluid. Inulin is impermeable to the cell membrane.

![[ch02_p010_indicator_dilution.png]]

*Indicator-dilution schematic for measuring fluid compartments.*

### Indicator-dilution calculations

- Inulin distributes throughout the entire ECF, and equilibrium is reached between plasma and ISF concentrations.
- Inulin concentration is measured in plasma.

\[
\text{Volume of distribution} = \frac{Q}{C}
\]

- **Q:** total amount injected.
- **C:** concentration in plasma.
- For substances that are easily metabolized, metabolism and excretion must be considered:

\[
\text{Volume} = \frac{Q-e}{C}
\]

- **e:** the excreted or metabolized part of the injected substance.
- A substance permeable to capillaries reaches the ISF.
- If it is also permeable to the cell membrane, it reaches the ICF and measures TBW.
- If the injected substance is impermeable to capillaries, it measures only plasma volume.

## Osmolar gap (*00:37:12*)

- Total osmolality is measured with an osmometer.
- Principle: **freezing-point depression**.
- Calculated osmolarity:

\[
\text{Osmolarity} = 2[\text{Na}^+] + \frac{\text{Glucose (mg\%)}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN (mg\%)}}{2.8}
\]

- The source also lists **BUN = urea / 2.14**.
- If all units are given in mmol/L: **2 × [Na⁺] + glucose + BUN**.
- Measured osmolality is greater than calculated osmolality.

\[
\text{Osmolar gap} = \text{Measured osmolality} - \text{Calculated osmolality}
\]

- Normal value stated: **10**.
- An increased osmolar gap is listed with:
  - Alcohol poisoning
  - Sorbitol
  - A huge amount of protein
  - Hyperlipidaemia

## Osmolarity and tonicity (*00:43:00*)

- **Osmolarity:** concentration of osmotically active particles (osmoles) in one litre of solution.
- **Osmolality:** osmoles per kilogram of water.
- Difference between osmolarity and osmolality: **1%**.
- **Tonicity** predicts the effect of a solution on cell volume at equilibrium and depends on impermeant solutes.
- If cell osmolarity is **300 mOsm/L** and the solution is **500 mOsm/L**, the solution is hyperosmotic compared with the cell. Without knowing the concentration of impermeant solutes, it is difficult to determine whether the solution is hypertonic, hypotonic, or isotonic.

**Examples in the prose**

- **200 mOsmol/L NaCl** (impermeable) + **300 mOsmol/L urea** (permeable) → described as hyperosmotic but hypotonic compared with the cell. A hypotonic solution increases cell volume at equilibrium.
- **300 mOsmol/L NaCl** (impermeable) + **200 mOsmol/L urea** (permeable) → described as isotonic compared with the cell; cell volume remains the same.

## Darrow–Yannet diagram (*00:59:35*)

- Shows changes in ECF and ICF volume after administration/infusion of solutions of different tonicity.
- At equilibrium, the osmolality of ICF and ECF is equal; the value stated is **280 mOsmol/L**.

![[ch02_p014_darrow_yannet_diagrams.png]]

*Darrow–Yannet diagrams for isotonic fluid gain/loss and hypotonic fluid gain.*

| Gain/loss of fluid | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Gain of isotonic fluid | ECF volume increases without a change in osmolarity. Example: 0.9% NaCl infusion. |
| Loss of isotonic fluid | In haemorrhage, urinary loss, acute vomiting, or diarrhoea, isotonic fluid loss decreases ECF volume without altering osmolarity. |
| Gain of hypotonic fluid | Examples: 0.45% NaCl, polydipsia, SIADH. ECF volume expands and ECF osmolarity decreases. Since ICF osmolality remains higher, the state is not at equilibrium; hypotonic ECF moves into ICF by osmosis, expanding ICF volume and decreasing osmolarity. Hypotonic fluid therapy is described as dangerous because brain tissue may swell, causing herniation. If 1 L of hypotonic fluid is infused, 1/3 (333 mL) enters ECF and 2/3 (667 mL) enters ICF. |

![[ch02_p015_darrow_yannet_diagrams.png]]

*Darrow–Yannet diagrams for hypotonic-fluid loss and hypertonic-fluid gain/loss.*

| Gain/loss of fluid | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Loss of hypotonic fluid | In diabetes insipidus or with inadequate water intake, excess water is excreted (insufficient ADH). ECF volume decreases. Because only water is lost and solutes remain, ECF osmolarity and tonicity increase, making it hypertonic relative to ICF. Water moves from ICF to ECF, decreasing ICF volume and increasing its osmolarity. The closing statement says that both ICF and ECF volume decrease while the osmolarity of both compartments increases. |
| Gain of hypertonic fluid | Infusing 3% NaCl expands ECF volume and increases its osmolarity. ECF becomes hypertonic relative to ICF, causing water to move out of cells, decreasing ICF volume and increasing ICF osmolarity. |
| Loss of hypertonic fluid | In adrenal insufficiency, solute is lost in excess of water. Hypertonic-fluid loss decreases ECF volume and makes it hypotonic relative to ICF. Water moves from ECF to ICF, and ICF volume increases slightly. The final statement says that ECF volume and ICF volume are reduced, with decreased tonicity in both compartments at a new steady state. |

> **Internal inconsistency retained:** the loss-of-hypertonic-fluid explanation first says that ICF volume increases slightly, then concludes that ICF volume is reduced. Both statements are preserved.

## MCQs

**Q1.** A research fellow studied volumes and electrolytes in body-water compartments. A sample contained Na⁺ = 10 mEq/L and K⁺ = 140 mEq/L. Which compartment is represented? (NEET 2021)

- A. ECF
- B. ICF
- C. ISF
- D. Plasma

**Answer: B.**

**Q2.** Calculate blood volume for a patient weighing 60 kg with haematocrit 45%. (JIPMER 2019)

- A. 4.8 L
- B. 5.0 L
- C. 5.45 L
- D. 6 L

**Answer: C.**

**Q3.** 100 mg of sucrose is injected into a 70 kg man. Plasma sucrose after mixing is 0.01 mg/mL. If 5 mg has been metabolized during this period, what is the ECF volume? (AIIMS 2020)

- A. 9.5 L
- B. 14 L
- C. 17.5 L
