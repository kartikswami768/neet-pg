### IMPORTANT INFORMATION ABOUT VESSEL WALLS
- Endothelium is present in all blood vessels.
- Elastic tissue and smooth muscle are present in all vessels except venules and capillaries.
- Outermost fibrous tissue is present in all vessels except capillaries.
- Sympathetic supply is present in all vessels except capillaries, metarterioles and precapillary sphincters.
- Parasympathetic supply is present in certain organs (salivary gland, GIT and genital erectile tissue), where it causes vasodilation.

### BLOOD FLOW

| Ohm's law | Hagen–Poiseuille equation |
|---|---|
| Aim: calculate blood flow. | Aim: calculate resistance directly. |
| V = IR, where V is the pressure difference (P₁ − P₂), I is blood flow and R is vessel resistance. | R = 8ηL/(πr⁴), where η is blood viscosity, L is vessel length and r is vessel radius. |
| P₁ is blood pressure at one end of the vessel; P₂ is blood pressure at the other end. | Resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of radius. |
| Flow = (P₁ − P₂)/R. | — |

**Ohm's law example**
- A 22-year-old man has muscle blood flow of 250 mL/min, mean arterial pressure of 130 mmHg and venous pressure of 5 mmHg. Calculate the vascular resistance in the muscle.
- I = 250 mL/min; P₁ = 130 mmHg; P₂ = 5 mmHg.
- Potential difference = P₁ − P₂ = 125 mmHg.
- Vascular resistance R = (P₁ − P₂)/I = 125/250 = 0.5 mmHg/(mL/min).

**Hagen–Poiseuille example**
- Calculate the change in vessel resistance after a 50% decrease in vessel radius.
- Normal radius = r; after a 50% decrease, radius = ½r.
- Since R ∝ 1/r⁴, the resistance becomes 16 times the normal resistance.

### TOTAL PERIPHERAL RESISTANCE

- The source represents the aorta, arteries, arterioles, capillaries and venous system as a series circuit, with resistances R₁, R₂, R₃, R₄ and R₅.
- For resistances in series, total resistance = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + R₄ + R₅.
- Total resistance is the total peripheral resistance (TPR), also called systemic vascular resistance (SVR).
- Resistance from arterioles (R₃) is the maximum in the series shown.

![[ch30_p232_total_peripheral_resistance_diagram.png]]

**Calculation of systemic vascular resistance**
- P₁ = mean aortic pressure, which pushes blood into systemic circulation.
- P₂ = right atrial pressure, where blood drains.
- P₁ − P₂ = I × R. Total cardiac output (CO) is total blood flow in systemic circulation per minute and is approximately 5 L/min normally.
- SVR/TPR = (MAP − RAP)/CO.

**Example:** A drug increases CO by 30% and decreases MAP by 10%. Assuming central venous pressure is 0 mmHg, what percentage change occurs in SVR?
- Central venous pressure = right atrial pressure (RAP) = 0.
- MAP becomes 0.9 of its original value; the question says CO increases to 1.3 of its original value.
- The source solution line says “CO is decreased by 30%” but then gives 1.3 of baseline; these statements conflict. The worked ratio uses 1.3: SVR ratio = (MAP − RAP)/CO = 0.9/1.3 = 0.69.
- SVR is reduced to 69% of the original resistance; the decrease is 100% − 69% = 31%.

### RESISTANCE IS HIGHEST IN ARTERIOLES
- Resistance is inversely proportional to radius⁴.
- The capillary lumen is much narrower than the arteriolar lumen, so the resistance of one capillary is greater than that of one arteriole.
- The number of capillaries in the body is greater than the number of arterioles.
- Capillaries are arranged in parallel. For parallel resistances: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/R₇.
- Adding more parallel resistances lowers total resistance.
- The combined resistance of arterioles is much greater than the combined resistance of capillaries.

### ARTERIOLES
- Have the maximum number of smooth muscles in vessel walls.
- Have rich sympathetic innervation of smooth muscle.
- Function: distribution of blood volume among different organs.
- Stimulation causes smooth-muscle contraction and vasoconstriction; inhibition causes smooth-muscle relaxation and vasodilation.

### REYNOLDS NUMBER
- Aim: determine the probability/type of blood flow.
- Reynolds number: Re = ρDV/η, where ρ is blood density, D is vessel diameter, V is blood-flow velocity and η is blood viscosity.

| Reynolds number | Type of blood flow stated in the source |
|---:|---|
| Greater than 3000 | Turbulent blood flow |
| Less than 2000 | Laminar blood flow |
| 2000–3000 | May be laminar or turbulent blood flow |

- The source identifies velocity as the most important parameter for calculating Reynolds number and a crucial determinant of turbulent flow.
- Critical velocity is the velocity beyond which blood flow is always turbulent.

![[ch30_p234_reynolds_number_and_flow_types.png]]

### VELOCITY OF BLOOD FLOW
- Blood-flow velocity is inversely proportional to the total cross-sectional area of the vessels: **V ∝ 1/A**.
  - V = blood-flow velocity.
  - A = total cross-sectional area.
- Total cross-sectional area of capillaries is obtained by adding the cross-sectional areas of all capillaries.
- The source lists the total cross-sectional area sequence as: capillaries > venules > veins > arterioles > artery > vena cava > aorta.
- Maximum velocity of blood flow: aorta.
- Minimum velocity of blood flow: capillaries.

### VELOCITY AND CROSS-SECTIONAL AREA
- Total cross-sectional area is maximal in the capillaries.
- The source graph displays systolic and diastolic pressure, velocity, resistance and total cross-sectional area along the vascular tree.
- Values described in the source: average pressure at the aorta/arteries is 90 mmHg and at arterioles 60 mmHg; pressure at capillaries 25 mmHg; average pressure at venules and veins 15 and 11 mmHg, respectively; pressure falls to 3 mmHg at the vena cava. The graph also marks systolic pressure at 120 mmHg, diastolic pressure at 80 mmHg, and a 40-mmHg level along the vascular tree.
- Resistance to blood flow is maximal in arterioles. The source notes that greater resistance produces a greater pressure drop downstream.
- Aorta and major arteries have high systolic and diastolic pressures; arterioles are resistance vessels and show a sudden pressure drop.

### LAPLACE'S LAW
- The source gives the relationship P = T/r, therefore wall tension T = P × r.
  - P = luminal pressure of the blood vessel.
  - T = tension generated (force trying to tear the vessel).
  - r = vessel radius.
- The source compares an arteriole of larger diameter with a capillary of smaller diameter. At equal luminal pressure (P₁ = P₂ = 25 mmHg), the arteriole has greater wall tension (T₁) than the capillary (T₂).

| Capillaries | Arteries and arterioles |
|---|---|
| Narrow diameter. | Large diameter. |
| Contain only endothelial cells. | Higher wall tension and greater tendency to rupture. |
| Low wall tension; less prone to rupture because of small radius. | To prevent rupture, require substantial elastic tissue and smooth muscle. |

- Comparing the normal heart (T₁) with a dilated heart (T₂), the source states that wall tension is greater in the dilated heart because its radius is larger.
- Increased wall tension means the heart muscle consumes more oxygen.

### BERNOULLI'S PRINCIPLE
- The source labels lateral pressure as potential energy and end pressure as kinetic energy: end pressure = kinetic energy = ½mv².
- When blood flows, the source distinguishes two pressure types:

| Pressure type | Cause | Relationship with flowing blood stated in the source |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral pressure | Potential energy (PE) of flowing blood. | Exerted on the lateral surface of the vessel wall. |
| End pressure | Kinetic energy (KE) of flowing blood. | Exerted in the direction of flow; KE = ½mV² and depends on mass and velocity. |

- Bernoulli's principle: total energy (PE + KE) remains constant. An increase in one component (PE or KE) causes a decrease in the other.
- **Atherosclerotic plaque:** the source diagram shows that a reduced diameter/cross-sectional area (A) increases velocity (V), reduces potential energy/lateral pressure and increases kinetic energy; the source diagram links this to further plaque formation and occlusion.
