# 31 — Blood Pressure and its Regulation

## 18. Blood Pressure & Its Regulation
## BLOOD PRESSURE
- Blood pressure (BP) - lateral pressure exerted on the vessel walls by flowing blood
### TYPES OF BLOOD PRESSURE
| Type | Definition/formula |
|---|---|
| Systolic BP | Maximum BP exerted during systole. |
| Diastolic BP | Minimum BP during diastole. |
| Pulse pressure | SBP − DBP. |
| Mean arterial pressure (MAP) | MAP = DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP) = ⅔ DBP + ⅓ SBP. This is the time-weighted average pressure throughout the cardiac cycle; more weight is given to diastolic than systolic BP. The source gives diastolic duration as 0.5 s and systolic duration as 0.3 s. |

### DETERMINANTS OF BP

| BP component | Determinant | Relationship with blood pressure stated in the source |
|---|---|---|
| Systolic blood pressure | Stroke volume (SV) or cardiac output (CO) | Directly proportional |
| Systolic blood pressure | Compliance of the aorta | Inversely proportional |
| Diastolic blood pressure | Elastic recoil of the aorta (main factor) and total peripheral resistance (TPR) | Directly proportional |

### SYSTOLIC AND DIASTOLIC BLOOD PRESSURE

#### SYSTOLIC BLOOD PRESSURE (SBP)
- During diastole, the semilunar valves are closed and the aortic arch is filled with blood.
- Pressure inside the aortic arch is at its minimum: diastolic blood pressure (DBP).
- Systolic blood pressure (SBP) is the increased pressure exerted on the vessel wall during systole.
- If SV and CO increase, SBP increases.
- Cardiac output = SV × heart rate.
- **Aortic compliance** means the stretchability of the vessel. With calcification of the aortic arch, the wall becomes stiffer and compliance is reduced; the same SV therefore produces a greater pressure and raises SBP.
$$\text{Compliance of the aorta} = \frac{∆V}{ΔP}$$
 for the aortic arch, 
	- ΔV = SV  
	- ΔP = SBP − DBP = pulse pressure (PP).

	Compliance = SV/PP; therefore 
 $$PP = \frac{SV}{compliance}$$

| Effect described for calcification of the aortic arch | Change stated in the source |
|---|---|
| Compliance | Decreases |
| Systolic BP | Increases |
| Diastolic BP | Decreases |
| Pulse pressure | Increases |



#### DIASTOLIC BLOOD PRESSURE
- **Diastolic blood pressure** is the minimum pressure inside the aortic arch when the semilunar valves are closed.
	Normal DBP as 80 mmHg.

- Maintenance of blood flow is mainly attributed to elastic recoil of the aortic arch:
  1. During systole, SV is ejected and the aortic arch expands.
  2. After ejection, the semilunar valve closes and the expanded arch recoils elastically.
  3. The recoil applies pressure to the blood, moves blood forward and exerts pressure on the vessel wall; the source gives this pressure as approximately 80 mmHg (DBP).
- Another factor is high resistance in blood vessels (TPR); greater peripheral resistance produces a higher DBP.
- The source states that during aortic calcification there is no expansion of the aortic arch during systole, no recoil of the aorta, and DBP decreases. This statement is retained as presented in the source.

#### MEAN ARTERIAL PRESSURE (MAP)
- MAP is the average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle.
- The source shows an aortic/carotid pressure curve. At t₁, ejection starts; pressure rises to a peak (SBP) and then falls to the dicrotic notch.
- The dicrotic notch indicates closure of the semilunar valves, after which the diastolic phase begins.
- During diastole, pressure falls and then stabilizes at the minimum value, shown as 80 mmHg (DBP).
- t₂ marks the end of the cardiac cycle.
- MAP = area under the pressure curve divided by the total duration of the cardiac cycle (t₂ − t₁): $$
\mathrm{MAP} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_{t_1}^{t_2} P(t)\,dt}{t_2-t_1}
$$
- Approximation: MAP ≈ ⅔ DBP + ⅓ SBP.



#### Systemic Filling Pressure
- **Systemic filling pressure (SFP)** is the pressure that would exist throughout the systemic circulation if **blood flow stopped** and pressures had enough time to equilibrate.
	It reflects the **combined effects of blood volume** and the **capacity of the systemic vasculature to contain that blood.**
	Mean SFP ≈ 7 mm HG
	$$
	\text{Venous Return} = \frac{MSFP - RAP}{\text{Resistance to Venous Return}}
	$$

	
	where,
	RAP = Right atrial Pressure ≈ 2 mmHg
	
## REGULATION OF BLOOD PRESSURE
| Rapidly acting/short-term (seconds to minutes)                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Intermediate acting (minutes to hours)                                                                                                                         | Long-term (within days)                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. **Baroreceptor reflex** (it acts rapidly within 5–8 s); <br>2. **chemoreceptor reflex**; <br>3. **CNS ischemic response** (the ==second most potent== and the ==strongest overall==); <br>4. **epinephrine** secretion from the adrenal medulla.<br> | 1. **Capillary fluid-shift** mechanism; <br>2. **stress** relaxation and reverse stress relaxation;<br>3. **renin–angiotensin**-mediated **vasoconstriction**. | 1. **Renal fluid conservation** by ADH, ANP and aldosterone; <br>2. **RAS–aldosterone system** (==most potent==). |

- The source separately states that the CNS ischemic response is the “2nd most potent” mechanism for BP regulation.

### INTERMEDIATE-ACTING MECHANISMS
| Mechanism | Description from the source |
|---|---|
| Capillary fluid-shift mechanism | ↑ BP in capillaries raises hydrostatic pressure; together with oncotic pressure from plasma proteins, this drives extra fluid out of capillaries into tissue spaces, reducing blood pressure. The source attributes this to Starling forces. |
| Stress relaxation | In arterioles, very high BP exerts more pressure on the vessel wall; smooth muscle relaxes, reducing BP in the vessel. |
| Reverse stress relaxation | If BP is low inside the vessel, smooth muscle contracts/spasms, constricting the vessel and causing a slight rise in BP. |



### BARORECEPTOR REFLEX AND CHEMORECEPTOR REFLEX
#### Overview
![[IMG_1357.jpeg]]

**Baroreceptors**
- The common carotid artery divides into the internal and external carotid arteries. At the origin of the internal carotid artery is a dilated region called the carotid sinus.
- Nerve fibres in the carotid-sinus wall contain mechanoreceptors (baroreceptors).
- A second location is the wall of the aortic arch, where small neuronal endings contain baroreceptors.
- Baroreceptors are **stretch receptors** in the adventitial layer of the vessel wall.
- Carotid-sinus baroreceptors are supplied by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX); aortic baroreceptors are supplied by the vagus nerve (CN X).

**Chemoreceptors**
- The carotid body lies outside the vessel wall near the division of the common carotid artery into internal and external carotid arteries; it contains chemoreceptors.
- The source identifies type I glomus cells in the carotid body.
- The source states that there are three aortic bodies on the aortic arch, containing glomus cells, and describes their receptors as “Baroreceptors.” This wording is retained as a source statement.
- Chemoreceptors are described as being in the same area as baroreceptors and supplied by the same nerve.
- The branch of CN IX is the sinus nerve of Hering; at the aortic arch, the source says both are supplied by the vagus nerve.


| Feature    | Baroreceptor                        | Chemoreceptor                                          |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Location   | Carotid sinus wall and aortic arch  | Carotid body and aortic body                           |
| Type       | Stretch/mechanoreceptor             | —                                                      |
| Activation | ↑ blood pressure (Pulse Pressure)   | Hypoxia (↓ PO₂), hypercapnia (↑ PCO₂), acidosis (↑ H⁺) |
| Afferent   | Branches of CN IX and CN X          | Branches of CN IX and CN X                             |
| Centre     | Medulla                             | Medulla                                                |
| Efferent   | Sympathetic/parasympathetic systems | Sympathetic/parasympathetic systems                    |

#### BARO-REFLEX PATHWAY
##### Pathway
- ↑ BP stretches the vessel wall and stimulates baroreceptors.
- Working range of baroreceptors: 50–200 mmHg. 
	- Set Point is 100 mmHg (MAP)
	- The source states that if BP is <50 mmHg or >200 mmHg, baroreceptors become inactivated.
	- Baroreceptor activation follows a sigmoid curve. 
	- Activation increases as BP changes from 50 to 200 mmHg and reaches a plateau above 200 mmHg.
- The baroreceptor **sensitivity to ==pulse pressure== > sensitivity to MAP (mean arterial pressure).**

**Pathway:**
1. signals from baroreceptors reach the **nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS)** via CN IX and X. 
2. NTS output activates the **caudal ventrolateral medulla (CVLM; glutamatergic)**, which inhibits the ==**rostral ventrolateral medulla<u>/vasomotor centre</u> (RVLM/VMC)**== via GABA.
3. Sympathetic outflow decreases, while the **cardioinhibitory centre (CIC)** increases parasympathetic outflow; HR and BP fall.

> ↑ BP → baroreceptor activation → ↓ VMC/sympathetic activity and ↑ CIC/parasympathetic activity → ↓ BP. 

>Neurogenic Shock: VMC/Sympathetic Outflow is affected → Both HR and BP ↓ 
>Neurogenic Hypertension: Baroreceptor/Parasympathetic Nerves affected → Both HR and BP ↑
- With low BP, the VMC/RVLM is tonically active and sympathetic activity increases.
- The VMC/RVLM is a tonically active centre, always active even during sleep.

##### CHANGE OF POSTURE
- Moving from supine to standing causes pooling of approximately 500 mL of blood in the lower part of the body.
- Venous return decreases, causing ↓ SV, ↓ CO and ↓ BP. Reduced baroreceptor activation increases sympathetic outflow because the VMC is tonically active.
- Sympathetic activation increases HR, force of contraction, vasoconstriction and TPR; these responses help restore BP and venous return.

###### ORTHOSTATIC HYPOTENSION
- In the supine position, the source gives MAP as 100 mmHg.
- Changing from supine to standing causes a BP drop; the source gives the normal fall as 5–10 mmHg.
- Baroreceptor firing falls, sympathetic activity increases and BP may rise above baseline; the rise activates baroreceptors, inhibits the vasomotor centre and brings BP toward the normal range. The source diagram shows fluctuations that stabilize in approximately 1 minute.
- In autonomic failure, the BP fall is greater than normal and does not stabilize within 1 minute.
- Orthostatic/postural hypotension: sustained reduction in SBP of at least 20 mmHg or DBP of 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing after being supine for 5 minutes.

##### CAROTID SINUS MASSAGE
- Carotid sinus massage is performed to terminate an episode of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT).
- Carotid sinus massage means applying external pressure to the carotid sinus region.
- ↑ carotid-sinus blood pressure activates baroreceptors.


**Baroreceptor response illustrated for carotid sinus manipulation**
- With baroreceptor activation: vasomotor centre (VMC) is inhibited and the cardioinhibitory centre (CIC) is activated. Sympathetic activity decreases and parasympathetic activity increases, reducing BP/TPR and HR/AV-node conduction.
- In PSVT, excessive atrial impulses increase ventricular contraction rate; ventricular filling, EDV, SV and CO are reduced. The stated treatment target is to reduce ventricular rate by blocking some impulses at the AV node.
- **Carotid sinus ligation:** the source states that distal ligation raises sinus blood pressure and activates baroreceptors; proximal ligation lowers sinus blood pressure and inhibits baroreceptors.
- The diagrams also show that baroreceptor inhibition activates the VMC and inhibits the CIC, with increased BP and HR.

#### CHEMO-REFLEX PATHWAY
- The source diagram labels BP <70 mmHg as the trigger to stimulate chemoreceptors.
- Direct pathway: VMC activation increases BP; CIC activation can decrease HR.
- Ventilation increases, causing hyperventilation; lung stretch receptors participate in the indirect pathway.
- BP (MAP) <70 mmHg with hypoxia and hypercapnia stimulates chemoreceptors.
- Mild chemoreceptor stimulation produces bradycardia; moderate/severe stimulation produces tachycardia.
- The source states that stretch receptors of the lungs induce hypoxic tachycardia.

### CNS ISCHEMIC RESPONSE
- BP (MAP) <50 mmHg activates the CNS ischemic response (CIR), causing brain hypoxia.
> CNS Ischemic response → Generalised peripheral vasoconstriction
- Local hypoxia and hypercapnia are described as the strongest stimulators of the vasomotor centre.
- This is called the “last-ditch resort” mechanism. If it fails to restore BP, death follows.


#### CUSHING REFLEX
- indirect activation of the CNS ischemic response, triggered by increased intracranial pressure (“intracranial tension” in the source).
1. Activation of the **VMC increases BP** (and pulse pressure), activating baroreceptors that attempt to inhibit the VMC and activate the CIC. The VMC is not inhibited because it is already activated.
2. **CIC** activation decreases HR, causing **reflex bradycardia** with hypertension.
3. Increased CSF pressure can compress the medullary respiratory centre and microvasculature, producing <u>brain hypoxia</u> and  **irregular ventilation**.
- The triad of **hypertension, bradycardia and irregular ventilation** is called the Cushing reflex.


### LOW-PRESSURE BARORECEPTOR
- Low-pressure baroreceptors are located in low-pressure regions and are also called volume receptors (detecting volume change more than pressure change) or cardiopulmonary baroreceptors.
- Sites shown/listed in the source include the right atrium, pulmonary arterial wall and left ventricle.

### LOW-PRESSURE BARORECEPTORS
- Stretch of the right atrial wall due to increased venous return activates atrial/pulmonary arterial receptors. The source associates this with activation of the Bainbridge reflex, decreased ADH secretion and increased ANP production by atrial myocytes.
- Activation of ventricular receptors (left ventricle > septum) activates the Bezold–Jarisch reflex, mainly through chemical signals.
#### Types

| Feature   | Bainbridge reflex                                                  | Bezold–Jarisch reflex                                                                                                                              |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Receptors | Atrial stretch receptors sensitive to blood volume/↑ venous return | Ventricular wall receptors (mechanosensitive and chemosensitive)                                                                                   |
| Activator | **Stretch of right atrium** (↑ blood volume)                       | Chemicals in the left ventricular wall: veratridine, *serotonin*, *capsaicin*, *nicotine*, *bradykinin* and *ANP* → **Ventricular Chemoreceptors** |
| Afferent  | Vagus nerves to medulla                                            | Unmyelinated components of the vagus nerve (C fibres)                                                                                              |
| Response  | ↑ HR; ↑ BP (pumps additional blood returning to the heart)         | ↓ sympathetic outflow → ↓ HR, BP and coronary vasodilation                                                                                         |

#### BAINBRIDGE REFLEX
- Stretch of the right atrial wall, especially receptors at the venoatrial junction, activates low-pressure baroreceptors.
- Afferent signals travel via the vagus nerve to the medulla; the source then describes an efferent parasympathetic system that is inhibited, producing an increase in heart rate (60% as given in the source).
- The Bainbridge reflex is an increase in HR during stretching of the right atrium.
- In experimental animals, infusion of a volume of saline increased HR. Cutting the vagus nerve abolishes the reflex component, but HR still increases.
- The source attributes the remaining increase to direct stretch of the SA node by high venous return: the prepotential slope of the action potential increases, opening an ion channel and increasing the activation rate of the SA node, thereby increasing HR.
- The source attributes a 60% increase in HR to the Bainbridge reflex and a 15% increase to direct stretch of the SA node.

#### **Clinical scenarios for activation of the Bezold–Jarisch reflex**
- Myocardial ischemia.
- Hypotension during coronary reperfusion.
- Coronary angiography using contrast media.
- Exertional syncope in aortic stenosis.
- Spinal anaesthesia-induced hypotension.
- Vasovagal syncope.

### SUMMARY
**Case I: infusion of a large amount of fluid**
- ↑ ECF.
- In a hypervolemic state, the source says the Bainbridge reflex is dominant over the baroreceptor reflex and HR increases.

**Case II: severe loss of ECF volume**
- ↓ ECF.
- In a hypovolemic state, the source says the baroreceptor reflex is more dominant than the Bainbridge reflex and HR increases.

**Q1.** A 65-year-old male patient visited the ER for chest pain. His BP was measured as SBP 190 mmHg and DBP 100 mmHg, with a pulse pressure of 90 mmHg. Which parameter should decrease for this high pulse pressure? (AIIMS 2020 May)
- a. Arterial wall compliance
- b. Stroke volume
- c. Cardiac output
- d. Myocardial contractility
- **Answer: (a)**

**Q2.** When a person changes posture from supine to standing: (AIIMS 2020 Nov)
- a. Increase in firing from baroreceptor
- b. Decrease in firing from baroreceptor
- c. Vagal output to cardiac fibres increases
- d. Mesenteric vasoconstriction
- e. Increased cardiac contractility
- **Answer: (b, d, and e are correct)**

- a. Increase in venous pressure
- b. Right atrial engorgement
- c. Injection of saline in left atrium
- d. Serotonin
