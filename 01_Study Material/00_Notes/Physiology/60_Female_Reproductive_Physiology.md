# 60. Female Reproductive Physiology

## Menstrual cycle

- The menstrual cycle follows an **infradian rhythm** (cyclicity greater than 24 hours).
- The interval between two menstrual cycles is approximately **28 days**.
- During the menstrual cycle:
  - Changes in the uterine endometrium form the **uterine cycle**.
  - Parallel changes in the ovary form the **ovarian cycle**.

| Uterine cycle | Ovarian cycle |
|---|---|
| 1. Shedding of endometrium → menstrual bleeding | 1. Follicular development |
| 2. Proliferation of endometrium | 2. Ovum released from the mature follicle → ovulation |
| 3. Secretory activity of endometrium (glycogen-containing secretions) | 3. Developing follicle after ovulation forms corpus luteum |

## Estrous cycles

- Cyclic appearance of behavioural sexual activity (estrus) occurs in mammals such as dogs, cows, sheep, deer and horses, except for higher primates.
- Shedding of endometrium (menses) does not occur in the estrous cycle.
- Body temperature increases.
- Receptivity of the ovum for fertilization increases.
- Cycle length listed: **21 days**.

## Phases of the menstrual cycle

| Uterine phase | Days | Ovarian phase | Days |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Menstrual phase | 1–4 | Follicular phase | 1–14 |
| Proliferative phase | 6–14 | Ovulation | 14 |
| Secretory phase | 15–28 | Luteal phase | 15–28 |

### Changes through the cycle

- Follicular development is controlled by LH and FSH.
- Follicular cells produce large amounts of estrogen, leading to endometrial proliferation.
- Estrogen and progesterone generally exert negative feedback on LH at the pituitary level.
- If estrogen levels rise to more than three times normal and persist for more than two days, positive feedback on LH occurs, triggering the **LH surge → ovulation**.
- After ovulation, the follicle transforms into the corpus luteum.
- LH stimulates luteinizing cells of the corpus luteum, promoting progesterone synthesis.
- After ovulation, progesterone is greater than estrogen.
- Progesterone maintains the secretory phase of the endometrium, making glands more tortuous and glycogen-rich.
- High progesterone inhibits LH by negative feedback, leading to corpus luteum regression, progesterone decline and endometrial shedding (menstruation).

## Hormonal changes

- **Before ovulation:** estradiol rises to a high level → LH surge.
- **After ovulation:** corpus luteum produces large amounts of progesterone → decreased LH → decreased progesterone → menstruation.

## Menstruation

- When the corpus luteum regresses, declining progesterone and estradiol lead to endometrial breakdown.
- Necrotic foci develop in the endometrium and merge.
- Spiral artery spasm and degeneration occur, causing haemorrhage and menstrual flow.

**Important information**

- The corpus luteum has a fixed lifespan of **14 days** (secretory/luteal phase).
- If fertilization occurs, it persists for up to **8 weeks**, secreting high levels of progesterone to maintain pregnancy.
- After 8 weeks, the placenta takes over progesterone production.

## Mechanism of ovulation

1. Luteinizing hormone (LH).
2. Follicular steroid hormones (progesterone).
3. Proteolytic enzymes (collagenase) → weakened follicle wall.
4. Follicular hyperemia and prostaglandin secretion → plasma transudation into the follicle.
5. Follicle swelling.
6. Follicle rupture.
7. Release of ovum.

## Ovulation

- In a woman with a normal 28-day female sexual cycle, ovulation occurs 14 days after the onset of menstruation.
- The source states:
  - “After 83 hours of Estradiol rise.”
  - About 24–36 hours of estradiol peak.
- Ovulation occurs:
  - 24–36 hours (Ganong's Physiology) or 34–36 hours (Dutta's Obstetrics) after onset of the LH surge.
  - About 9 hours (Ganong's Physiology) or 10–12 hours (Dutta's Obstetrics) after the LH peak.
- About 8 hours of progesterone rise.
- Secretory/luteal phase: fixed at 14 days.
- Proliferative/follicular phase: duration varies according to the menstrual cycle.

## Follicular development

- At birth: **1–2 million primordial follicles**.
  - Primordial follicles have a single layer of granulosa cells surrounding an ovum.
- At puberty: **3 lakh primary follicles**.
  - More granulosa cells surround the ovum.
- During every menstrual cycle:
  - Primary follicle → preantral follicle.
  - 6–12 follicles proliferate → one dominant follicle; 5–11 undergo atresia.
  - Dominant follicle → antral follicle → preovulatory mature follicle (Graafian follicle).
- LH acts on the mature Graafian follicle → follicle rupture → ovulation (release of ovum).
- During rupture, a blood vessel enters the follicle → corpus haemorrhagicum → corpus luteum.
- After 14 days, the corpus luteum dies and the corpus albicans remains.

## Ovarian steroidogenesis: follicular phase

- LH receptors are present in both theca and granulosa cells.
- FSH receptors are present only in granulosa cells.
- Aromatase activity is absent in theca cells and present in granulosa cells.
- During follicular development:
  - LH stimulates outer theca cells.
  - LH receptor is a G-protein-coupled receptor → cAMP production.
  - Theca cells take up LDL cholesterol from blood vessels via LDL receptors.
  - Progesterone is produced from cholesterol in theca cells.
  - Estrogen is produced only in granulosa cells due to aromatase.
- Estrogen production in the follicular phase requires both theca and granulosa cells: the **two-cell, two-gonadotropin hypothesis**.
- During the follicular phase: estrogen > progesterone → estrogen causes endometrial proliferation.
- Theca cells come into contact with ovarian capillaries.

**Follicular-phase ovarian steroidogenesis**

## Ovarian steroidogenesis: luteal phase

- The follicle has ruptured.
- Both theca and granulosa cells are in contact with capillaries.
- Cholesterol is taken up by both theca and granulosa cells.
- Granulosa cells also produce progesterone.
- This progesterone is not converted to androstenedione; it is released directly into blood.
- In the luteal phase: progesterone ≫ estrogen.

## Hypothalamic control

- Progesterone and estrogen from theca and granulosa cells exert negative feedback on LH at the pituitary and hypothalamus.
- Granulosa cells produce:
  - **Inhibin:** negative feedback on FSH at the pituitary.
  - **Activin:** stimulatory effect on FSH at the pituitary.
- During most of the menstrual cycle, progesterone and estrogen exert negative feedback on the pituitary and hypothalamus.
- Just before ovulation, a surge in progesterone produces positive feedback → LH surge.

## Estrogen versus progesterone

| Feature | Estrogen | Progesterone |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural forms** | Estradiol (E2): main during reproductive period; estriol (E3): main during pregnancy; estrone (E1): main during menopause | C21 progesterone and 17-α-hydroxyprogesterone |
| **Secreted from** | Granulosa cells, corpus luteum and placenta | Corpus luteum, placenta and ovarian follicle |
| **Effects on female genitalia** | Endometrium: stimulates proliferation. Uterine tubes: increased motility and secretion. Cervical mucus: clear, watery, elastic secretion (Spinnbarkeit). | Endometrium: stimulates secretion and tortuous-gland development. Uterine tubes: decreased motility and increased secretion. Cervical mucus: scanty, thick mucus. |
| **Female secondary sex characteristics** | Enlargement of breasts (duct and stroma), uterus and vagina (pubertal changes); female distribution of fat | Breast enlargement (growth of lobules and alveoli) |
| **Effects on CNS** | Increases libido (direct hypothalamic effect); proliferation of dendrites on neurons and increased number of synaptic knobs | Thermogenic; responsible for the rise in basal body temperature at ovulation |
| **Effects on endocrine organs** | Increased secretion of angiotensinogen and thyroid-binding globulin; epiphyseal closure in humans | Large doses inhibit LH secretion and potentiate the inhibitory effect of estrogens, preventing ovulation |
| **Other effects** | Salt and water retention; fluid secretion from sebaceous glands (prevents acne); decreases plasma LDL and increases HDL | Blocks aldosterone action (natriuresis); increases plasma LDL and decreases HDL |

### Important information

- Women have less body hair, more scalp hair and a characteristic pubic-hair distribution; the source attributes this pattern to estrogen.
- Growth of pubic and axillary hair in both sexes is primarily due to androgens.
- Gonadal steroids (testosterone in boys and estradiol in girls) are the major hormones for bone growth and osseous maturation.
