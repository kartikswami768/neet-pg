# 53. Mechanism of Hormone Action

## Hormone

### Older definition
Substances secreted by various endocrine glands and transported in the bloodstream to distant target organs.

### Recent definition
Chemical substances having a specific regulatory effect on the activity of a certain organ or organs. Hormones are signaling molecules.

## Cell signaling

| Type | Mechanism | Example(s) given in the source |
|---|---|---|
| **Endocrine** | Hormone is secreted into the bloodstream, reaches a distant organ, leaves the blood vessel, and acts on receptors at distant target cells. | T3, T4 |
| **Autocrine** | A hormone secreted by a cell acts on the same cell by binding to its own receptors. | Platelet-activating factor |
| **Paracrine** | The target cell is adjacent to the secretory cell. Hormones reach the target cell by diffusion. | Somatostatin secreted by gastric D cells reaches parietal cells by diffusion and inhibits parietal cell secretion. |
| **Juxtacrine** | Signaling and target cells are so close that the ligand expressed by the signaling cell directly contacts the receptor on the target cell. | Transforming growth factor-α (TGF-α) |

## Regulation of hormone secretion

### Feedback control
- **Negative feedback:** The typical regulatory mechanism for hormone secretion. Thyroid hormone secretion is stimulated by TSH, but excess thyroid hormone exerts negative feedback on TSH secretion.
- **Positive feedback:** Less common, but important in certain physiological processes. Example: the LH surge just before ovulation.

### Rhythmic control
- **Circadian/diurnal rhythm:** Hormonal secretion following a 24-hour cycle.
  - ACTH and cortisol secretion peak in the morning, decline at night, and peak again the next morning.
  - The interval between secretion peaks is 24 hours.
  - The sleep–wake cycle is synchronized with the light–dark cycle.
  - When the circadian rhythm is influenced by the light–dark cycle, the source calls it a **diurnal rhythm**.
- **Ultradian rhythm:** A biological rhythm with a cycle shorter than 24 hours.
  - Multiple small peaks of ACTH secretion occur throughout the day, at intervals shorter than 24 hours.
  - Feeding cycle follows an ultradian pattern.
- **Infradian rhythm:** A biological rhythm lasting longer than 24 hours.
  - Example: menstrual cycle.

## Types of hormones

| Type | Features and examples |
|---|---|
| **Steroids** | Cholesterol is the raw material for synthesis. Examples: glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, androgens, estrogens and progesterone. |
| **Amino-acid derivatives** | Catecholamines and thyroid hormones are derived from tyrosine, but their properties differ. Thyroid hormones resemble steroid hormones in being lipophilic and acting through intracellular receptors. Serotonin is derived from tryptophan. |
| **Proteins and peptides** | Hormones synthesized by rough endoplasmic reticulum. Examples: pituitary hormones, pancreatic hormones, parathyroid hormones and erythropoietin. |

## Hormone receptors

### Protein hormones
- Protein hormones circulate freely in blood.
- They cannot penetrate the cell membrane when they reach the target cell.
- A receptor must therefore be present on the cell membrane.
- The connection between the membrane receptor and cytoplasm uses second-messenger systems (cAMP, IP₃), leading to phosphorylation/dephosphorylation of cellular proteins; this produces the hormone's action.

### Steroid and thyroid hormones
- Easily cross the cell membrane by simple diffusion.
- Receptors may be in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
- If the receptor is cytoplasmic, the hormone–receptor complex must move to the nucleus.
- **Genomic action (major mechanism):** Acts through the nucleus by modifying gene expression.
- **Non-genomic action (faster response):** Some steroid hormones bind to cell-membrane receptors and trigger production of a second messenger. Example given: MAP kinase activation by estrogen, leading to vasodilation in the brain. The source states that this is faster than genomic action.

## G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)

### Structure and components
- G-protein is a heterotrimeric protein composed of α, β and γ subunits.
- The α and γ subunits remain anchored to the plasma membrane via a lipid chain.
- G-proteins bind guanosine diphosphate (GDP) or guanosine triphosphate (GTP):
  - GDP-bound → inactive G-protein.
  - GTP-bound → active G-protein.

### Activation process
1. **Inactive state:** When no ligand is bound to the receptor, the G-protein remains inactive (GDP-bound) and distant from the receptor.
2. **Ligand binding and activation:** Ligand binding induces a conformational change in the receptor, attracting the G-protein. GDP is replaced with GTP, activating the G-protein.
3. **Separation of subunits:** The α-subunit dissociates from the βγ complex and moves toward the effector molecule. The effector is activated, triggering production of second messengers such as cAMP, IP₃ and DAG.

### Inactivation
- Once separated from the βγ units, the α-subunit has intrinsic GTPase activity, which hydrolyses GTP to GDP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
- When GDP binds back to the α-subunit, it regains affinity for the βγ complex, reforming the inactive G-protein.

### Prevention of reactivation
- Once activated, the receptor undergoes phosphorylation, preventing it from attracting additional G-proteins.
- This phosphorylation is carried out by G-protein receptor kinase (GRK).
- Arrestin binds to the phosphorylated receptor, effectively terminating G-protein signaling.

## GPCR effectors and second messengers

### Adenylyl cyclase
- Gs (Gαs) activates adenylyl cyclase.
- Gi (Gαi) inhibits adenylyl cyclase.
- Activation of adenylyl cyclase produces the second messenger **cAMP**.
- cAMP activates or inhibits protein kinase A (PKA), leading to phosphorylation/dephosphorylation of proteins and formation of phosphoproteins; hormonal effects follow.
- If PKA enters the nucleus, it can activate CREB (cAMP response element-binding protein) and promote gene expression.
- An atypical action is described in some brain regions, e.g. the hippocampus during memory consolidation.
- The source mentions another G-protein, **G12/13**, and states that its function is not known yet.

**Adenylyl cyclase, cAMP, PKA and CREB pathway**

### Phospholipase C
- Phospholipase Cβ is activated by Gq.
- Activation produces the second messengers **IP₃ and DAG**.
- DAG acts on protein kinase C (PKC), producing phosphoproteins and cellular effects.
- IP₃ binds receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum, releasing Ca²⁺.
- Ca²⁺ forms a Ca–calmodulin complex, leading to kinase activation, phosphoprotein formation and cellular effects.

## Other membrane-receptor pathways

### Guanylyl cyclase receptor
- Activated by **ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide)** and **NO (nitric oxide)**.
- The receptor is attached to the effector enzyme guanylyl cyclase.
- Guanylyl cyclase produces cGMP, which activates cGMP-dependent kinase.
- This causes phosphorylation of intracellular proteins and cellular effects.
- The source's receptor summary also lists **EDRF (endothelium-derived relaxation factor)** and nitric oxide with the guanylyl cyclase receptor.

### Tyrosine kinase receptor
- The intracellular domain of the receptor contains tyrosine kinase activity.
- <u>Ligand binding causes two receptors to aggregate <strong>(dimerization)</strong>.</u>
- The receptor molecules phosphorylate one another <strong>(autophosphorylation</strong>).
- Autophosphorylation is followed by <strong>phosphorylation of other cytoplasmic proteins,</strong> including Ras–Raf–MAP kinase proteins.
- <strong>Ras–Raf–MAPK activation</strong> is associated in the source with <u>epidermal growth factor (EGF) and nerve growth factor (NGF).</u>
- When the activating hormone is insulin or insulin-like growth factor (IGF), intracellular phosphorylation of other proteins occurs; AKT and MAP kinase pathways are named.
>Homodimerization → autophosphorylation → phosphorylation of other cytoplasmic proteins.

### Cytokine receptor
- Similar to the tyrosine kinase receptor.
- Ligand attachment causes homodimerization.
- There is no receptor autophosphorylation; intracellular proteins such as JAK–STAT are phosphorylated.
- Hormones listed: growth hormone (GH), prolactin and leptin.

### Serine kinase receptor
- The intracellular domain contains serine kinase activity.
- It phosphorylates small cytoplasmic proteins called SMADs.
- Hormones/substances listed as acting through SMADs: Müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS) and activin; TGF-β is included in the receptor summary table.

### Hormone receptor summary

| Receptor type | Messenger/signaling pathway | Hormones or mediators listed |
|---|---|---|
| Guanylyl cyclase receptor | ↑ cGMP | ANP, EDRF, nitric oxide |
| Receptor tyrosine kinase | MAP kinases, AKT | Insulin, IGF-I |
| Receptor tyrosine kinase | Ras, Raf, MAPK | EGF, NGF |
| Cytokine receptor | JAK–STAT | GH, prolactin, leptin |
| Serine kinase | SMADs | Activin, TGF-β, MIS |

## Steroid hormones and intracellular receptors

- Steroid hormones are lipophilic and cross the plasma membrane by simple diffusion.
- Receptors may be in the cytoplasm or directly inside the nucleus.
- **Cytoplasmic receptor:** hormone binds the receptor, the hormone–receptor complex dimerizes and enters the nucleus.
- **Nuclear receptor:** hormone enters the nucleus and forms a hormone–receptor complex there.
- In either case, the complex upregulates or downregulates gene expression (genomic action).

### Type I steroid hormone receptor
- Receptors are in the cytoplasm in the absence of hormone/ligand.
- The receptor is associated with a heat-shock protein (HSP), described as a chaperone protein.
- When ligand approaches, the HSP separates from the receptor.
- The hormone–receptor complex associates with another complex, forms a homodimer and translocates to the nucleus.
- The complex acts on DNA and regulates gene expression.

**Type I steroid hormone receptor**

![[ch53_p370_type_i_steroid_receptor.png]]

### Type II steroid hormone receptor
- The receptor is already inside the nucleus, attached to part of DNA.
- Another receptor, RXR (retinoic acid receptor, as named in the source), is present; the two form a heterodimer.
- In the absence of hormone, a corepressor suppresses the heterodimer, preventing gene upregulation/downregulation.
- When hormone enters the nucleus, the corepressor is removed.
- Coactivators are then required; they bind the heterodimer and allow gene expression.
- Hormones listed: thyroid hormone, vitamin A and vitamin D.
