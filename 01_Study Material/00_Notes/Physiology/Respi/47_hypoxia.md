# Hypoxia

## Definition

- **Hypoxia:** decreased oxygen supply at the tissue level.
- **Hypoxaemia:** decreased arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO₂).

## Types of hypoxia

### Hypoxic hypoxia

- Definition: caused by decreased PaO₂.
- Causes listed:
  - High altitude → low atmospheric O₂ levels.
  - Hypoventilation.
  - Lung fibrosis/oedema → impaired O₂ diffusion.
  - Right-to-left shunt → reduced oxygenated blood flow.

### Anaemic hypoxia

- Reduced haemoglobin or reduction in its oxygen-carrying capacity.
- Causes:
  - Anaemia → reduced haemoglobin.
  - Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning → CO binds to haemoglobin.
  - Methaemoglobinaemia → ferric haemoglobin (Met-Hb) has reduced O₂-binding capacity. The source adds that Met-Hb can still bind some O₂ but has significantly reduced affinity.

### Stagnant / ischaemic hypoxia

- Slow or reduced blood flow to tissues.
- Causes listed:
  - Severe haemorrhage → decreased blood volume.
  - Heart failure → insufficient pumping.
  - Shock → inadequate blood delivery.

### Histotoxic hypoxia

- Tissues cannot utilise oxygen.
- Causes listed:
  - Cyanide poisoning → enzyme inhibition in mitochondria.
  - Beriberi disease → vitamin B₁ deficiency affects the electron transport chain.

## Features of hypoxia

### Hypoxic hypoxia

- ↓PaO₂ → ↓ haemoglobin oxygen saturation (SaO₂) → ↓ Hb-bound O₂.
- ↓PaO₂ → ↓ dissolved O₂ (DO₂).
- Decreased SaO₂ and DO₂ → decreased arterial blood oxygen content → tissue-level hypoxia.
- ↓CaO₂ leads to ↓ tissue oxygen uptake, causing ↓CvO₂.
- Arteriovenous O₂ difference: **normal**.

### Anaemic hypoxia

- PaO₂ and DO₂: normal.
- SaO₂: normal, except in the CO-poisoning discussion below.
- Total Hb-bound oxygen is decreased because of a low haemoglobin concentration.
- ↓CaO₂ and ↓CvO₂.
- Arteriovenous O₂ difference: **normal**.
- Exception — CO poisoning:
  - ↓SaO₂ → ↓CaO₂, as stated in the source.
  - The source states that pulse oximetry gives a falsely normal SpO₂ value in CO poisoning.

### Stagnant hypoxia

- PaO₂, SaO₂, DO₂, Hb–O₂, and CaO₂: normal.
- ↓CvO₂ due to continuous extraction of O₂ by tissues from stagnant blood.
- Arteriovenous O₂ difference: increased.

### Histotoxic hypoxia

- PaO₂, SaO₂, DO₂, Hb–O₂, and CaO₂: normal.
- CvO₂ is higher than normal because tissue is unable to utilise oxygen.
- Arteriovenous O₂ difference: decreased.

## Hypoxia and chemoreceptor stimulation

- Location listed: carotid and aortic bodies.
- The source states that decreased DO₂ stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors.

| Type of hypoxia | Chemoreceptor response described in the source |
|---|---|
| Hypoxic hypoxia | Decreased DO₂ → stimulated. |
| Anaemic hypoxia | Normal DO₂ → not stimulated. |
| Stagnant hypoxia | Reduced blood flow to carotid/aortic bodies → decreased DO₂ → stimulated. |
| Histotoxic hypoxia | Defective glomus-cell electron transport chain (ETC) → stimulated. |

## Hypoxia and cyanosis

Cyanosis is described as bluish/purplish discoloration of the skin or mucous membranes due to an increase in deoxygenated haemoglobin in blood or a structural defect in the haemoglobin molecule.

- Deoxyhaemoglobin: **> 4 g%**.
- Met-Hb: **> 1.5 g%**.
- Sulf-Hb: **> 0.5 g%**.

### Arterial and venous blood values

| Variable | Arterial blood | Venous blood |
|---|---:|---:|
| O₂ saturation | SaO₂ = 97% | SvO₂ = 75% |
| Deoxyhaemoglobin | 3% / 0.45 g% | 15% / 3.75 g% |

- Capillary deoxyhaemoglobin calculation given in the source: `(0.45 + 3.75) / 2 = 2.1 g%`.
- Capillary deoxyhaemoglobin above 4 g% is associated with bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes.

### Cyanosis by hypoxia type

- **Hypoxic hypoxia:** SaO₂ decreases → cyanosis +++.
- **Anaemic hypoxia:** SaO₂ normal and SvO₂ decreased → cyanosis is rare.
  - CO poisoning → cherry-red discoloration (the source adds “not present” in this context).
  - Met-Hb → cyanosis-like appearance.
- **Stagnant hypoxia:** cyanosis +++.
- **Histotoxic hypoxia:** normal SaO₂ and SvO₂ → cyanosis is never seen.

## Important points

- The source identifies hypoxic hypoxia due to V/Q mismatch as the most common form of hypoxia.
- Brain areas listed as most sensitive to hypoxia:
  - Hippocampus (CA1 neurons or Sommer’s sector).
  - Cerebellum (Purkinje cells).
  - Basal ganglia (striatum).
  - Neocortex (pyramidal neurons).
- Most resistant CNS areas to hypoxia: lower brainstem and spinal cord.
