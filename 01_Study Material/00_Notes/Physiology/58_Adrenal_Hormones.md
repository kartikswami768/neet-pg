# 58. Adrenal Hormones

## Adrenal gland

- Also called the **suprarenal gland**; located just above the kidney.
- Small gland, weighing around **6–11 g**.
- Cortex is superficial to the medulla.
- Cortex layers, from superficial to deep:
  - **Zona glomerulosa:** ball-like cell arrangement.
  - **Zona fasciculata:** cords/bundles of cells.
  - **Zona reticularis:** net-like cells.
- The medulla is innermost.

## Hormones of the adrenal gland

| Region | Layer / proportion | Hormones listed | Main regulator listed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cortex (72%) | Zona glomerulosa (15%) | Mineralocorticoids; mainly aldosterone; deoxycorticosterone | Angiotensin II |
| Cortex (72%) | Zona fasciculata (50%) | Mainly glucocorticoids: cortisol, corticosterone; sex steroids | ACTH |
| Cortex (72%) | Zona reticularis (7%) | Mainly sex steroids: dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), androstenedione; glucocorticoids | ACTH |
| Medulla (28%) | — | Epinephrine (90%); norepinephrine (10%) | Sympathetic system |

Mnemonic for cortical zones: **GFR**.

## Aldosterone

- Main site of action: collecting duct of the nephron.
- Mainly acts on principal (P) cells and intercalated cells.
  - **Principal cells:** reabsorb Na⁺ into blood via epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; secrete K⁺ via ROMK (renal outer medullary potassium) channel into urine.
  - **Intercalated cells:** secrete H⁺ into urine via H⁺-ATPase.
- Increased aldosterone causes increased blood Na⁺, decreased blood K⁺ and alkalosis.

### Disorders of excess aldosterone production

| Disorder | Causes / mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Primary hyperaldosteronism** | Adrenal adenoma secreting aldosterone (Conn's syndrome); bilateral adrenal hyperplasia. High aldosterone → decreased renin (increased Na⁺ absorption). |
| **Secondary hyperaldosteronism** | Cardiac failure, liver failure and nephrotic syndrome → decreased ECF (renal hypoperfusion) → increased renin → increased angiotensin II → increased aldosterone. |

### Clinical features of Conn's syndrome

- Increased Na⁺ reabsorption → increased ECF volume and hypertension.
- Increased K⁺ excretion → hypokalaemia.
- Increased H⁺ excretion → alkalosis.
- Blood pressure may rise by **more than 15–20 mmHg** (as printed in the diagram).
- Increased blood pressure stimulates ANP secretion.
- ANP blocks the ENaC channel, decreasing sodium reabsorption and increasing sodium excretion.
- Pressure natriuresis and diuresis occur → **no oedema**.
- Altered resting membrane potential (RMP) → muscle weakness.

## Glucocorticoids

### Circadian rhythm of CRH, ACTH and cortisol

- CRH, ACTH and cortisol follow a circadian rhythm.
- Maximum secretion: morning, around **8:00–8:30 AM**.
- Minimum secretion: midnight, around **12:15 AM**.

### ACTH production

- Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) is the precursor of ACTH.
- POMC has multiple fragments, including γ-MSH, ACTH and β-LPH.
- In anterior pituitary “troph cells,” POMC is fragmented into:
  - Amino-terminal fragment, further converted into γ-MSH.
  - ACTH, which produces cortisol by stimulating MC2R on the adrenal cortex.
  - β-lipotropin (β-LPH), further processed into γ-LPH (42–101) and β-endorphin (104–134; endogenous opioid which relieves pain).
- MC1R is present on melanocytes. Excess ACTH can stimulate MC1R and produce hyperpigmentation.

## Functions of cortisol

| System / process | Effects listed |
|---|---|
| **Carbohydrate metabolism** | Increases blood glucose (“adrenal diabetes”) by increasing hepatic gluconeogenesis, increasing secretion of glycogenolytic hormones (glucagon and epinephrine), and exerting an anti-insulin effect by inhibiting GLUT4 expression on cells |
| **Protein metabolism** | Increases proteolysis and decreases protein synthesis in peripheral tissues, particularly muscle, except liver; muscle protein breakdown supplies amino acids for hepatic gluconeogenesis and protein synthesis |
| **Lipid metabolism** | Increases lipolysis by activating hormone-sensitive lipase: triglycerides → free fatty acids and glycerol (used for gluconeogenesis). Fat breakdown occurs mostly at extremities and is deposited centrally, causing truncal obesity, buffalo hump and moon face. Extremity fat wasting produces thin skin and fragility of cutaneous blood vessels. |
| **Cardiovascular system** | Increases myocardial contractility (increased β₁ receptor and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, as listed); increases vascular reactivity to catecholamines and angiotensin II |
| **Blood cells** | Decreases basophils, eosinophils and lymphocytes; increases neutrophils, platelets and RBCs |
| **Anti-inflammatory agent** | Decreases basophils, eosinophils and lymphocytes; lymphocytes are sequestered in the reticuloendothelial system |
| **Immunosuppressive agent** | Decreases circulating lymphocytes by increasing apoptosis; inhibits IL-2 production and T lymphocytes responsible for cellular immunity |

### Permissive action of cortisol

- A small amount of cortisol is essential for some physiological effects of other hormones, such as glucagon and catecholamines.
- Effects listed:
  - Vasoconstriction and bronchodilation.
  - Lipolytic effect of catecholamines.
  - Calorigenic effects of glucagon.
- Cortisol itself does not produce these effects.

## Disorders of cortisol production

### Cushing's syndrome

- Clinical syndrome resulting from chronic exposure to excess glucocorticoids of any aetiology.

| Type | Pattern / causes listed |
|---|---|
| **ACTH-dependent** | Increased ACTH and increased cortisol. Cushing's disease: ACTH-producing pituitary adenoma (listed as 90%); ectopic ACTH production, e.g. lung carcinoma. |
| **ACTH-independent** | Cortisol excess with decreased ACTH due to negative feedback. Adrenal tumour (adenoma or carcinoma); iatrogenic cause (listed as most common). |

**Clinical features of Cushing's syndrome**

- Hair thinning and hirsutism.
- Acne and plethora.
- Moon face.
- Peptic ulcer.
- Loss of height and back pain due to compression fracture.
- Hyperglycaemia.
- Menstrual disturbance.
- May have exuberant callus with fractures.
- Osteoporosis.
- Tendency to infections with poor wound healing and little inflammatory response.
- Psychosis.
- Cataracts and mild exophthalmos.
- Hypertension.
- Centripetal obesity.
- Striae and decreased skin thickness.
- Wasting and weakness of proximal thigh muscles.
- Bruising.
- Excess cortisol acts on mineralocorticoid receptors → increased Na⁺ reabsorption → hypertension.

### Adrenal insufficiency

| Primary (Addison's disease) | Secondary |
|---|---|
| Inability of the adrenal cortices to produce sufficient adrenocortical hormones due to primary atrophy or injury of the cortices. Causes listed: autoimmune (most common, 80%), TB, HIV/AIDS, etc. | Consequence of dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary component of the HPA axis. |

### Clinical features of Addison's disease

| Cause | Signs and symptoms listed |
|---|---|
| **Glucocorticoid deficiency** | Hypoglycaemia; fatigue/lack of energy; weight loss and anorexia; muscle and joint pain; anaemia, lymphocytosis and eosinophilia |
| **Mineralocorticoid deficiency** | Hypotension, postural hypotension and dizziness; salt craving; hyperkalaemia and hyponatraemia |
| **Sex steroid deficiency** | Lack of energy; dry and itchy skin (women); loss of libido; loss of axillary and pubic hair |
| **ACTH excess (loss of negative feedback)** | Hyperpigmentation of skin and mucous membrane, especially areas exposed to friction and shear stress, due to MC1R stimulation; also seen in ectopic ACTH production and Cushing's disease |
