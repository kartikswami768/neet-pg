# 05 — Neuron & Nerve

## Nerve

A nerve consists of bundles of nerve fibres (neurons/axons), called **fascicles**. Multiple fascicles form a nerve.

- **Endoneurium:** connective-tissue covering around an axon.
- **Perineurium:** connective-tissue covering around each fascicle.
- **Epineurium:** loose connective-tissue covering around the entire nerve.
- Capillaries supplying blood and nutrition to the nerve are located between the fascicles.

### Blood-neuron barrier and blood-brain barrier

| Blood-neuron barrier                                                                                      | Blood-brain barrier (BBB)                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Function:** protects neurons from toxins and blood products.                                            | Formed by endothelial cells of brain capillaries.                                                                              |
| Formed by the perineurium and endothelium                                                                 | Continuous brain capillaries have endothelial gaps of less than 2 nm bridged by tight junctions, forming a continuous barrier. |
| Connective-tissue cells of the perineurium, with tight junctions, form a continuous blood-neuron barrier. | BBB is **not formed by astrocytes**. Astrocytes regulate BBB permeability.                                                     |

## Neuron

![[ch05_p032_single_neuron.png]]

### Structure and properties of a neuron

| Part | Properties |
|---|---|
| **Dendrites** | Afferent part; receives synaptic input from other neurons. |
| **Axon** | Efferent part; information flows from the cell body to other regions through axons. |
| **Soma (cell body)** | Contains **Nissl bodies**, aggregates of rough endoplasmic reticulum and free ribosomes. They provide protein-synthesising machinery, contain abundant ribosomal RNA (basophilic staining), and are never present within the axon. Greater neuronal activity is associated with more Nissl bodies; motor neurons are listed as having the maximum density. |
| **Axon hillock and initial segment** | The axon hillock is the part of the cell body attached to the axon. The first region after the hillock is the initial segment, 50–100 µm long. The myelin sheath follows the initial segment. |
| **Node of Ranvier** | Bare area of axon between two myelin sheaths; the text gives its length as approximately 1 µm. A single myelin segment is stated to be up to 1 mm long. |
| **Axon terminal** | At the end of the neuron; axon knobs/telodendria contain neurotransmitter vesicles and form synapses for transmission to other neurons. |

### Important information

- Total number of neurons in the central nervous system (CNS): approximately 100 billion; the stated exact number is 86 billion.
- Glial-cell number: 2–10 times the number of neuronal cells.
- Neuron:synapse ratio: `1:2000 synapses` in the CNS.
- Total number of synapses in the CNS: `2 × 10^14`.
- The source states that 40% of the total human genome is responsible for CNS development.
- The human genome is described as consisting of 19,000–23,000 “subgenes.”

### Functional areas of the neuron

| Functional area | Details |
|---|---|
| **Graded electrogenesis** | Generated at dendrites and soma. Examples: EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential) and IPSP (inhibitory postsynaptic potential). |
| **Action-potential initiation** | In sensory neurons: first node of Ranvier. In motor neurons: initial segment. |
| **Voltage-gated Na⁺ channel density — maximum** | Node of Ranvier: 2,000–12,000/µm²; initial segment: 350–500/µm²; soma/dendrites: 50–75/µm²; telodendria: 20–75/µm². |
| **Voltage-gated Na⁺ channel density — minimum** | Surface of myelin sheath: less than 25/µm². |

## Different types of neurons

![[ch05_p034_neuron_types.png]]

| Type | Properties and locations given in the source |
|---|---|
| **Unipolar neuron** | One nerve process extends from one cell body. Stated to occur only in invertebrates; in humans, the source lists the mesencephalic nucleus of the 5th cranial nerve. |
| **Pseudounipolar neuron** | One nerve process arises from one cell body and divides into two nerve processes. Present in sensory neurons, including dorsal-root-ganglion neurons. |
| **Bipolar neuron** | Two nerve processes arise from one cell body: one process resembles dendrites and the other resembles an axon. Present in the retina (synapses with rods and cones) and olfactory sensory epithelium. |
| **Multipolar neuron** | Multiple processes and one axon arise from the cell body. Listed in cerebellar Purkinje cells; a motor neuron is given as an example. |

## Axonal transportation

- Nissl bodies are described as organelles producing protein molecules.
- Protein molecules are packed into vesicles by Golgi bodies.
- Axonal transport occurs through the axoplasm; vesicles are transported from the cell body to the terminal end of the neuron.
- **Microtubules** are tubular structures in the cytoplasm and provide the pathway along which molecular motors move.
- A **molecular motor** transports a protein, packed molecule, or component from one end to another. The source states that the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump aids cargo transport, with packaged molecules attaching to molecular-motor proteins and moving along microtubules.

### Directions and motors

- **Anterograde transport** (+ end-directed): moves materials from the cell body towards the axon; based on **kinesin**.
- **Retrograde transport** (− end-directed): moves materials from the axon to the cell body; based on **dynein**.
- When cargo reaches the end of a microtubule, it is transferred to the cell membrane via myosin molecular motors on actin filaments.
  - Myosin types 5 and 1: (+) end-directed motors.
  - Myosin type 6: (−) end-directed motor.
  - Myosin type 2: muscle contraction.

![[ch05_p035_axonal_transport.png]]

### Types of axonal transport

| Type | Direction and speed | Mechanism | Transport material |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fast anterograde | Plus-end directed; approximately 400 mm/day | Kinesin | Vesicles and mitochondria |
| Fast retrograde | Minus-end directed; approximately 200 mm/day | Dynein | Toxins, growth factors and viruses |
| Slow anterograde | Plus-end directed; 0.5–10 mm/day | Not known | Components of microtubules and microfilaments |

## MCQs

### Multiple sclerosis

A 55-year-old female complains of numbness, blurred vision, fatigue and spasms. Investigations identify the autoimmune disease multiple sclerosis. Which cells are most commonly affected?

- a. Schwann cells
- b. Oligodendrocyte
- c. Astrocyte
- d. Microglia

**Answer: b. Oligodendrocyte.**

### Site of action-potential initiation

Action potential is initiated at the axon hillock/initial segment of the neuron because:

- a. Threshold for excitation is lowest
- b. Neurotransmitter is released at this site
- c. It is an unmyelinated segment
- d. It has the lowest concentration of voltage-gated sodium channels

**Answer: a. Threshold for excitation is lowest.**

### Axonal transport

Axonal transport is:

- a. Anterograde
- b. Retrograde
- c. Anterograde and retrograde
- d. None

**Answer: c. Anterograde and retrograde.**
