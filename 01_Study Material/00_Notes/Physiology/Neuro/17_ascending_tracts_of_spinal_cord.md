# 17. Ascending Tracts of Spinal Cord

## Ascending tracts

- Sensory fibres from the skin/receptor enter through the dorsal root of the spinal cord.
- After entering, these fibres ascend to the level of the medulla or thalamus, or may cross to the opposite side.
- A group of fibres (axons) in the spinal cord/CNS forms a **tract**.
- A collection of multiple cell bodies in the central nervous system is called a **nucleus**.
- The types of ascending tracts depend on their location within the spinal cord.

### Arrangement of fibres within the spinal cord

- **Dorsal column pathway:** The source describes its lateral-to-medial arrangement as cervical column fibres → thoracic → lumbar → sacral. It gives injury to the cervical dorsal-column region from lateral damage as an example.
- **Anterolateral spinothalamic tract:** The source states that cervical-region fibres are located in the innermost region and gives the inner-to-outer sequence as cervical → thoracic → lumbar → sacral. With lateral damage to the spinothalamic tract, the sacral-region fibres are stated to be damaged first, followed by lumbar → thoracic → cervical fibres.

## Dorsal column pathway

- The sensory neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal-root region and ascends directly to the medulla.
- **First-order neuron:** A single pseudounipolar neuron.
- **Second-order neuron:** The first-order neuron synapses with the cell body of another neuron; its axons cross to the opposite side. For example, fibres carrying sensation from the right side cross to the left and ascend towards the thalamus.
- **Third-order neuron:** From the thalamus, another neuron carries the skin sensation to the cortex.
- **Nucleus gracilis:** Fibres from the lower part of the body (e.g. legs) synapse with second-order neurons to form a nucleus in the medulla. The tract of gracilis contains fibres from the sacral, lumbar, and lower six thoracic regions.
- **Nucleus cuneatus:** Fibres from the upper part of the body synapse with second-order neurons to form a nucleus in the medulla. The tract of cuneatus contains fibres from all cervical and upper six thoracic regions.
- **Medial lemniscus pathway:** The ascending part of the second-order neuron travelling towards the thalamus.
- **Ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus:** The second-order neuron synapses in the thalamus with the cell body of the third-order neuron, which forms the VPL nucleus.

## Spinothalamic pathway

The pathway includes the **anterior spinothalamic tract** and **lateral spinothalamic tract**.

1. The first-order neuron ends directly at the spinal-cord level.
2. It synapses with the second-order neuron in the substantia gelatinosa (laminae 2 and 3, as stated in the source).
3. The second-order neuron crosses to the opposite side of the spinal cord.
4. It ascends towards the thalamus and synapses with the third-order neuron at the VPL nucleus.
5. The third-order neuron reaches the cortex.

- If the crossed second-order neuron ascends through the lateral region of the spinal cord, it forms the **lateral spinothalamic pathway**.
- If it ascends through the anterior region, it forms the **anterior spinothalamic tract**.

## Brown–Séquard syndrome

- Functional hemisection of the spinal cord, damaging one half of the cord.
- Pathways affected:
  - Ipsilateral dorsal-column pathway.
  - Contralateral spinothalamic tract.
  - Ipsilateral descending tract.
- **On the same side:** Loss of fine touch, proprioception, vibration, and tactile discrimination.
- **On the opposite side:** Loss of pain, temperature, crude touch, and pressure, as described in the source.
- **Motor deficit:** Damage to the corticospinal tract on the side of the lesion:
  - At the level of the lesion: LMN paralysis.
  - Below the level of the lesion: UMN paralysis.

## Syringomyelia

- **Syrinx:** A cavity; a lesion in and around the central canal of the spinal cord.
- **Myelia:** Degeneration of the spinal cord.
- With degeneration, the cavity enlarges.
- Enlargement damages second-order neurons of the spinothalamic tracts crossing in front of the canal for pain and temperature, without affecting dorsal-column fibres.
- It generally affects the cervical region of the spinal cord.
- In cervical syringomyelia, pain, temperature, crude touch, and pressure on both hands are not felt, while fine touch, proprioception, and vibration are perceived.
- **Dissociated sensory loss:** Pain and temperature are not perceived, but fine touch and proprioception remain perceivable.
- Motor paralysis may occur if anterior-horn cells are involved.

![[ch17_p124_syringomyelia_lesion.png]]

## Tabes dorsalis

- *Tabes* means wasting away; the source describes destruction of the dorsal root and dorsal-column fibres.
- May be bilateral.
- **Cause:** Neurosyphilis.
- Clinical features:
  - Loss of position and vibration senses.
  - Numbness and lightning pain (irritated pain fibres).
  - Sensory ataxia (disturbance of gait).
  - Diminished reflexes and weakness.

![[ch17_p124_tabes_dorsalis_cross_section.png]]

## Anterior spinal artery syndrome

- The anterior spinal artery supplies the anterior two-thirds of the spinal cord.
- Obstruction of the artery causes infarction, resulting in anterior myelopathy.
- The damaged area includes the crossing region for both the spinothalamic tract and the descending motor tract.
- Pain and temperature sensation are lost below the level of the lesion without affecting proprioceptive sensation.
- Spastic paralysis occurs due to involvement of the corticospinal tract.

## Sensory cortex

All sensory fibres end in the somatosensory area of the cerebral cortex.

![[ch17_p124_sensory_cortex_map.png]]

### Somatosensory cortical areas

| Area | Source description | Effects of damage listed in the source |
|---|---|---|
| **Somatosensory area I / primary somatosensory area** | Just behind the central sulcus; Brodmann areas 3, 1, 2; area for perception of sensation. Ascending tracts from the thigh, thorax, neck, shoulder, hand, fingers, tongue, and intra-abdominal regions terminate here. | Graphesthesia and stereognosis are lost earliest; fine touch and proprioception are most affected; localization is affected; inability to judge an object's weight or critical pressure. Pain and temperature are not lost because these are perceived at the thalamus (source wording). |
| **Somatosensory area II / secondary somatosensory area** | Brodmann area 40. | Function is stated as not known; body representation is inadequate. |
| **Somatosensory association area** | Brodmann areas 5, 7. | Neglect/hemineglect (neglect of one half of the body) and loss of complete body image (*amorphosynthesis*). |

### Homunculus

- **Homunculus (“the little man”):** Body-image representation at the somatosensory cortex; the body image is distorted at the sensory cortex.
- The source describes representation of the body in the vertical direction.

![[ch17_p125_motor_sensory_homunculus.png]]

- **Sensory homunculus:** Largest representation—face (lips), followed by hand; smallest representation—trunk/leg. The size depends on the number of sensory receptors in the area.
- **Motor homunculus:** Largest representation—vocalization muscles, followed by hand; smallest representation—trunk/leg. The size depends on the particular motor use of that area.

### Cortical plasticity

- The brain's ability to change and reorganize itself based on experience.
- If a digit is amputated, the cortical representation of neighbouring digits spreads into the cortical area.
- If the cortical area representing a digit is removed, the somatosensory map of that digit moves to the surrounding cortex.
- Cortical plasticity explains phantom-limb sensation.

## Laws in sensory physiology

| Law/theory | Description in the source |
|---|---|
| **Bell–Magendie law** | The dorsal root is sensory and the ventral root is motor. |
| **Labelled-line theory** | All sensations from different parts of the body travel along a specified pathway. |
| **Müller’s doctrine of specific nerve energies** | The nature of perception is defined by the pathway over which sensory information is carried. |
| **Law of projection** | Stimulation of a sensory system at any point central to the sense organs produces a sensation projected to the receptor, not to the point of stimulation. This is given as an explanation for phantom-limb sensation. |
| **Weber–Fechner law** | Relates the actual change in physical strength of a stimulus to the perceived change in intensity. |
