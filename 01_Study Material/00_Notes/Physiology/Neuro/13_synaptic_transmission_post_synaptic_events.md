| Excitatory | Inhibitory |
|---|---|

### Other neurotransmitters

Acetylcholine (ACh), norepinephrine, serotonin, dopamine, nitric oxide (NO) and carbon monoxide (CO).

#### Acetylcholine

- **Location:** Basal forebrain complex (septal nuclei and nucleus basalis, also called nucleus of Meynert).
- **Functions:** Regulates the sleep–wake cycle, learning and memory.

![[ch13_p086_acetylcholine_projection.png]]

#### Norepinephrine

- **Locations:** Locus coeruleus; pontine and medullary nuclei.
- **Function:** Part of the ascending reticular activating system; associated with the sleep–wake cycle and alertness.

![[ch13_p086_norepinephrine_projection.png]]

#### Serotonin

- **Location:** Raphe nuclei.
- The highest concentration is listed in platelets, enterochromaffin cells and the myenteric plexus of the gastrointestinal tract.
- Part of the ascending reticular activating system.

![[ch13_p086_serotonin_projection.png]]

#### Dopamine

- **Major source:** Substantia nigra.
- Neurons project to parts of the basal ganglia, including the caudate nucleus and putamen (striatum); this is called the **nigrostriatal system**.
  - Defect of the nigrostriatal system → Parkinson's disease.
- Dopamine is also associated with the mesocortical system (ventral tegmental area).
  - The source associates the mesocortical system with rewards, behaviour and addiction.
  - Overstimulation of D2 and D4 receptors in this system is associated in the source with schizophrenia.
  - Clozapine inhibits D4 receptors → improvement in schizophrenic symptoms.

![[ch13_p087_dopamine_pathways.png]]

#### NO and CO

- Very low nanomolar concentrations of CO and NO act as neurotransmitters.
- **Second messenger listed:** GMP.
- **Functions:**
  - NO → synaptic plasticity (memory and learning).
  - CO → olfaction, pain and long-term potentiation (memory formation).

#### Anandamide

- An endocannabinoid.
- **Second messenger listed:** cAMP.
- Acts on a cannabinoid receptor (Cb1).
- Can also be stimulated by tetrahydrocannabinol, a component of marijuana/cannabis.
- Anandamide has a modulatory effect on brain-rewarding circuitry and sexual-behaviour regulation.

## Glutamate receptors and glutamate neurotoxicity

Glutamate receptors on the postsynaptic membrane are classified as **metabotropic** and **ionotropic** receptors.

### Metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR)

- mGluR is a G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) on the postsynaptic membrane.
- Glutamate binds to mGluR → GPCR stimulation → changes in IP₃/DAG and cAMP (second messengers).
- IP₃/DAG → opens calcium channels in the cell membrane → depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.
- Reduced cAMP is described as closing potassium channels in the cell membrane → depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.

![[ch13_p087_metabotropic_glutamate_signaling.png]]

### Ionotropic glutamate receptors

The receptor itself is an ion channel. Glutamate binds to the channel receptor → the channel opens → Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ enter → depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.

Three subtypes are listed:

- **AMPA receptor** (*α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methylisoxazole-4-propionate*): activation → Na⁺ entry greater than K⁺ exit → net gain of positive charge.
- **Kainate receptor:** activation causes Na⁺ entry greater than K⁺ exit.
- **NMDA receptor** (*N-methyl-D-aspartate*): a depolarisation-induced channel.
  - Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ enter while K⁺ exits → depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.
  - At rest, NMDA is blocked by Mg²⁺ in the core of the receptor.
  - Positivity of the inner membrane during depolarisation repels Mg²⁺, opening the channel.
  - Glutamate activates AMPA and kainate receptors → Na⁺ enters and depolarises the postsynaptic membrane → Mg²⁺ is repelled from the NMDA receptor pore → the NMDA channel opens.
  - NMDA receptors are densely packed in the hippocampus and are associated in the source with memory formation.
  - Proper functioning of the NMDA receptor requires a small amount of glycine in the extracellular fluid. Glycine binds to the extracellular part of the NMDA receptor and facilitates channel opening.

![[ch13_p089_nmdar_mg_block_diagram.png]]

### Glutamate neurotoxicity/cytotoxicity

- Glutamate released from the presynaptic terminal stimulates NMDA receptors on the postsynaptic membrane → influx of Ca²⁺ and Na⁺.
- Glutamate along with Na⁺ is taken up by astrocytes; this is described as a protective phenomenon.
- Increased extracellular glutamate → Ca²⁺ entry into neuronal cells → activation of the neuronal apoptotic pathway → reactive oxygen species (ROS) → neuronal death.
- Glutamate-induced neurotoxicity/cytotoxicity is listed as a pathogenic mechanism in:
  - Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)
  - Alzheimer's disease (AD)
  - Parkinson's disease (PD)
  - Stroke
  - Multiple sclerosis
- **NMDA receptor antagonists listed:**
  - Riluzole for ALS
  - Memantine for AD
  - Amantadine for PD

![[ch13_p089_glutamate_neurotoxicity_diagram.png]]

## CNS synapses

### Excitatory synapse: excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)

- The presynaptic neuron contains glutamate, the major excitatory neurotransmitter; the postsynaptic membrane contains glutamate receptors.
- The source's receptor/ion summary is:
  - mGluR → Ca²⁺ entry.
  - AMPA/kainate receptor → Na⁺ entry.
  - NMDA receptor → Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ entry.
  - NMDA receptor is coupled with the AMPA receptor.
- **Resting membrane potential (RMP)** of the postsynaptic membrane: −70 mV.
- Entry of positively charged ions → gradual depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.
- Gradual depolarisation produces a local depolarisation that cannot travel to a distal part of the neuron.
- Increasing glutamate release → increased Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ influx → stepwise increase in positivity.
- If the postsynaptic potential reaches −55 mV (the neuronal threshold voltage) or above, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open at the initial segment → an action potential is generated and travels a long distance through the neuron.
- Local potential generated in the postsynaptic membrane → may lead to an action potential; this is described as EPSP.
- The source describes local potential, graded potential, non-propagative potential and decremental potential as properties of EPSP.

![[ch13_p090_epsp_diagrams.png]]

#### Graphical representation

After glutamate receptors are stimulated, a short latency precedes a gradual, stepwise rise in membrane potential. When threshold reaches −55 mV, an action potential is generated. If glutamate release is insufficient, EPSP is generated but subsequently decreases back to RMP.

#### Ionic basis of EPSP

Entry of Na⁺, entry of Na⁺/Ca²⁺, or closure of K⁺ channels.

#### Types of EPSP based on latency

| Type | Latency | Ionic basis | Location listed in source |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Fast EPSP | 0.5 ms | Entry of Na⁺ and/or Ca²⁺ | All parts of the CNS |
| Slow EPSP | 100–500 ms | Closure of K⁺ channels | Autonomic ganglia, cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and cortical neurons |
| Very slow/late slow EPSP | 1–5 s | Closure of K⁺ channels | Autonomic ganglia |

### Inhibitory synapse: inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)

- RMP of the postsynaptic membrane: −70 mV.
- GABA released from presynaptic neurons → Cl⁻ enters the postsynaptic membrane through GABA receptors and K⁺ exits through GABA receptors → local hyperpolarisation.
- The source states that local hyperpolarisation cannot produce an action potential in the postsynaptic membrane; the inhibitory potential is called IPSP.
- An action potential travelling through the presynaptic neuron stops at the synapse in inhibitory synaptic transmission.
- The source describes IPSP as a local potential, graded potential and “non-decremental potential”; it does not follow the all-or-none law.
- **Ionic basis:** Cl⁻ entry or K⁺ exit.

![[ch13_p091_ipsp_schematic.png]]

#### Types of IPSP based on latency

| Type | Latency | Ionic basis | Location listed in source |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Fast IPSP | 0.5 ms | Cl⁻ entry | All parts of the CNS |
| Slow IPSP | 100–500 ms | K⁺ exit | Autonomic ganglia, cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and cortical neurons |

The source contrasts CNS excitatory synapses (generate EPSP) with inhibitory synapses (generate IPSP).

## Peripheral nervous system synapses

### Neuromuscular transmission: neuromuscular junction (NMJ)

- The α motor neuron contains ACh at its terminal end.
- The synapse is formed between the motor-neuron terminal and the muscle membrane/sarcolemma.
- The depressed area of the sarcolemma is the **motor end plate**.
- The motor end plate has multiple invaginations to increase surface area and multiple ionotropic ACh receptors of the **Nm** type.

Sequence:

1. Action potential in the motor neuron → ACh release from the presynaptic terminal.
2. ACh binds to ionotropic ACh receptors on the motor end plate.
3. Na⁺ enters through ACh receptors → gradual depolarisation of the motor end plate.
4. When threshold for skeletal muscle (−65 mV) is crossed, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and a large amount of Na⁺ enters.
5. An action potential is generated (about +35 mV).
6. The action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and reaches T-tubules → depolarisation activates DHPR → activates RyR in the sarcoplasmic reticulum → Ca²⁺ release → muscle contraction.

![[ch13_p092_neuromuscular_junction.png]]

- **RMP of skeletal muscle:** −90 mV.
- **Threshold potential:** −65 mV.
- **Ionic basis:** Na⁺ entry.
- The action potential is generated by fast voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
- ACh-receptor-mediated Na⁺ entry produces only a local potential at the motor end plate, called the **motor end-plate potential (MEPP)**.
- MEPP is local, non-propagated and graded.

#### Disorders of neuromuscular transmission

| Myasthenia gravis | Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) |
|---|---|
| Autoimmune disorder. | Autoimmune attack against presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (P/Q and N types). |
| Anti-AChR antibodies reduce the number of available ACh receptors at the NMJ. | Antibodies predominantly cross-react with P/Q-type Ca²⁺ channels (CaV2.1); they can also cross-react with N-type channels (CaV2.2). |
| Source listed: thymus (myoid cells). The source explains that the body does not recognise muscle cells formed in the thymus and produces antibodies against them; the antibodies cross-react with ACh receptors at the NMJ. | Proximal muscles of the lower extremities are primarily affected → waddling gait and difficulty raising the arms. Repeated stimulation → improvement in muscle strength. |
| Repeated stimulation → muscle weakness, particularly at the end of the day. Most commonly affected muscles listed: ocular muscles. | — |

### Miniature end-plate potential

- A single action potential in an α motor neuron releases **60–125 (up to 200) ACh vesicles** from the presynaptic terminal and produces a motor end-plate potential with amplitude **+40 mV**.
- One vesicle contains **10,000 ACh** molecules.
- Without an action potential in the α motor neuron, a few ACh vesicles are spontaneously released → a postsynaptic potential with amplitude of only **0.5 mV**.
- A potential generated even at rest is the **miniature end-plate potential**; it is a normal membrane-potential fluctuation.

![[ch13_p093_miniature_endplate_potential.png]]

### Ganglionic transmission

- Presynaptic-neuron neurotransmitter: acetylcholine.
- Sympathetic postsynaptic-neuron neurotransmitter: epinephrine/norepinephrine (as listed in the source).
- Parasympathetic postsynaptic-neuron neurotransmitter: acetylcholine.
- An action potential in the preganglionic fibre → ACh release → acts on Nₙ-type receptors, M2, M1 and various peptide receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  - Stimulation of Nₙ, M1 and various peptide receptors by ACh → EPSP.
  - Stimulation of M2 by ACh → IPSP.
- In the source's graphical representation, the x-axis is time and the y-axis is postsynaptic-neuron membrane voltage. If the positive potential crosses −55 mV, an action potential is generated.
  - Fast EPSP → Nₙ receptor.
  - Fast IPSP → M2 receptor.
  - Slow EPSP → M1 receptor.
  - Late slow EPSP → various peptide receptors.

## Synaptic inhibition

### Direct inhibition (postsynaptic inhibition)

One neuron inhibits another without help from other neurons. GABA released from neuron A's presynaptic terminal → hyperpolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane of neuron B → inhibition of the synapse.

![[ch13_p094_direct_inhibition.png]]

### Indirect inhibition (presynaptic inhibition)

- Normally, neuron A stimulates neuron B through an excitatory synapse using glutamate.
- Inhibitory neuron C projects its axon to the presynaptic terminal of neuron A and inhibits it using GABA.
- Stimulation of neuron C → GABA release into the synaptic cleft → acts at the presynaptic terminal → Cl⁻ entry or K⁺ exit → hyperpolarisation of the presynaptic terminal.
- Hyperpolarisation → reduced action-potential amplitude → reduced Ca²⁺ entry into the presynaptic neuron → reduced glutamate release → insufficient depolarisation in the postsynaptic membrane → no action potential in the postsynaptic neuron.
- Presynaptic inhibition occurs at the **axo-axonic synapse** (described as rare).
- Picrotoxin (a GABA-receptor antagonist) inhibits presynaptic inhibition → the synapse becomes excitatory → convulsions.

![[ch13_p094_presynaptic_inhibition.png]]

### Feedback inhibition / Renshaw-cell inhibition / recurrent inhibition

- Occurs at the spinal-cord level.
- A brain neuron stimulates spinal-cord motor neurons → skeletal-muscle contraction.
- Motor neurons branch at the spinal-cord level; a branch stimulates an inhibitory interneuron (Renshaw cell), which inhibits the same motor neuron.
- Neurotransmitter of the Renshaw cell: **glycine**.
- This is described as typical feedback inhibition in the CNS and spinal cord.
- Tetanus toxin prevents glycine release from Renshaw cells → spasmodic contraction.

![[ch13_p094_renshaw_recurrent_inhibition.png]]

### Tetanus toxin and strychnine

| Tetanus toxin | Strychnine |
|---|---|
| Acts on the presynaptic terminal and prevents glycine release from the presynaptic terminal. | Glycine-receptor antagonist on the postsynaptic membrane. |
| Acts at inhibitory synapses in the spinal cord and also at the Renshaw-cell terminal. | Also acts on Renshaw cells. |
| Prevents self-inhibition → repeated motor-neuron stimulation → spasmodic muscle contraction (spastic paralysis). | Generalised muscle spasms. |

### Feedforward inhibition

- Typically seen in the cerebellum.
- Granular cell (cerebellum) stimulates basket/stellate cells → inhibition of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum.
- Inhibition occurs in the forward direction.

![[ch13_p095_cerebellar_feedforward_inhibition.png]]

## MCQs

**1.** A 40-year-old patient presents with ptosis and fatigue that worsen towards the end of the day. Which disease is likely?

- a. Myotonia congenita
- b. Malignant hyperthermia
- c. Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome
- d. Myasthenia gravis

**Answer:** (d)

**2.** Which statement is true for excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)?

- a. It is self-propagating.
- b. It shows an all-or-none response.
- c. It is proportional to the amount of transmitter released by the presynaptic neuron.
- d. It is inhibitory at the presynaptic terminal.

**Answer:** (c)

**3.** Which statement is true about presynaptic inhibition?

- a. It results from failure of the action potential to reach the synapse.
- b. It results from hyperpolarisation of the presynaptic membrane.
- c. It occurs due to inhibition of neurotransmitter release from the presynaptic terminal.
- d. It occurs due to blockade of neurotransmitter receptors.

**Answer as printed:** (c>b)
