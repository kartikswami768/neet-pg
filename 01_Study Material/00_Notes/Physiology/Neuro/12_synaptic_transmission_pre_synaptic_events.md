# 12. Synaptic Transmission: Pre-Synaptic Events

## Types of synapses

- The term **synapse** was first coined by **Charles Sherrington**.
- A synapse is a junction between two neurons.

### Functional classification

![[ch12_p079_electrical_chemical_synapses.png]]

| Feature | Electrical synapse | Chemical synapse |
|---|---|---|
| Synaptic cleft | 2–4 nm | 20–40 nm |
| Gap junctions | Present between presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons; the cytoplasms are described as continuous. | Absent. |
| Neurotransmitter-containing vesicles | Absent. | Present in presynaptic terminals. |
| Transmission | Action potentials (Na⁺) pass through gap junctions from pre- to postsynaptic membrane without synaptic delay. | An action potential triggers neurotransmitter release from the presynaptic neuron; neurotransmitters bind to postsynaptic receptors. This is described as triggering Ca²⁺ or Na⁺ release and generating an action potential in the postsynaptic neuron. |
| Delay | Nil | Approximately 0.5 ms (range 0.3–5 ms) |
| Direction | Bidirectional | Always unidirectional: presynaptic → postsynaptic |
| Sites listed | Inferior olivary nucleus (ION), thalamus, hypothalamus and cerebral cortex | Throughout the CNS; most synapses are chemical. |

### Anatomical classification

- **Axodendritic synapse:** between the axon of one neuron and the dendrite of another neuron; the most common type in the CNS.
- **Axosomatic synapse:** between the axon of one neuron and the cell body of another neuron.
- **Axo-axonal synapse:** between the axon of one neuron and the axon of another neuron.

## Synaptic transmission and presynaptic terminals

Synaptic transmission is divided into:

- **Presynaptic events:** vesicle release from the presynaptic neuron.
- **Postsynaptic events:** potential generation at the postsynaptic membrane.

EPSP/IPSP formation occurs on the postsynaptic neuron.

Presynaptic terminals contain large numbers of mitochondria and multiple vesicles filled with neurotransmitters. Based on microscopic appearance, vesicles are classified into three types:

| Vesicle type | Characteristics | Neurotransmitters listed |
|---|---|---|
| **Small clear** | Primarily recycled in the presynaptic terminal; axoplasmic transmission. | Glutamate, GABA, glycine and acetylcholine. |
| **Small dense core** | Primarily recycled in the presynaptic terminal; axoplasmic transmission. | Catecholamines. |
| **Large dense core** | Transported from the cell body to the axon terminal by axoplasmic transmission. | Neuropeptides, substance P, neuropeptide Y and cholecystokinin. |

## Active zone, postsynaptic density and synaptic specificity

- **Active zone of the presynaptic terminal:** the area from which the presynaptic terminal releases vesicular contents. It is particularly associated with small vesicles. Exception: large dense-core vesicles can release neurotransmitters from any part of the presynaptic terminal.
- **Postsynaptic density:** the area where receptors are densely packed on the postsynaptic membrane.
- **Synaptic specificity:** maintained by **neurexin**, a presynaptic-membrane protein, and **neuroligin**, a postsynaptic-membrane protein. They interact and make the synapse stronger. When neurotransmitters are released from a presynaptic terminal, they cannot enter a nearby synapse and must be in the same synaptic cleft. Neurexin and neuroligin are described as creating a boundary that prevents neurotransmitter from leaving the cleft and thereby maintains synaptic specificity.

## Steps of vesicle release

### 1. Docking

- Vesicles move closer to the presynaptic membrane.
- Vesicular-membrane proteins do not yet interact with presynaptic-membrane proteins.

### 2. Priming

- Both the presynaptic membrane and vesicles contain **SNARE proteins**.
- When a vesicle comes close to the presynaptic membrane, ATP facilitates interaction between vesicular- and presynaptic-membrane proteins, bringing the vesicle closer to the membrane.

### 3. Fusion and release

- Continued recoiling of the interacting proteins brings the membranes together.
- The vesicular membrane fuses with the presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitters.

## Fate of vesicles after neurotransmitter release

1. **Kiss and stay:** the vesicle releases its contents and remains in the presynaptic membrane.
2. **Kiss and run:** the vesicle releases its contents and returns to the vesicle pool for reuse; this is marked “M/c”.
3. **Kiss and full recycle:** the vesicle merges with an endosome; fresh vesicular budding occurs on the other side and the vesicle returns to docking.

## Molecular mechanism of vesicular release: SNARE proteins

- **SNARE** is expanded as “SNAP receptors.”
- **SNAPs** are soluble NSF (N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive fusion protein) attachment proteins.
- Two types of SNARE proteins are listed:
  - **V-SNARE (R-SNARE):** synaptobrevin, present on the vesicular membrane.
  - **t-SNARE (Q-SNARE):** syntaxin 1 and SNAP-25, present on the target/terminal membrane.
- **Docking:** the vesicle is close to the target membrane, but the proteins do not interact with one another.
- **Priming:** requires ATP and SM proteins (S = Sec1; M = Munc18). In the presence of ATP and SM protein, vesicular synaptobrevin interacts with presynaptic-membrane syntaxin with the help of SNAP-25. Increasing recoiling of synaptobrevin with syntaxin brings the vesicular membrane close to the presynaptic membrane.
- **Primary priming:** SNARE proteins interact with one another.
- **Secondary priming:** complexin is introduced into the SNARE complex. Complexin inhibits SNARE-protein recoiling, preventing release; the vesicle remains close to the presynaptic membrane but does not release its contents.

![[ch12_p082_snare_release_mechanism.png]]

### Role of calcium and fusion

- Calcium channels allow Ca²⁺ influx into the presynaptic terminal.
- Ca²⁺ binds to **synaptotagmin** on the vesicle → complexin is removed from the SNARE complex → the vesicle moves close to the membrane.
- Vesicular and presynaptic membranes fuse, releasing vesicular contents.

## Effects of toxins on SNARE proteins

These toxins have **zinc endopeptidase** activity and cleave/inactivate SNARE proteins.

| Toxin | Action |
|---|---|
| **Tetanus toxin** | Cleaves synaptobrevin (V-SNARE) or VAMP (vesicular-associated membrane protein). It first enters through the neuromuscular junction, then travels retrogradely through axonal transport in the motor neuron to the spinal cord. In the spinal cord it acts on inhibitory synapses; destruction of synaptobrevin prevents inhibitory-neurotransmitter release. Excitatory synapses then dominate, causing strong muscle contractions and spasms. |
| **Botulinum toxin** | May enter the body through food poisoning. Seven types (A–G) are listed; A, E and B are described as toxic to humans. Types A and E cleave synaptosome-associated protein-25 (SNAP-25); type B cleaves synaptobrevin; type C also cleaves syntaxin, which is described as not toxic to humans. It acts mainly at the neuromuscular junction. Breaking the SNAP protein prevents ACh release and causes flaccid paralysis. |

## Presynaptic transmission: summary

Sequence:

1. Action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal → voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ enters → the inside becomes positive and the terminal membrane depolarises.
2. Depolarisation opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ influx.
3. Ca²⁺ binds to synaptotagmin → complexin is removed from SNARE protein → vesicular membrane fuses with the presynaptic membrane → vesicle release.

![[ch12_p083_presynaptic_transmission_summary.png]]

### Toxins affecting presynaptic release

| Toxin | Action |
|---|---|
| **Tetrodotoxin and saxitoxin** | Irreversible blockers of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; vesicular release from the presynaptic terminal is blocked. |
| **Omega-conotoxin** | Blocks voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels of the N-type; vesicular release from the presynaptic terminal is blocked. |
| **Dendrotoxin** | Acts on potassium channels. Blocks K⁺ channels in the presynaptic terminal → increases intracellular K⁺ → depolarisation → opening of Ca²⁺ channels → enhanced vesicle release. |

### Calcium-channel types

| Type | Channel | Distribution/function |
|---|---|---|
| L-type | CaV1.1 | Skeletal muscle. |
| L-type | CaV1.2 | Cardiac and smooth muscle. |
| P/Q-type | CaV2.1 | Presynaptic-membrane calcium channel. |
| N-type | CaV2.2 | Presynaptic-membrane calcium channel; blocked by omega-conotoxin. |
| T-type | CaV3.1, CaV3.2 and CaV3.3 | — |
