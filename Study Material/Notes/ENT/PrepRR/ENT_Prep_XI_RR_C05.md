# 15. Pharynx Part 1

## Anatomy of the pharynx (00:01:50)

- The pharynx is a fibromuscular tube.
- It extends superiorly from the base of the skull and inferiorly to the lower border of the cricoid cartilage, corresponding to the ==C6 vertebra==.
- Three subsegments of the pharynx:
  - Nasopharynx.
  - Oropharynx.
  - Hypopharynx.

### Nasopharynx
[[Thieme_Atlas_of_Anatomy,_Anne_M.pdf#page=600]]
- Extends superiorly from the base of the skull and inferiorly to the imaginary line passing through the palate.
- Corresponding vertebra: C1.

**Boundaries**

- Anteriorly: communicates with the nasal cavity through the choanae.
- Posteriorly: related to the C1 vertebra.
- The roof and posterior wall are continuous with each other.

**Structures of the lateral wall**

- Opening of the Eustachian tube.
- Torus tubarius (TT).
- <strong>Fossa of Rosenmüller</strong> — ==the most common site of origin for nasopharyngeal cancer, as stated in the source.==
- <strong>Sphenopalatine foramen —</strong> communication between the nasopharynx and the pterygopalatine fossa, as stated in the source.

#### Adenoids

- Lymphoid tissue in the midline of the posterior wall of the nasopharynx.
- Especially seen in children.
- Can be identified by endoscopy or X-ray/portable endoscopy, as stated in the notes.

> ![[images/chunk05_p135_source_page.jpeg]]

##### Grading of adenoids

Based on the choanal space occupied:

- Grade 1: 0–25%.
- Grade 2: 25–50%.
- Grade 3: 50–75%.
- Grade 4: >75%.

#### Passavant's ridge

- Present between the nasopharynx and oropharynx.
- Formed by fibres of the palatopharyngeus muscle.
- Prevents nasal regurgitation.
- Injury during adenoidectomy/tonsillectomy can lead to nasal regurgitation.

### Oropharynx

- Extends:
  - Superiorly from the imaginary line passing through the palate.
  - Inferiorly to the imaginary line passing through the tip of the epiglottis, corresponding to the hyoid bone.
- Corresponding vertebrae: C2 and part of C3.

**Boundaries**

- Anteriorly: oral cavity.
- Posteriorly: C2 vertebra.

**Structures of the lateral wall**

- Two pillars:
  - Anterior pillar: palatoglossus.
  - Posterior pillar: palatopharyngeus.
- Palatine/paucial/pharyngeal tonsil: lymphoid tissue between palatoglossus and palatopharyngeus.

**Contents listed in the source**

- Tonsil.
- ==Posterior one-third of the tongue (base).==
- Median glossoepiglottic fold.
- Lateral glossoepiglottic fold.
- Vallecula — the space between the median and lateral glossoepiglottic folds.

### Oral cavity (00:16:05)

**Boundaries of the oral cavity**

- Anteriorly: lips.
- Posteriorly: anterior pillar.
- Superiorly: hard palate.
- Inferiorly: anterior <u>two-thirds of the tongue (presses on the floor of the mouth).</u>

#### Tongue development and nerve supply (00:16:36)
[[Netter's Atlas of Human Embryology, Updated Edition.pdf#page=268]]
##### Anterior two-thirds

- Derived from the first pharyngeal arch.
- Formed by fusion of:
  - Two lingual swellings.
  - Tuberculum impar.
- **Nerve supply (first arch):**
  - General sensation: ==lingual nerve,== a branch of the <u>mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve.</u>
  - Taste (special sensation): ==chorda tympani==, a branch of the facial nerve.
- Pathology in the <u>anterior two-thirds</u> (ulcer, cancer) may cause <u>referred otalgia:</u>
  - Mediated via the lingual nerve (branch of the trigeminal nerve).
  - The <u>trigeminal nerve supplies the ear</u> via the <u>auriculotemporal nerve</u>.

##### Posterior one-third

- Develops from the <strong>hypobranchial eminence</strong>, which has cranial and caudal parts.
- **Cranial part:**
  - Derived from the third arch.
  - Forms most of the posterior one-third (base of tongue).
  - General sensation: glossopharyngeal nerve.
- **Caudal part:**
  - Derived from the fourth arch.
  - Forms the ==vallecula==.
  - General sensation: vagus nerve.

##### Motor nerve supply of the tongue

- All muscles of the tongue are supplied by the ==hypoglossal nerve.==
- <strong>Exception: palatoglossus</strong>.

### Hypopharynx (00:21:50)

- Also called the laryngopharynx.
- Lies behind the larynx.

**Boundaries**

- Superiorly: an imaginary line through the tip of the epiglottis.
- Inferiorly: lower border of the cricoid cartilage, corresponding to C6.
- Anteriorly: related to the larynx.
- Posteriorly: related to vertebrae <u>C3, C4, C5 and C6</u>.

> [!example]- Source page visual — PDF page 137
> ![[images/chunk05_p137_source_page.jpeg]]

#### Subdivisions of the hypopharynx

- Pyriform fossa.
- Post-cricoid region.
- Posterior pharyngeal wall.

##### Pyriform fossa

- The <u>internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve</u> (ILN, as abbreviated in the source) <u>traverses the pyriform fossa.</u>
- The source gives the example of a patient with a fish bone impacted in the pyriform fossa: the superior laryngeal nerve may be injured during extraction.
- The ILN is sensory to the larynx above the level of the vocal cord; injury leads to loss of sensation.
- Also called **Smuggler's pouch**.

##### Post-cricoid region

- Most common site for <strong>carcinoma in Plummer–Vinson syndrome</strong>, according to the source.

### Waldeyer's ring (00:27:03)

- Ring of lymphoid tissue in the upper aerodigestive tract.
- Protective tissue that traps pathogens entering the body.

**Composition of Waldeyer's ring**

- Superiorly: adenoids (also called <strong>Luschka's tonsil or Pharyngeal tonsil)</strong>.
- Lateral wall: **tubal tonsil** (lymphoid tissue around the Eustachian tube; also called **Gerlach's tonsil**) and **palatine tonsil**.
- Base of tongue: lingual tonsil.

### Pharyngeal wall layers (00:28:56)
1. Innermost layer: mucosa.
2. Second layer: pharyngobasilar **fascia**.
3. Third layer: **muscular layer**; external row includes the superior, middle and inferior constrictors.
4. Fourth layer: buccopharyngeal **fascia**.

#### Structures passing through the pharyngeal wall

The source diagram labels the pharyngobasilar fascia, base of skull, sinus of Morgagni, pharyngeal recess (fossa of Rosenmüller), glossopharyngeal nerve and stylopharyngeal muscle, middle constrictor, internal laryngeal nerve and superior laryngeal vessels, inferior constrictor, and recurrent laryngeal nerve and inferior laryngeal vessels.

### One-liners (00:35:10)

- Lower limit of the nasopharynx: imaginary line passing through the palate.
- Lower limit of the oropharynx: imaginary line passing through the tip of the epiglottis, corresponding to the hyoid bone.
- Lower limit of the hypopharynx: lower border of the cricoid cartilage, corresponding to C6.
- The capsule of the tonsil is formed by **“Phargobasilar fascia”** (spelling as printed in the source).
- Killian's dehiscence is between fibres of the inferior constrictor muscle: oblique fibres of thyropharyngeus and horizontal fibres of cricopharyngeus.
  - Zenker's diverticulum is an outpouching of Killian's dehiscence.
- Passavant's ridge is formed by palatopharyngeus.
- Sinus of Morgagni lies between the base of the skull and superior constrictor muscle.
- Luschka's tonsil is the adenoids.
- Gerlach's tonsil is the tubal tonsil.
- The Eustachian tube opens in the nasopharynx approximately 1 cm behind the inferior turbinate.
- The sphenopalatine foramen is 8–10 mm behind the middle turbinate.
- Lining epithelium of the adenoid: pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium.
- Lining epithelium of the tonsil: stratified squamous epithelium.
- Crypts/capsules are seen in the tonsil (hemi-encapsulated), as stated in the source.
- A “bag of worms” feel on palpation of the adenoid:
  - The internal carotid artery (ICA) lies close to the adenoids.
  - Palpation may detect pulsations of an aberrant ICA.
  - If strong pulsations are felt, surgery is stopped and angiography is performed to identify the ICA position.
- Hot-potato voice is seen in quinsy (peritonsillar abscess).
- Referred otalgia:
  - Oral lesions: fifth nerve.
  - Oropharyngeal lesions: ninth nerve.
  - Hypopharyngeal and laryngeal lesions: tenth nerve.

## Adenoid hypertrophy (00:41:45; FMGE 2020, 2021)

- **Age of presentation:** 5-10 years (with peak around 6-7 years)

### Presentation

- Adenoid facies: pinched nose, high-arched palate, crowded teeth, dull expression.
- Nasal obstruction.
- Sinusitis.
- Middle-ear disease due to Eustachian-tube obstruction from adenoid hypertrophy.
- Sleep apnoea due to adenoid and tonsillar hypertrophy.
- **Rhinolalia clausa (nasal twang).**

### Diagnosis
![[p140_lateral-neck-radiograph-adenoid-hypertrophy.jpg]]
- Endoscopy.
- Lateral X-ray view of the nasopharynx:
  - Draw two imaginary lines: one along the base of the skull and one along the hard palate.
  - These divide the nasopharyngeal airway into four equal compartments (approximately 25% each).
  - Grade 1: 0–25%.
  - Grade 2: >25–50%.
  - Grade 3: 51–75%.
  - Grade 4: >75%.

### Treatment

- Grade 1 and 2: conservative treatment with intranasal corticosteroids and antihistamines.
- Grade 3 and 4: surgery.
- Adenoid hypertrophy with serous otitis media: adenoidectomy plus <u>myringotomy with grommet.</u>

### Indications for surgery

**Absolute indications**

- <u>Obstructive sleep apnoea.</u>
- Grade 3 and 4 hypertrophy.

**Relative indications**

- <u>Recurrent infections.</u>
- Recurrent colds.
- ==Acute otitis media.== (weird that it is not an absolute contraindication)

### Contraindications for surgery, as listed in the source

- <u>Age below 3 years</u> (source rationale: “compromises the immunity”).
- <strong>Cleft palate</strong> (source rationale: “causes lifetime nasal regurgitation”).
- Hb below 10 mg/dL (unit reproduced as printed).
- Active URTI.
- Bleeding disorder.

### Adenoid hypertrophy — history, examination, investigations and management flowchart

**History and examination**

- Child with nasal obstruction, pinched nose, absent nasolabial crease, open mouth (PYQ), crowding of anterior upper teeth, high-arched palate, adenoid facies and sleep apnoea.
- Recurrent infections (sinusitis, “ADM” as printed in the diagram).
- Bilateral decreased hearing due to SOM.
- Rhinolalia clausa.
- Failure to thrive (PYQ).

**Investigations**

- Endoscopy to assess size.
- Lateral X-ray of the nose and nasopharynx.
- <strong>==Dodd/crescent sign==</strong>: negative (PYQ); a crescent of air is not present between the mass and pharyngeal wall because the mass arises from the pharyngeal wall itself, as stated in the figure.
- Type B tympanogram (PYQ).

**Initial management**

- Medical.

**Surgical management**

- Adenoidectomy with grommet insertion (PYQ).
- Position: <strong>Rose's.</strong>
- Cold: blind curettage, microdebrider.
- Hot: monopolar suction diathermy, coblation.

**Indications for surgery (PYQ)**

- Sleep apnoea.
- <u>Chronic SOM.</u>
- Recurrent infections (sinusitis, AOM).
- <u>Dental malocclusion</u>.

**Contraindications for surgery below 3 years (as shown in the source flowchart)**

- Velopharyngeal insufficiency.
- Acute infection.
- Bleeding diathesis.

**Complications of surgery (PYQ)**

- Haemorrhage (most common).
- Unmasking velopharyngeal insufficiency.
- <strong>Grisel syndrome</strong> — non-traumatic inflammatory atlantoaxial subluxation.

## Tonsillitis

### Organism and associations

- Organism: Group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus.
- The source states that it has cross-reactive antigens against myocardium, glomeruli and joints, resulting in complications such as rheumatic fever, myocarditis, subacute bacterial endocarditis and arthritis.
- Responsible for both acute and chronic tonsillitis, according to the notes.

### Types

| Type                | Features given in the source                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Acute superficial   | Duration of infection **under 4 weeks**; <u>tonsils are red, <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">without enlargement</mark>.</u>                |
| Acute follicular    | ==Yellowish-white dots== appear as <u>infection spreads into the **crypts**.</u>                                                                 |
| Acute parenchymal   | Tonsils are <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">red and enlarged</mark>, and touch each other at the midline.                                   |
| Acute membranous    | Dead bacteria and tonsillar tissue form a ==true membrane== over the tonsils; <u>no bleeding on peeling</u>; membrane is limited to the tonsils. |
| Chronic follicular  | Duration <u>over 12 weeks</u>; ==yellowish-white dots.==                                                                                         |
| Chronic parenchymal | Tonsils are <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">red and enlarged.</mark>                                                                        |
| Chronic fibroid     | Tonsils are small; **Irwin Moore Sign** is positive                                                                                              |
| Subacute            | Duration of infection 4–12 weeks.                                                                                                                |

**Irwin Moore sign**

- Used to differentiate chronic fibroid tonsillitis from normal tonsils.
- Pus oozes out when pressure is applied to the tonsillar crypts.
- Seen in chronic fibroid tonsillitis, as stated in the source.

### Symptoms

- Throat pain.
- High-grade fever, malaise and fatigue.
- Dysphagia.
- Odynophagia.
- Drooling of saliva.
- Difficulty swallowing.
- Cervical lymphadenopathy on examination.

### Treatment and indications for tonsillectomy

| Treatment category                         | Indications/details listed in the source                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Medical treatment                          | Acute tonsillitis.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| Absolute indications for tonsillectomy     | Obstructive sleep apnoea; carcinoma of the tonsil.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| Relative indications for tonsillectomy     | Chronic/recurrent tonsillitis <br>≥7 episodes in 1 year, <br>≥5 episodes per year for 2 consecutive years, or <br>≥3 episodes per year for 3 consecutive years <br><br>second episode of quinsy (Peritonsillar abscess); <br>streptococcal infections.                                                   |
| Non-tonsillar indication for tonsillectomy | Excision of the styloid process. <br>The source explains that elongation of the styloid process due to calcification of the stylohyoid ligament causes **Eagle syndrome**; the styloid process is approached externally or intra-orally, with the *tonsil removed to access it by the intra-oral route*. |
| Palatal surgery                            | Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP): reshapes the uvula, palate and pharynx in obese patients to treat sleep apnoea; the tonsil is removed as part of the surgery.                                                                                                                                          |

### Complications

**Complications of tonsillitis**

- Streptococcal-related complications.
- Neck abscesses: peritonsillar, parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal and prevertebral space abscesses.
- Referred otalgia.

**Complications of tonsillectomy**

- Haemorrhage:
  - Primary: occurs intraoperatively.
  - Reactionary: within 24 hours of surgery, due to slippage of the ligature; treatment listed is emergency re-exploration and ligation.
  - Secondary: after 24 hours to 14 days, due to infection; treatment listed is IV antibiotics and observation, with re-exploration if bleeding continues.
- Injury to teeth.
- Injury to gums.
- Injury to the temporomandibular joint (TMJ).
- Aspiration (rare).

### Membrane over tonsil: tonsillitis versus diphtheria (01:11:15)

| Feature                | Tonsillitis                                             | Diphtheria                                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Colour                 | Pearly white                                            | Greyish white                                                                   |
| ==Membrane==           | ==True membrane==                                       | ==Pseudomembrane==                                                              |
| Vascularity            | **Avascular** (from dead bacteria and tonsillar tissue) | **Vascular** (from exotoxin liberated by the bacteria), as stated in the source |
| Peeling                | No bleeding on peeling                                  | Bleeds on peeling                                                               |
| Spread                 | Limited to tonsils                                      | Spreads                                                                         |
| Neck/systemic findings | No bull neck or toxemia                                 | Bull neck, toxemia                                                              |

### MCQ (01:13:30; FMGE July 2023)

**Question:** A child with a mild cold who also has adenoid facies has been identified as having chronic adenoid hypertrophy. Which is the best surgical management for this condition?

A. Coblator  
B. Microdebrider  
C. Curettage  
D. Breathing exercise and symptomatic treatment

**Printed answer: A**

**Explanation in the source**

- Coblation: less tissue damage and less bleeding.
- Microdebrider: does not seal the cut ends.
- Curettage: no control over tissue damage and bleeding.
- Breathing exercise and symptomatic treatment: recommended in mild enlargement.

## Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA) (00:00:01)
- Most common benign tumour of the nasopharynx.
- **Benign but locally invasive.**
- Most commonly seen in the juvenile age group.
- The source states that JNA <u>occurs only in males because the tumour is dependent on testosterone for growth.</u>
- It "CAN" be die to HPV

### Site of origin

- **Sphenopalatine foramen**, located 8–10 mm behind the posterior end of the middle turbinate.
- The sphenopalatine foramen is related to the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus.

### Clinical presentation

- Bleeding.
- Unilateral SOM → <em>conductive hearing loss (CHL).</em>
- “**==Frog face==**” → proptosis and widening of the nasal bridge.
- Superior spread: sphenoid sinus → cavernous sinus → nerve involvement/palsy:
	- <u>Cranial nerve II:</u> at the roof of the sphenoid, as stated in the source.
	- Cranial nerves <u>III, IV, V and VI: cavernous sinus.</u>

### Classification systems

- **Radkowski:**
	- Stage 1: limited to the nose, nasopharynx and sinuses.
	- Stage 2: orbital, pterygopalatine fossa or infratemporal fossa involvement.
	- Stage 3: cranial involvement.
- FISCH.
- SESSIONS.

### Investigation

- **Biopsy**, FNAC, probe test and digital palpation 
	- **contraindicated (C/I)** 
	- On *HPE* JNAis a vascular tumour — lacks tunica media → and that is why it bleeds profusely
- CECT: investigation of choice (IOC), with:
	- **Holman–Miller sign/Antral Sign:** anterior bowing of the posterior maxillary sinus.
	- “**Hondousa sign**” (spelling as printed): widening of the gap between the maxilla and mandible.
- Axial CT:
	- **Chopstick sign:** postoperative sign after drilling out of pterygoid wedge
- MRI: to assess intracranial extension.
- **Digital Subtraction Angiography**: to identify the feeding vessel.
	- Most common feeding artery listed: **internal maxillary artery.**

![[IMG_1284.jpeg]]![[IMG_1285.png]]

>Handousa Sign and Holman Miller Sign

### Treatment

1. Angioembolization and surgical resection
	- Surgical excision → Laser or Bipolar Cautery
	- **Approaches:**
		- Endoscopic Excision (Rigid)
		- Lateral Rhinotomy (Weber Ferguson's Incision)
2. Radiotherapy, Chemotherapy
3. Hormonal Therapy

## Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (00:06:20)

- **Bimodal** age distribution; the source notes it **mostly affects the older population.**
- **Males** > females.

### Etiology/risk factors and EBV screening

- **Chinese/Mongoloid** population (source wording).
- Smoking and tobacco.
- Smoke from an incense stick.
- **EBV** screening:
  - IgA viral capsid antigen: most widely used.
  - IgA against early antigen: most specific.

### Site of origin

- **Fossa of Rosenmüller**, behind the torus tubarius in the lateral wall of the nasopharynx.

### Clinical presentation

- **Most common presentation: ==Cervical lymphadenopathy (neck nodes).==**
- Blood-stained nasal discharge.
- Unilateral SOM → CHL.
- Nerve palsies listed: cranial nerves II, III, IV, V, VI, IX, X and XI.
	- The tumour invades the base of the skull and skull foramina.
- Horner's syndrome.
- ==**Trotter's triad:**==
	- **Conductive hearing loss** due to SOM.
	- Fifth-nerve (V3) palsy: **Facial pain** along the distribution of the **Mandibular Branch** of fifth nerve.
	- Tenth-nerve palsy: **Soft palatal paralysis**
- **“HO'S triangle”** aka Subclavian Triangle or Supraclavicular fossa or Omohyoid Triangle: 
	site for lymphadenopathy, formed by:
	  - Superiorly: Inferior belly of omohyoid
	  - Inferiorly: Clavicle 
	  - Anteriorly: Posterior border of sternocleidomastoid
### Classification

- WHO.
- HO'S.

**Variants**
- Well differentiated.
- Moderately differentiated.
- Anaplastic — poor prognosis.

### Investigation and treatment

- Investigation: biopsy (IOC).
- Early stage: **radiotherapy**.
- Late stage: **chemoradiation**.

## Ranula (00:18:34)
- Extravasated cyst from the sublingual salivary gland.
- Location: paramedian.
- Due to injury to the duct of the sublingual salivary gland.
- A ranula may pierce the mylohyoid muscle, enter the neck and become a **plunging ranula**, as shown in the source diagram.
### Features of swelling
- Translucent.
- Bluish, cystic swelling in the floor of the mouth.
- Transillumination test: positive.
### Investigation
- History and examination.
- USG.
### Treatment
- Marsupialization with excision of the sublingual gland.
## Pharyngeal spaces (00:20:48)
### Peritonsillar space

![[p148_peritonsillar-space-and-pharyngobasilar-fascia.jpg]]![[p148_neck-neurovascular-anatomy-carotid-sheath-and-cranial-nerves.jpg]]![[p148_deep-neck-spaces-retropharyngeal-parapharyngeal-and-danger-space.jpg]]

- Space between the pharyngobasilar fascia and the superior constrictor muscle.

### Quinsy (peritonsillar abscess)

- Aetiology: tonsillitis.
- The source states it is most common in adults/older people because the tonsillar crypts are deeper and infection spreads into the peritonsillar space easily.

> [!example]- Source page visual — PDF page 147
> ![[images/chunk05_p147_source_page.jpeg]]

**Clinical features**

- Throat pain.
- Difficulty swallowing.
- Drooling of saliva.
- <strong>Hot-potato or muffled voice.</strong>
- Unilateral bulge of the tonsil with the uvula pushed to the opposite side.

**Treatment**

- <strong>Hot tonsillectomy</strong>: I&D plus tonsillectomy, as represented in the source.
- <strong>I==nterval tonsillectomy:==</strong> I&D, wait 4–6 weeks, then tonsillectomy; marked as preferred.

### Parapharyngeal space
- Described as the largest space.

**Boundaries and relations listed in the source**
- **Superiorly:** base of skull.
- **Inferiorly:** hyoid bone.
- **Laterally:**
	- Angle of mandible.
	- Masseter and medial pterygoid.
	- Parotid.
	- Sternocleidomastoid.
- **Anteriorly and medially:** <strong>buccopharyngeal space</strong>, as labelled in the source.
- **Posteriorly:** carotid sheath and its contents.
- Posteromedially <strong>communicates with the retropharyngeal space.</strong>
- Divided by the styloid process into:
	- Pre-styloid compartment.
	- Post-styloid compartment.

**Pre-styloid compartment abscess**

- Unilateral bulge of the tonsil.
- Neck swelling in the upper third of the sternocleidomastoid muscle.

**Post-styloid compartment abscess**

- Bulge behind the posterior pillar.

### Retropharyngeal space

- Space behind the pharyngeal wall.
- Buccopharyngeal fascia and prevertebral fascia fuse in the midline to form a septum, dividing the retropharyngeal space into two compartments, as stated in the source.

### Retropharyngeal, danger and prevertebral spaces

**Retropharyngeal space**

- Boundaries listed in the source:

## Ludwig's angina (00:47:24)

- Also called Vincent's angina in the source.
- Swelling in the floor of the mouth.
- Pushes the tongue upwards and backwards.

### Goldring's criteria

- **Cellulitis, not an abscess.**
- A**lways bilateral** in presentation.
- **Serosanguinous fluid**, not pus.
- Spread via tissue spaces, not lymphatics.
- Sparing of the submandibular gland.

### Source of infection
- Infections from the teeth, gums, buccal mucosa, submandibular gland and sublingual salivary glands.
- **Mixed infection.**
- *Borrelia Vincenti* (spelling/capitalization as printed).

### Treatment listed in the sourc
- **With stridor:** tracheostomy, I&D, IV antibiotics.
- **Without features of stridor:** the source page ends at this heading; the continuation on the next page lists fibreoptic intubation, I&D and IV antibiotics.

## Pharyngeal pouch (00:51:31)

- Outpouching of the mucosa through Killian's dehiscence.
- **Killian's dehiscence:** also called Zenker diverticulum; lies between thyropharyngeus (oblique fibres) and cricopharyngeus (horizontal fibres) of the inferior constrictor muscle.
- False diverticulum.
- Seen in the older population.

### Etiology/features listed in the source

- Loss of satiety.
- Gurgling sound → **Boyce sign**.
- Dysphagia.
- Sleep → cough (source wording retained).

### Investigation and treatment

- Investigation: barium swallow.
- Treatment: Dohlman's operation — endoscopic diverticulectomy.

## Oral and oropharyngeal cancers (00:53:38)

- Most common carcinoma: squamous cell carcinoma.
- Most common site worldwide: lip (lower lip 98%), as stated in the source.
- Most common carcinoma of the upper lip: basal cell carcinoma.
- Prolonged exposure to sunlight is an important predisposing factor for carcinoma of the lip.
- Most common site in India: gingivobuccal sulcus.
- Most common carcinoma sites listed:
  - Oropharynx: tonsil.
  - Hypopharynx: pyriform fossa.
- Risk factors: smoking, tobacco chewing, repeated trauma from an ill-fitting/sharp denture, alcohol and HPV.
- Premalignant conditions listed:
  - Erythroplakia.
  - Leukoplakia.
  - Submucous fibrosis.
  - Chronic hyperplastic candidiasis.
- HPV 16/18: source describes this as a leading cause of oropharyngeal carcinoma, seen in a younger age group, with a better prognosis than non-HPV SCC.

### Submucous fibrosis

**Pathology sequence shown in the source**

Betel nut → presence of arecoline → normal tissue is replaced with fibrous tissue → blanching of the oral mucosa and trismus.

**Treatment listed**

- Intralesional steroids (triamcinolone).
- Hyaluronidase.
- Antioxidants.
- Cessation of betel-nut chewing.

### Lymph nodes and metastasis

- Lymph-node metastasis in oral and oropharyngeal carcinoma is classified as levels I, II, III and IV.
- Midline tumours or tumours crossing the midline can have bilateral lymph-node metastasis.
- Most common site of metastasis listed: lung.

### Verrucous carcinoma

- Also called Ackerman's tumour.
- Well differentiated.
- Slow-growing SCC.
- Low malignant potential.
- Presents as a fungating/warty soft-tissue papillary lesion of the oral cavity.

### Plummer–Vinson syndrome

- Also called Patterson–Kelly–Brown syndrome / sideropenic dysphagia.
- Triad listed in the source:
  - Post-cricoid dysphagia.
  - Hypopharyngeal/upper oesophageal webs.
  - Iron-deficiency anaemia (IDA).
- Most common carcinoma in females with Plummer–Vinson syndrome: post-cricoid cancer, as stated in the source.

> [!warning] ENT-A016 — Printed adenoid-hypertrophy age range (PDF p. 140)
> The source image itself prints **“19–14 yrs”**. This is not an OCR-only error; it is an invalid/reversed range, but the intended age range cannot be reconstructed safely from the source. The original value is therefore retained and flagged, not silently changed to a guessed range.

> [!note] ENT-A017 — Adenoid grade boundaries (PDF pp. 136, 140)
> The two pages differ at the grade boundaries, including overlap at 25% and a “>25–50%” formulation on page 140. One published endoscopic choanal-obstruction scheme uses Grade I 0–25%, Grade II 26–50%, Grade III 51–75%, and Grade IV 76–100%; another commonly used scheme grades contact with adjacent structures instead. Thus the source is internally inconsistent, but grading systems are not entirely universal. The original ranges remain in the transcription; the 0–25/26–50/51–75/76–100 scheme is offered only as a cited comparison. Reference: [Wiley 2026 study/review of endoscopic adenoid classification](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wjo2.70019).

> [!note] ENT-A018 — Styloidectomy and tonsillectomy (PDF pp. 142–143)
> Styloidectomy is a treatment for **Eagle syndrome**. A transoral route may use the tonsillar fossa and may be performed with or without tonsillectomy; tonsil-sparing approaches are also described. Therefore “excision of the styloid process” is not itself a disease indication for tonsillectomy; it describes a possible surgical access/context. The source phrase is retained. References: [Systematic review of transoral robotic styloidectomy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38290994/) and [Systematic review of transoral styloidectomy approaches](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13155192/).

> [!note] ENT-A019 — JNA vascular histology (PDF p. 145)
> The University of Iowa Head and Neck Protocol describes juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA) as a highly vascular lesion whose vessels lack a true muscular layer, predisposing them to haemorrhage. The source’s “lacks tunica media” wording is a simplified rendering of this characteristic rather than a clear factual error. Preserve the source wording, while understanding the pathology description as a general histological feature rather than a substitute for a full pathology description. Reference: [University of Iowa: Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma](https://iowaprotocols.medicine.uiowa.edu/protocols/juvenile-nasopharyngeal-angiofibroma).
