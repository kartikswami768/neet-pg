# 11. Complications of ASOM and CSOM

## Routes of spread of infection from the middle ear

- **Natural communications**
  - Oval window and round window.
  - These are natural communications between the middle ear and inner ear.
- **Bony erosion**
  - Caused by cholesteatoma or atticoantral type of CSOM.
  - Proteolytic enzymes produced lead to bony erosion, facilitating infection spread.
- **Venous spread**
  - The sigmoid sinus is located posteriorly in the mastoid.
  - Venous spread can cause sigmoid sinus thrombosis (lateral sinus thrombosis).
  - There is no arterial route of spread for infection from the middle ear.

### Classification of complications

| Type                           | Complications listed in the source                                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Extracranial complications** | Mastoiditis; labyrinthitis; petrositis; osteomyelitis; septicaemia; facial nerve palsy                          |
| **Intracranial complications** | Meningitis; extradural abscess; subdural abscess; brain abscess; otitic hydrocephalus; lateral sinus thrombosis |

| Feature | Acute otitis media | Chronic otitis media |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | The answer usually ends with “-itis”. | The answer usually does not end with “-itis”. |
| Most common extracranial complication | Mastoiditis | Post-auricular abscess |
| Most common intracranial complication | Meningitis | Intracranial abscess (brain abscess) |

### Anatomical basis of extracranial complications

**Parts of the temporal bone**

- **Petrous part (leaf-like bone)**
  - Contains the inner ear.
  - The internal auditory canal is present here.
- **Tympanic part**
  - Forms part of the external auditory canal; also labelled as the tympanic process.
- **Squamous part.**
- **Tegmen tympani:** roof of the middle ear.
- **Mastoid part:** contains mastoid air cells.

## Routes and mechanisms of complications

### Extracranial complications

Spread of infection is described as follows:

- To mastoid air cells → **mastoiditis**.
- Medially to the inner ear → **labyrinthitis** (pus in the inner ear).
- Medially to the petrous bone apex → **petrositis**.
- To the squamous temporal bone → **osteomyelitis**.
- Through the blood → **septicaemia**.
- To the facial nerve (shown as travelling from IAC → inner ear → middle ear → mastoid) → **facial nerve palsy**.

### Routes of intracranial complications

- **Route 1 (most common):** middle-ear disease → breakthrough of the tegmen tympani (roof of middle ear) → middle cranial fossa. The temporal lobe is in this fossa and is described as the most common site for intracranial complications.
- **Route 2 (less common):** middle ear → inner ear → petrous bone → cerebellum.

### Intracranial complications — anatomical sequence

- Disease erodes the tegmen → inflammation of the meninges → **meningitis**.
- Pus between bone and dura → **extradural abscess**.
- Pus breaks through the dura and lies between dura and brain parenchyma → **subdural abscess**.
- Pus enters the brain → **brain abscess (intracerebral abscess)**.
- Disease reaches the sigmoid sinus → thrombosis of the sigmoid sinus → **lateral sinus thrombosis**.
- Sigmoid sinus thrombosis → impaired cranial venous drainage → fluid retention in the ventricles → **otitic hydrocephalus**.

## Acute mastoiditis
- Most common extracranial complication of acute otitis media.
- Causative organism listed: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (same as AOM).

### Pathophysiology

Eustachian tube blockage → pus collects in the middle ear (AOM) → pus travels via the aditus and reaches the mastoid antrum → infection spreads through the aditus into mastoid air cells → inflammation and pus filling the air cells → septa between air cells break → the cells merge into a single pus-filled cavity (**coalescent mastoiditis**).

### Symptoms — exaggerated ASOM symptoms

- Pain, more severe and also behind the pinna.
- Fever.
- Hearing loss.
- <u>Ear discharge, which may be profuse, persistent and pulsatile.</u>
- <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Lighthouse sign.</mark>
- Mastoid tenderness.

### Signs

- **“Ironed-out appearance” of the mastoid:** stretched, red, oedematous skin; identified in the source as the first sign of acute mastoiditis. 
- **Pinna changes:** pinna pushed outwards and forwards.
- Sagging of the posterior meatal wall.
- <u>Tenderness over the mastoid region at <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">MacEwen’s triangle.</mark></u>
- **Retroauricular groove:** the source describes deepening of the groove associated with swollen, oedematous mastoid and an apparent deepening of the groove.
- Discharge on otoscopy reflects light and is pulsatile:
	- Pulsatile otorrhoea.
	- Called the **<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">lighthouse sign</mark>**.
	- Also seen in ASOM; the source labels it a <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">non-specific sign.</mark>
- **==Reservoir sign== (specific sign stated for mastoiditis):** 
	- mucopurulent pus is present in the external auditory canal; 
	- after the pus is removed, the canal immediately fills again. 
	- If it takes time to reappear, this is more likely AOM than mastoiditis.

### Diagnosis

Clinical findings listed:

- Ironed-out appearance.
- Pinna pushed outwards and forwards.
- Deepening of the retroauricular groove.
- Lighthouse sign
- Positive reservoir sign.

**Imaging — X-ray or CT scan**

CT findings listed
- Coalescence of air cells.
- Destruction/defect in the roof of the mastoid.
- Haziness in the mastoid.
- Comparison with the opposite side: the diseased side appears hazy (not black/air-filled).

### Treatment

- **Surgery:** cortical mastoidectomy.
	- Delineate the entire mastoid from:
		- Roof (superiorly).
		- Sigmoid sinus (posteriorly).
		- Tip cells (inferiorly).
		- Posterior wall of the external auditory canal (anteriorly).
	- Remove all diseased air cells.
- **Medical treatment:** IV antibiotics.

### Local abscesses related to the mastoid

- **Bezold’s abscess**
  - Pus breaks through the tip of the mastoid.
  - Extends into the ==sternocleidomastoid muscle.==
  - Presents in the <u>upper part of the neck.</u>
- **Post-auricular abscess**
  - Described as the ==most common abscess.==
  - <u>Pus breaks through the outer cortex of the mastoid.</u>
  - Presents behind the pinna.
- **Luc’s abscess**
  - ==Pus breaks into the external auditory meatus.==
  - Memory aid: “L follows M” — Luc’s = meatus.
- **Citelli’s abscess (digastric abscess)**
  - At the tip of the mastoid, related to the posterior belly of the digastric muscle.
  - Abscess in the digastric region.
  - Memory aid: “C follows D” — Citelli’s = digastric.
- Image-based location clues:
  - Near the zygomatic bone → zygomatic abscess.
  - Behind the pinna → post-auricular abscess (MC).
  - Upper part of the neck in SCM → Bezold’s abscess.
  - Near the angle of the mandible → digastric/Citelli’s abscess.
  - In the external auditory meatus → Luc’s abscess.

## Petrositis

- **Definition:** inflammation of petrous apex cells.
- **Anatomy:** the petrous bone is described as leaf-like. At the apex are two cranial nerves:
  - ==CN V — trigeminal nerve.==
  - CN VI — ==abducens nerve.==

### Pathophysiology

Infection spreads from the middle ear to the petrous apex → pus/inflammation at the apex → involvement of the fifth and sixth cranial nerves.

### Clinical features — Gradenigo’s triad

- **==Persistent ear discharge==:** constant otorrhoea from middle-ear disease.
- **Deep-seated ==retro-orbital pain==:** due to fifth-nerve (<u>trigeminal</u>) involvement; pain around the eye.
- **Lateral rectus palsy (diplopia):** due to sixth-nerve (<u>abducens</u>) involvement.

## Labyrinthitis

- **Definition:** inflammation of the inner ear.
- Three forms are described: circumscribed, diffuse serous and diffuse suppurative labyrinthitis.

### Circumscribed labyrinthitis

**Pathology**

- Localised area of labyrinth involved.
- Small area of erosion between middle ear and inner ear.
- Communication formed between middle and inner ear.

**Clinical features**

- Fistula sign: positive.
	- increased pressure in the external auditory canal → pressure reaches the middle ear → through the erosion area, infection reaches the inner ear.
- Transient vertigo occurs when pressure is applied.

**Prognosis:** localised; the source states that it can be managed.

### Serous (diffuse serous) labyrinthitis

**Pathology**

- The entire labyrinth is filled with sterile fluid.
- No pus is present.
- Liberation of bacterial toxins causes sterile inflammation.
- Hyperactive/irritated labyrinth.

**Clinical features**

- Vertigo: a single, long episode lasting a few days to a few weeks.
- Nystagmus towards the same side (diseased side), as stated in the source.
- Rule given: hyperactive labyrinth → nystagmus towards the same side.
- Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL).
- Tinnitus.
- No fever (“no pus = no toxic features”).

**Prognosis:** reversible; the source lists appropriate antibiotics, clearance of infection/disease and possible reversal of the condition.

### Suppurative (diffuse suppurative) labyrinthitis

**Pathology**

- The entire labyrinth is filled with pus.
- Purulent fluid throughout the inner ear.
- Hypoactive/destructive labyrinth.

**Clinical features**

- Severe vertigo.
- Nystagmus towards the opposite side (away from the diseased side).
- Rule given: hypoactive labyrinth → nystagmus towards the opposite side.
- SNHL and tinnitus.
- High-grade fever; toxic symptoms are present.

**Prognosis and treatment**

- Irreversible condition; requires immediate attention.
- Labyrinthine sedatives.
- Mastoid exploration (MRM).
- Clear the disease source.
- IV antibiotics.
- If untreated, it progresses to intracranial complications.

### Comparison of labyrinthitis types

| Feature | Circumscribed labyrinthitis | Diffuse serous labyrinthitis | Diffuse suppurative labyrinthitis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pathology | Thinning or erosion of bony capsule of labyrinth | Diffuse inflammation of labyrinth without pus | Pus infection of labyrinth |
| Labyrinth status | Labyrinth exposed to external stimuli | Labyrinth inflamed and irritable | Loss of vestibular and cochlear functions |
| Reversibility | Reversible | Reversible | Irreversible |
| Fistula test | Positive | Negative | Negative |
| Nystagmus | Positive pressure → nystagmus to same side; negative pressure → nystagmus to opposite side | Nystagmus to same side | Nystagmus to opposite side |
| Symptoms | Transient vertigo on applying pressure to tragus | Vestibular and cochlear symptoms | Toxic, severe symptoms of labyrinthitis |

**Key rule for nystagmus (as printed):**

- Hyperactive/irritated labyrinth (serous) → nystagmus towards the same side.
- Hypoactive/destroyed labyrinth (suppurative) → nystagmus towards the opposite side.

## Approach to a patient with AOM and complications

The source diagram maps these presentations and management steps:

- **Fever, ear discharge, postauricular oedema and tenderness:** examination includes a pinna pushed outward and forward, postauricular “ironed-out” appearance, sagging of the posterior-superior meatal wall and abscess following mastoiditis. Reservoir sign, Bezold’s, Citelli’s and Luc’s abscess locations are noted. Diagnosis: **mastoiditis** (labelled MC, caused by pneumococcus). Management: IV antibiotics plus cortical/simple mastoidectomy or Schwartze operation.
- **Persistent ear discharge with retro-orbital pain (fifth-nerve involvement) and diplopia (sixth-nerve palsy):** Gradenigo triad; diagnosis: **petrositis**. Management: IV antibiotics, MRM and drainage of petrous air cells.
- **Mixed hearing loss and vertigo:** diagnosis: **labyrinthitis** (erosion is stated to be seen most commonly at the lateral semicircular canal). Management: labyrinthine sedative; if chronic otitis media is present, mastoid surgery is shown.
- **Deviation of the face to the normal side:** diagnosis: **facial palsy**. Management: MRM with facial-nerve decompression.

## Intracranial complications

### Meningitis

- Most common intracranial complication of AOM.
- Causative organism listed: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (same as AOM).

**Clinical features**

- High-grade fever.
- Projectile vomiting.
- Projectile headache.
- Altered sensorium/unconsciousness.
- Irritability.

**Specific signs**

- **Brudzinski’s sign (neck sign):** flexion of the neck → automatic flexion of the hip and knee; tests meningeal irritation.
- **Kernig’s sign:** thigh flexed towards the abdomen; attempt to extend the leg causes pain; tests meningeal irritation.
- The source states that both signs are specific for meningitis.

**Diagnosis:** lumbar puncture is labelled the gold standard; send CSF for analysis and decide treatment based on CSF analysis.

**Treatment:** based on CSF findings; IV antibiotics with mastoid exploration.

### Extradural abscess

- **Location:** between dura and bone; outside the dura (“extra-dural”).
- **Characteristic feature:** usually silent; may not present with dramatic symptoms.
- **Treatment:** modified radical mastoidectomy; drain the abscess with IV antibiotics.

### Subdural abscess

- **Location:** between dura and brain parenchyma.
- **Characteristic feature:** very rare; not a common complication.
- **Clinical features:** fever, headache, projectile vomiting (raised ICP) and some localising signs.
- **Treatment:** IV antibiotics and drugs to reduce ICP; mastoid exploration.

### Brain abscess (intracerebral abscess)

- The most common type is labelled intracerebral abscess (brain abscess), **not extradural** and **not subdural**.
- **Location:** temporal lobe is most commonly involved. Disease spreads from the middle ear through the tegmen to the temporal lobe.
- **Features due to raised intracranial tension:** headache, projectile vomiting, papilloedema, drowsiness, confusion, stupor and bradycardia.
- **Localising features listed:**
  - Temporal lobe: speech cortex → aphasia.
  - Motor cortex → hemiplegia.
  - Sensory cortex → sensory loss.
  - Visual cortex → hemianopia.
  - Cerebellar abscess → ataxia, dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia.

**Diagnosis:** MRI with contrast.

- Characteristic finding: ring sign — central dark area (necrotic pus) surrounded by an enhancing ring/capsule.
- The source equates the ring sign with brain abscess.

**Treatment**

- Medical management: IV antibiotics, dexamethasone and medications to reduce intracranial pressure (mannitol is listed).
- Neurosurgical drainage: drain the abscess first.
- Ear surgery: modified radical mastoidectomy for CSOM/cholesteatoma.

The source reiterates: most common type of brain abscess = intracerebral; lobe involved = temporal; ring sign on MRI is seen in intracerebral abscess.

### Lateral sinus thrombosis (sigmoid sinus thrombosis)

**Anatomy**

- The sigmoid sinus forms the posterior border of the mastoid.
- It is part of the venous drainage of the brain.

**Pathophysiology**

Disease spreads from middle ear → mastoid air cells → outer wall of the sigmoid sinus (**periphlebitis**) → inner wall (**endophlebitis**) → platelet aggregation and thrombus formation (infective thrombus) → later abscess formation and blockage of the sigmoid sinus.

**Clinical features**

- **Hectic “picket-fence” fever:** constant fever that does not touch baseline; the source relates the characteristic pattern to emboli released into the circulation.
- **Griesinger sign:** oedema and bluish discoloration of the mastoid due to improper drainage of mastoid emissary veins.
- Raised intracranial pressure, presenting with papilloedema.
- **Tobey–Ayer test (historical; not done now):** compression of the internal jugular vein (IJV) on the healthy side produces a rise in CSF pressure.
- **Crowe–Beck test (historical; not done now):** compression of the IJV on the healthy side produces engorgement of retinal veins.

**Diagnosis**

- Modern investigation: contrast-enhanced CT/MRI.
- Characteristic finding: **delta sign / empty delta sign**, described as diagnostic of sigmoid sinus thrombosis.

### Otitic hydrocephalus

- Sigmoid sinus thrombosed.
- Cranial circulation cannot drain; venous drainage is obstructed.
- Fluid retention in the ventricles → dilatation of ventricles = hydrocephalus.
- Clinical features listed: headache, papilloedema and projectile vomiting.
- Diagnosis by CT: dilated ventricles.
- Treatment: medical treatment to reduce ICP; VP shunt (ventriculoperitoneal shunt) to redirect CSF.

### Integrated diagnostic diagram and common errors to avoid

The flowchart contrasts cases suggestive of temporal-lobe abscess, lateral sinus thrombophlebitis and meningitis. Findings shown include nominal aphasia, homonymous supra-quadrantanopia, headache, seizures and contralateral hemiparesis; picket-fence fever with mastoid tenderness/oedema (Griesinger sign); positive historical venous-pressure tests; CT/MRI ring sign; delta sign/empty triangle sign; and fever, headache, vomiting, drowsiness, neck rigidity, irritability, Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs. Management branches in the diagram include abscess drainage with IV antibiotics and MRM when stable, IV antibiotics plus MRM for lateral sinus thrombophlebitis, and IV antibiotics plus MRM when the patient is stable for meningitis.

**Common errors to avoid (as printed)**

- **Routes of spread:**
  - Natural communications (oval window, round window).
  - Bone destruction (proteolytic enzymes).
  - Venous spread (sigmoid sinus).
  - No arterial route of spread.
- **Pulsatile otorrhoea / lighthouse sign:** non-specific; the source states that it is seen in both AOM (acute suppurative otitis media) and acute mastoiditis.
- **Specific sign for mastoiditis:** reservoir sign — after pus is removed, the canal immediately refills.
- **Gradenigo’s triad:** seen in petrositis; at the petrous apex are CN V and CN VI; triad is persistent ear discharge, fifth-nerve retro-orbital pain, and sixth-nerve lateral rectus palsy (diplopia).

## Anatomy of the nose

### External nose anatomy

The external nose is shown as a **bony pyramid** (upper one-third) and a **cartilaginous pyramid** (lower two-thirds; marked PYQ).

| Bony pyramid                                                   | Cartilaginous pyramid                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Upper 1/3                                                      | Lower 2/3 (PYQ)                                                                                      |
| **Paired bones:** nasal bone; ascending process of the maxilla | **Paired cartilage:** upper lateral cartilage; lower lateral cartilage; small sesamoid cartilage     |
| **Unpaired bone:** frontal bone                                | **Unpaired cartilage:** septal cartilage, which comes from below and supports the dorsum of the nose |

- **Nasion:** point where the frontal bone meets the nasal bone in the midline.
- **Rhinion:** the nasal bone unites with the upper lateral cartilage in the midline (PYQ).
- **Limen nasi:** 
	- <u>lower border of the upper lateral cartilage</u>; 
	- *site of incision in internal rhinoplasty*; 
	- forms the ==boundary of the internal nasal valve, described as the narrowest portion of the nasal cavity.==

### Walls of the nasal cavity

#### Inlet — vestibule
- The ==only area in the nose lined by stratified squamous epithelium with hair follicles and sebaceous glands==; the rest is lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium.
- Folliculitis is stated to be most common in this region.
- <u>Contains an internal nasal valve.</u>
- **Formed by:**
	- Medially — septum.
	- Superiorly — lower border of the upper lateral cartilage.
	- Laterally — inferior turbinates.

#### Outlet — choana
- Communication between the nose and nasopharynx.

#### Superiorly — roof
- Has specialised sensory olfactory epithelium (perceives smell).
- Separates the nasal cavity from the cranial cavity.
- **Formed by:**
	- Anterior 1/3 — <u>nasal bone and frontal bone.</u>
	- Middle 1/3 — cribriform plate of the <u>ethmoid bone.</u>
	- Posterior 1/3 — <u>sphenoid bone</u>.

#### Inferiorly — floor
- Separates the nasal cavity from the oral cavity.
- Formed by the <u>crest of the maxilla</u> and the <u>crest of the palatine bone.</u>

#### Medially — septum
- Separates the right and left halves of the nose.
- Made of cartilaginous and bony parts:
	- Cartilage — septal cartilage / quadrangular cartilage.
	- Bone — perpendicular plate of the ethmoid and vomer.
	- Other small contributions — nasal spine of the frontal bone, rostrum of the sphenoid bone, and crest of the maxilla and palatine bone.

#### Blood supply to the septum
- **Internal carotid artery — upper half:**
	- Anterior ethmoidal artery — anterosuperior part of the septum.
	- Posterior ethmoidal artery — posterosuperior part of the septum.
- **External carotid artery — lower half:**
	- Superior labial artery — anteroinferior part of the septum.
	- Sphenopalatine and greater palatine arteries — posteroinferior part of the septum.

## Blood supply, lateral wall and nasal plexuses

- The lateral wall of the nose has bony projections called **turbinates**.
- **Kiesselbach’s (Little’s) area:** arterial plexus located in the anteroinferior portion of the septum, where the septal arteries anastomose except for the posterior ethmoidal artery.
- **Woodruff’s venous plexus:** at the posterior end of the inferior turbinate. The venous anastomosis is associated in the source with posterior epistaxis.
- The lateral wall has three bony projections (turbinates/conchae) and **meatuses**, the spaces between the bony projections.

## Paranasal sinuses

- Four sinus groups are listed.

### Maxillary sinus

- Also called the **antrum of Highmore**.
- The source describes it as the ==first sinus to develop and be visible radiologically==, the largest sinus, and the most common sinus affected in adults.

**Boundaries**

- **Base:** separates the maxillary sinus from the lateral wall of the nose.
- **Apex:** at the level of the zygoma.
- **Roof:** formed by the floor of the orbit; fracture of the floor of the orbit results in a tear-drop sign.
- **Floor:** related to the ==second premolar and first molar tooth.==
- **Anterior wall:** skin and subcutaneous tissue.
- **Posterior wall:** ==pterygopalatine fossa, containing the internal maxillary artery,== which divides into the sphenopalatine artery (through the sphenopalatine foramen) and the greater palatine artery (through the greater palatine foramen).

### Frontal sinus

- ==Last sinus to develop.==
- ==Absent at birth==; therefore not visible radiologically at birth.
- An air-filled cavity within the frontal bone.
- Pyramidal in shape, with anterior and posterior walls:
  - Anterior wall — anterior table of the frontal bone.
  - Posterior wall — posterior table of the frontal bone.
- Chronic frontal sinusitis can erode the posterior wall and affect the frontal lobe, causing a frontal-lobe abscess, personality changes, features of raised ICP, headaches, projectile vomiting and altered sensorium.
- The floor of the frontal sinus is related to the roof of the orbit, which can lead to orbital complications.
- Medially, the right and left frontal sinuses are separated by the interfrontal septum.

### Ethmoid sinus

- Described as the ==most pneumatized sinuses at birth.==
- Sandwiched between the frontal sinus anteriorly and the sphenoid sinus posteriorly.
- **Roof:** a thin plate separating it from the anterior cranial fossa, called the fovea ethmoidalis.
- ==Medially related to the middle turbinate.== <u>The anterior and posterior ethmoids are divided by the basal lamella of the middle turbinate.</u>
- ==Laterally related to the medial wall of the orbit.==

**Air cells**

- **Agger nasi:** anteriormost ethmoidal cell.
- **Bulla ethmoidalis:** the biggest ethmoidal air cell.
- **Haller cell:** ethmoidal cell in relation to the floor of the orbit.
- **Onodi cells:** posterior ethmoidal cells extending into the sphenoid sinus; may have the optic nerve and internal carotid artery.

### Sphenoid sinus

- The posterior-most sinus of the sphenoid bone.
- The **sella turcica** is described as a depression in the roof of the sphenoid sinus that lodges the pituitary gland.

**Relations of the sphenoid sinus**

- Anterior — posterior ethmoid cells.
- Posterior — sphenoid bone.
- Lateral — optic nerve, cavernous sinus (CN III, IV, VI, V1, V2) and internal carotid artery (ICA).
- Floor — foramen rotundum above and vidian canal below.

### Lining membrane of the nose

- Schneidarian membrane / respiratory epithelium / pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium lines the entire nasal cavity.
- **Exceptions:**
  - Vestibule — stratified squamous epithelium with hair follicles.
  - Roof of the nose — olfactory epithelium.

### Dangerous triangle of the nose

- The area bounded between the nasion and the angle of the lips is described as a region where infection can spread retrogradely and cause cavernous sinus thrombosis.
- The pterygoid venous plexus is described as valveless, allowing retrograde spread of infection towards the cavernous sinus; the source states that infections are therefore better treated with antibiotics.

9. Largest sinus: maxillary sinus.

## Phase C — P2 verification notes (editorial layer)

> [!warning] ENT-A008 — Serous labyrinthitis prose/illustration conflict (PDF p. 83)
> The text describes serous labyrinthitis as containing sterile fluid without pus and as potentially reversible, while the adjacent illustration says “filled with pus,” “dead labyrinth/hypoactive labyrinth,” and “irreversible.” These are materially different descriptions. Purulent/suppurative labyrinthitis is associated with pus and destructive inner-ear injury; the illustration may have been mismatched or mislabelled, but the source alone does not prove how the mismatch occurred. Both the prose and original image are preserved; do not reconcile them by silently relabelling the figure. References: [Merck Manual: Purulent Labyrinthitis](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/purulent-labyrinthitis) and [StatPearls: Labyrinthitis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560506/).

> [!note] ENT-A011 — Woodruff’s plexus location / MCQ key (PDF pp. 93, 101; compare C04 p. 116)
> Woodruff’s venous plexus is described in the anatomical reference as lying on the lateral nasal wall at the **posterior aspect of the inferior turbinate**. On page 101 the printed MCQ key instead selects the posterior end of the middle turbinate. That key conflicts with the anatomy text and reference; **option C (posterior end of the inferior turbinate) is likely intended**. The printed answer remains in the original transcription and this note records the likely key error. Reference: [StatPearls: Anatomy, Head and Neck, Nasal Concha](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK546636/).

> [!note] ENT-A015 — Sphenoid sinus pneumatization and endoscopy MCQs (PDF pp. 104–105)
> “Concha bullosa” is the pneumatization of a nasal turbinate, classically the middle turbinate; it is not one of the usual sphenoid sinus pneumatization types (e.g. conchal, presellar, and sellar variants). Therefore the printed “concha bullosa” exception in the sphenoid-pneumatization question is supported. The “superior turbinate” answer to the endoscopy question is likely the intended exam answer, but the absolute word “only” depends on the examination method and should not be generalized beyond the source’s exam context. References: [2026 systematic review of sphenoid sinus pneumatization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12565-026-00955-5) and [Concha bullosa: radiological study/classification](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S217357351930047X).
