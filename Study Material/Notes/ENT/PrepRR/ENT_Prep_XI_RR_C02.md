### Glomus tumor
Two Types
1. G. Jugulare → Jugular Fossa
	- Can involve IX, X, XI passing through Jugular foramen
	- **Can also involve XII nerve** → which do not pass through jugular foramen
2. G. Tympanicum → HypoTympanic
 


- **Nature:** Slow-growing, benign, highly vascular tumour.
- **Epidemiology:** 5–8 times more common in females; usually presents at 40–60 years of age.

#### Clinical Features

- Conductive hearing loss (CHL)
- **Pulsatile tinnitus** — most common presenting symptom
- Ear bleeding (otalgia? no, recorded separately)
- Cranial nerve involvement
- Otalgia

#### Four Important Signs

1. **Rising sun sign**
2. **Brown's sign:** Blanching of the tumour on Siegle's speculum compression.
3. **Phelps' sign**
4. **Aquino's sign**

#### Investigations

- **Investigation of choice:** CECT
- **MRI:** Salt-and-pepper appearance
- **DSA (digital subtraction angiography):** Before surgery
  - Most common feeding vessel: Ascending pharyngeal artery
- **Secretory tumour (~5%):** 24-hour urinary VMA
- **Biopsy:** Contraindicated due to risk of severe haemorrhage.

#### Treatment

- **Treatment of choice:** Preoperative embolisation followed by surgical excision.
  - Embolisation is guided by DSA.
  - Surgical excision may involve laser or bipolar cautery.
  - **HPE:** Zellballen pattern of cells.
- **Radiotherapy (RT) and chemotherapy (CT):** Other treatment modalities.
- **Secretory tumour (~5%):** Alpha-blocker followed by beta-blocker, when indicated.
## Pathogenesis of middle-ear diseases

All middle-ear diseases occur due to Eustachian tube obstruction.

### Causes of Eustachian tube obstruction

- **Nasal causes:**
  - Deviated nasal septum (DNS).
  - Nasal polyps.
  - Allergic rhinitis.
  - Nasal packing for epistaxis.
  - Nasal foreign body.
- **Nasopharyngeal cause:** any pathology affecting nasopharyngeal ventilation can obstruct the Eustachian tube.
- **Palatal cause:** cleft palate.
  - Eustachian tube opening depends on the tensor veli palatini and levator veli palatini muscles.
  - If these muscles are weak, the tube cannot open and middle-ear ventilation decreases.

### Pathway shown in the source

Nasal / nasopharyngeal / palatal pathology → Eustachian tube obstruction → negative pressure in the middle ear → middle-ear effusion.

- Infected effusion: suppurative otitis media.
- Non-infected effusion: non-suppurative otitis media.
- **Less than 4 weeks:** acute suppurative otitis media (ASOM).
- **More than 12 weeks:** chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM).

## Acute suppurative otitis media (ASOM)

*Exam references printed on the page: AIIMS 2020; NEET PG 2018, 2022; INI-CET 2022, 2024, 2025; FMGE 2018, 2022, 2024, 2025.*

### Predisposing factors leading to Eustachian tube blockage

- Recurrent attacks of common cold / upper respiratory tract infection (URTI).
- Exanthematous fever, such as measles; diphtheria; whooping cough.
- Chronic rhinitis and sinusitis.
- Allergy.
- Nasopharyngeal tumours.
- Nasal packing for epistaxis.
- Cleft palate.

### Stages of ASOM

| Stage | Pathology | Symptoms | Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Tubal occlusion** | Tubal oedema of the Eustachian tube leads to negative pressure and retraction of the tympanic membrane (TM). | Ear block; mild deafness. | Mild retraction of the TM. |
| **2. Presuppuration** | Invasion of organisms and mild inflammation. | Severe ear pain, hearing loss, fever. | Blood vessels radiate from the periphery to the centre: **cartwheel appearance**. |
| **3. Suppuration** | Frank pus formation. | Excruciating ear pain, high-grade fever, hearing loss. | Bulging TM; pus discharges in pulsatile form (**pulsatile otorrhoea**). Pus reflects light: **lighthouse sign**. |
| **4. Resolution** | TM ruptures with release of pus. | — | TM perforation in the anteroinferior quadrant. |
| **5. Complications** | — | — | The source lists the preceding clinical features as key findings. |

- **Most common organism (MC):** ==*Streptococcus pneumoniae*==.
- **Symptoms:** child with a history of URTI, ear pain, fever and hearing loss.
- **Signs:** TM retraction, cartwheel appearance, bulging drum, pulsatile otorrhoea and lighthouse sign.
- **Investigations:** usually not required.
- **Diagnosis:** history and clinical examination.

### Treatment and myringotomy

- **Antibiotic:** amoxicillin / ampicillin for 8–10 days.
- **Supportive treatment:** nasal/oral decongestants, analgesics and antipyretics.
- **Myringotomy**
	- **Indications listed in the source:**
		- Excruciating pain with a bulging drum.
		- Incomplete resolution despite antibiotics.
		- Persistent effusion for more than 12 weeks.
		- ASOM with facial palsy.
	- **Incision:** curvilinear incision in the posteroinferior quadrant.

>
>| Feature | ASOM | SOM/OME |
|---|---|---|
| Pathology | Acute infection with suppurative effusion | Persistent middle-ear effusion |

| Classical incision type | Curvilinear/J-shaped or circumferential | Radial |

| Desired outcome | Drainage, followed by healing of the incision | Continued ventilation until the tube extrudes |

## Non-suppurative otitis media (SOM)

- **Definition:** accumulation of non-inflammatory exudate in the middle-ear cavity.
- **Common age:** 2–6 years.
- **Associated causes:** nasal, nasopharyngeal and palatal pathology.

| Condition | Age group | Cause listed in the source |
|---|---|---|
| Bilateral SOM | Children | Adenoid hypertrophy |
| Unilateral SOM | Juveniles | Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA) |
| Unilateral SOM | Older age | Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) |

### Etiology and pathology
**Most common cause: 
In Children: Adenoid Hypertrophy in children (Bilateral SOM)
In adults: Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (Unilateral)**
- Tubal occlusion due to nasopharyngeal causes, such as **adenoids** and benign or malignant tumours.
- Allergy: mucosal oedema of the Eustachian tube orifice.
- ==Hypogammaglobulinaemia== predisposes to an inflammatory response in the middle-ear cavity.
- Sterile effusion in the middle ear; the fluid is non-infective and there is no pus formation.

### Clinical features

**Symptoms**

- Painless blocked sensation in the ear.
- Conductive hearing loss (CHL).
- Delayed speech and poor school performance.
- Autophony.
- Nasal discharge and obstruction.
- No pain and no fever.

**Signs**

- Dull, lustreless TM with ==absent light reflex.==
- ==Retracted TM.==
- Fluid level.
- ==Air bubbles.==
- Decreased mobility of the TM.
- Foreshortened handle of malleus

### Hearing tests

| Test | Finding / interpretation |
|---|---|
| Tuning-fork test | Rinne negative; Weber lateralised to the affected ear; indicates conductive hearing loss. |
| Pure-tone audiometry | Conductive hearing loss. |
| ==Tympanometry (IOC==, as labelled in the source) | Type B tympanogram indicates middle-ear effusion. The source also illustrates Type A, B, C, As and Ad tympanograms. |

## Cholesteatoma

- **Definition:** presence of stratified squamous epithelium in the middle ear.
- **Congenital cholesteatoma:** EAC epithelium trapped in the middle ear during development (the source describes a cleft in the pouch).
- **Acquired cholesteatoma:**
  - Primary: no prior pathology.
  - Secondary: TM perforation or retraction pocket.

| Feature | Congenital cholesteatoma | Acquired cholesteatoma |
|---|---|---|
| Common sites | Middle ear, petrous apex, cerebellopontine angle | Attic; posterosuperior pars tensa |
| Otoscopy | Whitish mass behind an intact, otherwise normal TM | TM perforation or retraction pocket with debris |
| Levenson's criteria | White mass behind an intact TM, with no prior otorrhoea, surgery or disease | Not applicable |
| Laterality | Often unilateral (commonly left-sided in some series) | Commonly right-sided |

### Behaviour, symptoms, diagnosis and treatment

- The source calls cholesteatoma **unsafe** because it spreads like a tumour and destroys bone.
- Collagenase, alkaline phosphatase and proteolytic enzymes cause bone erosion.
- It spreads along the path of least resistance.
- The source lists the ==lateral semicircular canal== as the most commonly involved canal.
- **Symptoms:** hearing loss (most common); scanty, ==foul-smelling discharge.==
- **Diagnosis:**
  - Otoscopy under a microscope: whitish keratinous plaque (hallmark).
  - Investigation of choice: ==CT scan of the temporal bone to assess disease extent and bone erosion==; no contrast, as stated in the source.
  - Audiological tests: CHL.
- **Treatment:** medical therapy does not cure it; the source lists ==modified radical mastoidectomy (MRM)== as the surgery of choice.

### Cholesteatoma hearer and round-window shielding effect

- **Cholesteatoma hearer — mechanism:** cholesteatoma first e==rodes the incus (usually its long process)==, but ==can bridge the ossicular gap and allow sound conduction.==
- **Clinical significance:** hearing may be relatively preserved before surgery; a==fter removal of the cholesteatoma, the gap appears and postoperative hearing may worsen.==
- **Normal sound transmission (source sequence):** TM → malleus → incus → stapes → oval window → scala vestibuli → scala tympani → round window.
- **Sequential vibration:** oval window first, round window later.
- ==**Effect of TM perforation (as stated):** sound reaches both windows simultaneously; vibrations cancel each other, causing hearing loss.==

| Ear state | Discharge effect on hearing (as stated in the source) |
|---|---|
| Dry ear | ==Worse hearing==: sound hits both windows simultaneously. |
| Ear with discharge | ==Better hearing:== discharge shields the round window. |

### High-yield cholesteatoma points

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Mos==t common site of origin== | ==Prussak's space== (between pars flaccida and the neck of the malleus) |
| Most ==common site of recurrence== | Sinus tympani (also called the infrapyramidal space) |
| Most commonly ==eroded ossicle== | Long process of incus (==lenticular process==, as written in the source) |
| Canal involved | Lateral semicircular canal |

### Mastoidectomy

**Types listed in the source**

- Cortical mastoidectomy (Schwartze operation).
- Modified radical mastoidectomy (MRM).

**Cortical mastoidectomy**

- Mastoid air cells are removed.
- Mastoid is converted into a single cavity.
- Posterior canal wall is kept intact.
- Also called ==**intact-canal-wall / Schwartze operation**==.

**Modified radical mastoidectomy (MRM)**

- Mastoid air cells are removed.
- Posterior canal wall is removed.
- Mastoid, middle ear and external auditory canal are connected into a single cavity.
- Also called ==**canal-wall-down surgery**==.
- After MRM, **meatoplasty** is done to reshape the external auditory canal.

## Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM)

- **Definition:** chronic middle-ear infection of the whole or part of the cleft with ear discharge and permanent perforation.
- **Characteristic feature:** permanent TM perforation; the perforation edges are lined by squamous epithelium, preventing healing.

| Old classification | New classification |
| ------------------ | ------------------ |
| Tubotympanic       | Mucosal type       |
| Atticoantral       | Squamosal type     |

### Mucosal and squamosal types

- **Mucosal CSOM:**
	- Inactive mucosal: dry, no pus.
	- ==Active mucosal==: ==pus present.==
- **Squamosal type (atticoantral):**
	- Inactive: dry, with cholesteatoma associated.
	- ==Active==: ==pus present.==

**Medical treatment
	- **Aural toilet:** remove debris and an aural polyp before local treatment. Do not avulse an aural polyp.
	- **Ear drops:** neomycin + polymyxin + chloromycetin, or gentamicin, as listed.
	- **Systemic antibiotics:** limited use, only in acute exacerbation.
	- Keep the ear dry.

# 7. Otosclerosis

## Types and pathology

- Excessive bony deposition around:
  - **Footplate of stapes:** stapedial type.
  - **Round window:** cochlear type.
- **Stapedial type:** most common site is the ==fistula ante fenestrum== (anterior to the footplate of the stapes).
- **Cochlear type:** most common site starts at the ==round window and extends to the scala tympani.==.

### Stapedial type — etiology

- Hereditary: autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance.
- Sex: **female** > male; the source notes male predominance in India.
- Age of onset: 20–40 years.
- Increases in **==pregnancy==** and during **menopause.**
- **Measles** is a provocative factor.
- ==**Van der Hoeve syndrome==:** otosclerosis + blue sclera + osteogenesis imperfecta.

### Symptoms

**Stapedial type**

- Most common presenting feature: bilateral conductive hearing loss.
- **Paracusis Willisii:** the patient hears better in a noisy environment than in a silent environment.

**Cochlear type**

- Sensorineural hearing loss.
- Tinnitus.

| Feature | Stapedial | Cochlear |
|---|---|---|
| Incidence | Common | Less common |
| Hearing loss | Conductive | Sensorineural |
| Tinnitus / vertigo | Rare | Common |
| Paracusis Willisii | Common | No |

- The TM is normal in 90% of cases.

> [!example]- Source page visual — PDF page 50
> ![[images/chunk02_p050_source_page.jpeg]]

## Signs, tests and audiometry

- The TM is mobile.
- **Schwartze sign (10%):** reddish/pinkish hue; active lesions have a flamingo-pink hue on the promontory visible through an intact TM.
- **Classic presentation (source wording):** gradually progressive bilateral conductive hearing loss in a middle-aged female.

### Tests in stapedial otosclerosis

| Test | Finding |
|---|---|
| Rinne | Negative |
| Weber | Lateralised to abnormal ear |
| Absolute bone conduction | Normal |
| Gellé test | Negative |
| Tympanometry (labelled LOC in the source) | As-type graph |
| Stapedial reflex | Absent |
| Pure-tone audiometry | Carhart's notch: dip in the bone-conduction curve at 2000 Hz |

### Audiometry

- AC: lower frequencies are affected.
- BC: maximum dip at 2000 Hz, called **Carhart's notch**.
- The source says the notch reverses after stapedectomy.
- **Cochlear type:** a **cookie-bite curve** is a dip in the mid-frequency range of both AC and BC.

### Treatment

**Medical**

- Hearing aid.
- **Sodium fluoride (NaF):** given to arrest the focus and prevent further sensorineural loss due to cochlear otosclerosis.
  - **Mechanism of action (as listed):** inhibits osteoclastic and osteoblastic activity and inhibits release of proteolytic enzymes; only prevents further progression.
  - Indication: only in an active focus.
  - Contraindications continue on the next page.

## Otosclerosis — continued treatment

### Sodium fluoride contraindications listed in the source

- Pregnancy and lactation.
- Renal disease.
- Rheumatoid arthritis.
- Skeletal fluorosis.

### Surgical treatment

- Stapedectomy with placement of a prosthesis.
- Stapedotomy with placement of a prosthesis.
- **Lempert's fenestration operation:** reserved for cases where the footplate cannot be mobilised during stapedectomy.

| Indications for surgery | Contraindications for surgery |
|---|---|
| Hearing threshold is 30 dB, or air–bone gap is 25 dB or more. | Only hearing ear. |
| If there is bilateral otosclerosis, operate on the worse ear first. | Pregnancy. |
| — | Occupational: pilots and divers. |

### Local versus general anaesthesia

Local anaesthesia is preferred for stapes surgery because intraoperative assessment is possible:

- Vertigo can be assessed.
- Facial nerve function can be assessed.

### Stapedectomy / stapedotomy

- **Stapedectomy:** the stapes is removed completely and replaced with a prosthesis.
- **Stapedotomy:** a hole is made in the footplate of the stapes, and an opening is made from the incus to the oval window.
- A tear in the oval window can cause perilymph leakage; a fat plug is put in to prevent further leakage.
- The source labels stapedotomy as the treatment of choice.

**Management diagram: mature versus active focus**

- **Mature:** stapedotomy (preferably under local anaesthesia) + Teflon piston (the source says to operate on the worst ear first). After cutting the stapedius tendon and removing the stapes superstructure, the piston is placed between the long process of the incus and the opening on the stapes footplate.
- **Active:** NaF (sodium fluoride) increases maturation of the focus by inhibiting osteoclastic and proteolytic activity and accelerating osteoblastic activity.
- Other options: hearing aid, cochlear implant.

A 20–30-year-old patient presents with bilateral progressive hearing loss, first noticed after pregnancy, with a similar history in her mother. Hearing is better in noise (**paracusis Willisii**).

## Meniere's disease

Meniere's disease is characterised by:

- Vertigo (for 20 minutes to 24 hours)
- Sensorineural hearing loss.
- Tinnitus and aural fullness.
>Vertigo < 20 minutes is BPPV
### Pathogenesis shown in the source
**Causes:**
1. Mutation of Short arm of Chromosome 6
2. Earlier → it was thought *Idiopathic*

Increased endolymphatic pressure is attributed to increased production or reduced reabsorption of endolymph at the stria vascularis.
Overproduction or malabsorption leads to endolymphatic hypertension, gross enlargement of the membranous labyrinth and endolymphatic hydrops.

**Other predisposing factors listed:** allergy; sodium and water retention; hypoadrenalism and hypopituitarism; hypothyroidism.

### Cardinal features

| Feature                  | Description in the source                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Vertigo                  | Lasts minutes to hours. Severe, sudden vertigo with nystagmus, nausea, vomiting and vagal disturbances such as diarrhoea, cold sweat, pallor and bradycardia.                              |
| Hearing loss             | Sensorineural; low-frequency hearing loss; distortion of sound (**diplacusis**); intolerance to loud sounds due to recruitment → **Tullio phenomenon** (as stated); typically fluctuating. |
| Tinnitus                 | Low-pitched roaring type.                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Sense of fullness of ear | Aural fullness.                                                                                                                                                                            |

### Variants

- **Cochlear:** cochlear symptoms only, without vertigo.
- **Vestibular hydrops:** vertigo only, without cochlear symptoms.
- **Lermoyez syndrome:** tinnitus followed by hearing loss and vertigo.
- **Tumarkin's otolith crisis:** sudden drop attack without loss of consciousness.
- **Secondary Meniere's:** congenital/acquired syphilis, Paget's disease, post-stapedectomy.

### Treatment
## Treatment Protocol of Ménière's Disease
#### Step 1: Lifestyle Modification
- Dietary sodium restriction < 1.5-2 g/day(avoid excessive salt).
- Identify triggers; maintain regular sleep and hydration.
- Avoid caffeine/alcohol if they trigger symptoms.
#### Step 2: Medical Management
- **Acute attack:** Vestibular suppressants (e.g., meclizine) + antiemetics.
- **Maintenance:** Betahistine ± diuretic (e.g., thiazide).
#### Step 3: Intratympanic Therapy
- **Intratympanic corticosteroids:** Persistent symptoms; hearing-preserving option.
- **Intratympanic gentamicin:** Refractory vertigo despite nonablative treatment; risk of hearing loss.
#### Step 4: Surgery (Refractory Disease)
- **Serviceable hearing:** Vestibular nerve section in selected patients.
- **Non-serviceable hearing:** Labyrinthectomy.

## Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

- Transient positional vertigo.
- Due to displacement of otoliths (canaliths) from the utricle into a semicircular canal.
- Cause listed: trauma or sudden movement.
- Most commonly involved canal: posterior semicircular canal.
- **Mechanism:** otoliths stimulate canal receptors, causing sudden vertigo.

### Clinical features

- Sudden vertigo lasting < 20 seconds
- Nystagmus.
- As the cochlea is not involved, there is no hearing loss or tinnitus.
>Momentoary Vertigo (like 1 or 2 seconds) → psychogenic
### Diagnosis and treatment

- **Dix–Hallpike test.**
- Nystagmus pattern:
	- Posterior canal: upbeating + torsional.
	- Lateral canal: horizontal.
	- Superior canal: downbeating.
- **Treatment:** Epley manoeuvre (canalith-repositioning manoeuvre) for posterior-canal involvement.

## Vestibular neuritis and differential diagnosis of vertigo without hearing loss

### Vestibular neuritis

- Inflammation of the vestibular nerve only.
- Sudden vertigo; a single prolonged episode lasting days to weeks.
- No cochlear symptoms because only the vestibule is involved and the cochlea is not.
- Usually due to viral infection; mostly self-limiting.

### BPPV versus vestibular neuritis

|---|---|---|
| Definition | Transient positional vertigo due to displacement of otoliths | Acute inflammation limited to the vestibular nerve |
| History | Recurrent, brief episodes of spinning; each lasts seconds; triggered by specific head movements or changes in position | Sudden onset of severe vertigo; a single prolonged episode lasting days to weeks; hearing remains normal; no associated central neurological signs |
| Examination | Dix–Hallpike: upbeating, torsional nystagmus; fast phase directed toward the affected/hyperactive ear; suggests posterior semicircular canal (most common) | Peripheral-type nystagmus; fast phase directed toward the healthy/active side; positive head-thrust test (corrective saccades present) |

## Superior semicircular canal dehiscence (SSCD) 
- Also known as Superior Canal Dehiscence Syndrome (SCDS) and Minor's Disease
- It is a rare inner ear condition where the protective layer of bone covering the superior semicircular canal is missing or unusually thin. 

![[IMG_1295.jpeg]]![[IMG_1294.jpeg]]

### What Happens in SSCD
* **Third Window Effect:** Normally, the inner ear has only two pressure-release points (the oval window and the round window). A bony gap creates an abnormal "third window," which allows sound and pressure changes to improperly leak into or out of the balance and hearing systems.
* **Causes:** It can be congenital (incomplete bone development from birth), triggered by head trauma, or caused by progressive bone thinning over time.

### Common Symptoms
* **Balance & Vestibular Issues:** 
  * *Tullio phenomenon:* Vertigo, dizziness, or eye movements (nystagmus) triggered by loud noises.
  * *Hennebert's sign:* Vertigo or unsteadiness brought on by pressure changes, such as coughing, sneezing, straining, or blowing your nose.
  * Chronic unsteadiness or a feeling of "brain fog" and fatigue.
* **Hearing & Sound Issues:**
  * *Autophony:* Hearing your own voice, footsteps, eye movements, or heartbeat extremely loudly inside your head.
  * Conductive hearing loss (feeling like your ear is plugged).
  * Pulsatile tinnitus (a rhythmic whooshing sound matching your pulse).

### Diagnosis
* **High-Resolution CT Scan:** A specialized thin-slice CT scan of the temporal bone is the primary imaging test used to visualize the bony defect.
* **Clinical & Audiometric Tests:** Specialists use hearing tests and specialized balance evaluations (such as cVEMP — cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials) to confirm how the inner ear responds to sound and pressure.

### Treatment Options
* **Observation:** Mild or asymptomatic cases often require no intervention other than avoiding known triggers (like excessively loud environments).
* **Surgical Repair:** For debilitating symptoms, an otologist or neurotologist may recommend surgery. Common techniques include:
  * *Canal Plugging:* Sealing the canal to stop abnormal fluid and pressure shifting.
  * *Resurfacing:* Covering or rebuilding the thin or missing bone area.
  * *Approaches:* These are typically performed via a minimally invasive middle cranial fossa craniotomy or a transmastoid approach. Learn more about evaluation and treatment pathways at [Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://hopkinsmedicine.org) and the [Cleveland Clinic](https://clevelandclinic.org).

# 9. External Ear Diseases

## Congenital abnormalities of the pinna

- **Microtia:** pinna is completely small.
- **Macrotia:** pinna is completely big.
- **Anotia:** complete absence of the pinna.
  - Hearing rehabilitation: BAHA (same reason as microtia, as stated in the source).
- **Bat ear:** protrusion of the pinna.
- **Hearing rehabilitation:** BAHA (bone-anchored hearing aid).
  - A regular hearing aid cannot be accommodated because the pinna is small/absent.
  - BAHA bypasses the external and middle ear and transfers sound to the inner ear.

### Pre-auricular sinus

**Embryological basis**

- The pinna develops from six tubercles called the hillocks of His.
- First hillock → tragus.
- Remaining hillocks → rest of the pinna.
- Incomplete fusion of the first hillock with the rest → pre-auricular sinus.

**Clinical features:** small pit/hole near the ear; may become recurrently infected; purulent discharge from the pit/hole.

**Treatment:** repeated infections are an indication for surgery; otherwise, no surgery is required.

### Acquired abnormalities of the pinna: haematoma auris (boxer's ear)

- Repeated trauma to the ear (common in boxers).
- Accumulation of blood between cartilage and overlying perichondrium.
- Clinical features: painful swelling and bluish discoloration of the pinna.

## Haematoma auris, perichondritis, keloid and canal atresia

### Haematoma auris — treatment

- Incision and drainage.
- Pressure packing to prevent re-accumulation of blood.
- Antibiotics (marked mandatory in the source).
- Infection of a haematoma → perichondritis → cartilage infection → necrosis → pinna deformity.

### Perichondritis

- Infection of the entire pinna.
- Common causative organism: *Pseudomonas*.
- Clinical features: entire pinna is red, stiff and painful.
- **Hallmark:** sparing of the lobule, because the lobule contains fat and no cartilage.

### Keloid

**Etiology:** trauma (iatrogenic or accidental), including surgical incision or ear piercing.

**Pathophysiology listed:** abnormal epithelialisation; abnormal epithelial growth; tissue hypertrophy; very high recurrence rate.

**Treatment to prevent recurrence**

- First-line therapy:
  - Intralesional hyaluronidase injections.
  - Intralesional steroids.
  - The source lists 8–10 injections at 4–5-day intervals.
- If recurrence follows first-line treatment:
  - Short course of radiotherapy.
  - Short course of chemotherapy.

### Congenital abnormalities of the external auditory canal

**Complete atresia**

- No canal at all.
- Rehabilitation: a hearing aid cannot be inserted; BAHA is required.

## Acquired external auditory canal abnormalities: wax and otitis externa

### Wax (cerumen)

- The source calls wax the most common acquired cause of hearing loss in adults.
- **Source:** secretions from sebaceous glands of the external auditory canal, specifically the cartilaginous portion.
- **Functions:** acidic pH; protects against microorganisms (bacteria, viruses and other microorganisms); prevents material from reaching the TM.
- **Causes of accumulation:** excessive production; narrow canal, where cilia cannot move all wax outside.
- **Precipitating factor:** water entry during bathing/swimming causes wax to swell.
- **Clinical features:** pain, hearing loss, ear fullness and tinnitus (ringing in the ear).
- **Treatment:** wax-solytic agents; manual syringing.

### Otitis externa — classification

| Classification basis | Types |
|---|---|
| Extent | Localised / diffuse |
| Duration | Acute / chronic |
| Cause | Infectious (bacterial / viral) / non-infectious (eczema, seborrhoeic) |

### Localised otitis externa (furunculosis)

- Staphylococcal infection of a hair follicle in the cartilaginous portion of the external auditory canal.
- Clinical feature: pain increases with jaw movement.

## Otitis externa — continued

### Localised otitis externa (furunculosis): examination and treatment

- Pain on touching the ear; pain on chewing movement.
- **Examination:**
  - Positive tragal sign (pressing the tragus makes the patient wince in pain).
  - Purulent discharge: pathology in the external auditory canal.
  - Mucoid/mucopurulent discharge: disease in the middle ear (contrast given in the source).
  - Retroauricular groove is not obliterated, unlike mastoiditis, where there is deepening.
- **Treatment:** oral antibiotics and aural toileting.
- If furunculosis recurs, look for immunosuppression or diabetes.

### Diffuse otitis externa

- Also called swimmer's ear, Singapore ear or telephone ear.
- The source says it is seen in immunocompetent patients.
- **Important distinction printed in the source:** *Pseudomonas* infection in an immunocompetent patient → diffuse otitis externa; *Pseudomonas* infection in an immunocompromised patient → malignant otitis externa.
- **Predisposing exposures:** swimming/water, moisture, excessive telephone use and high humidity in tropical countries (examples: Singapore/Malaysia).
- **Pathophysiology:** normal wax pH is acidic and protective; exposure to humidity/water/moisture makes the pH alkaline; alkaline pH favours bacterial growth, causing canal infection.
- **Clinical features:** pain, purulent discharge, hearing loss and pain increased by jaw movement.
- **Examination:** entire external auditory canal is inflamed and oedematous (compared with localised swelling in furunculosis); tragal sign present.
- **Treatment:** anti-pseudomonal antibiotics and aural toileting.

> ![[images/chunk02_p064_source_page.jpeg]]

## Malignant otitis externa

- Not a malignancy; called “malignant” because it is very aggressive.
- Predisposing patient groups listed: elderly, diabetic and immunosuppressed patients.
- Cause: *Pseudomonas*.

### Clinical features

- Excruciating pain, often preventing sleep at night.
- Purulent discharge.
- Hearing loss (HOH, as abbreviated in the source).
- Sometimes fever.

### Examination

- **Hallmark:** granulation tissue at the bony-cartilaginous junction of the external auditory canal.
- **Cranial nerve involvement:**
  - Initially, seventh nerve (facial nerve) palsy.
  - The source attributes this to *Pseudomonas* being bone-eating, causing osteoclastic activity and bone erosion.
  - Later, skull-base involvement with ninth, tenth and eleventh nerve palsies.

### Investigations

- **Culture:** to confirm *Pseudomonas*.
- **Technetium-99 scan:** labelled the earliest diagnostic test in the source.
  - Radioisotopes are picked up by osteoclasts.
  - May be positive at a very early stage, before bony erosion is visible on CT.
  - Described as most important for early diagnosis.
- **CT scan:** identifies bony erosion and extent of disease, but the source says it is not as early as Technetium-99.
- **MRI:** identifies intracranial extension.
- **Gallium-67 scan:** used to monitor prognosis and decide whether to continue or discontinue therapy.
  - Gallium is taken up by neutrophils, described as cells of acute rather than chronic inflammation.
  - Initially, during active disease, the scan is positive.
  - After treatment, neutrophils disappear and the scan becomes negative (resolution).
  - If the scan remains positive, neutrophils remain present and therapy is continued.

## Malignant otitis externa — management; otomycosis; foreign body

### Management of malignant otitis externa

- Anti-pseudomonal antibiotics.
- Topical therapy.
- Symptomatic therapy.
- **Follow-up:** repeated cultures and repeated scans to determine whether disease is localised and regressing or remains extensive/progressing.

### Otomycosis

**Common causative organisms**

- *Aspergillus niger* (most common, as stated in the source).
- *Candida*.
- *Aspergillus fumigatus*.

**Characteristic appearance:** “wet newspaper appearance”.

- White discharge.
- Black spores overlying it.
- *Candida*: cotton-ball appearance.

**Clinical features:** itching (the source calls this the most important complaint), discharge, pain and possible secondary bacterial infection.

**Treatment:** topical antifungal; aural toileting (clean and dry the ear); patient education about keeping the ear dry.

### Foreign body in the external auditory canal

- Common in toddlers and young children.
- Unilateral foul-smelling discharge from the ear or nose in a child signifies a foreign body unless told otherwise (as phrased in the source).

**Methods of removal**

- Inorganic bead/pellet: remove with a probe.
- Graspable non-living object (paper, rubber): use crocodile forceps.
- Living insect:
  - First asphyxiate to prevent movement.
  - The source says not to use oil.

## Foreign body removal, syringing and keratosis obturans

### Living insect / organic foreign body / button battery

- Use 4% Xylocaine drops to make the insect immobile/asphyxiated, then remove it.
- **Organic foreign body or button battery: never syringe.**
  - Organic foreign body absorbs water, swells and causes further occlusion.
  - Button battery requires urgent removal with a probe.

### Syringing technique

- Instrument: Simpson's aural syringe.
- Water temperature: exactly 37°C; above or below this can cause a caloric reaction and syncope.
- Direction of ear pull: posterosuperior.
- Direction of water jet: posterosuperior.
- A straight-directed jet may perforate the TM.

**Contraindications to syringing**

- Hygroscopic foreign body.
- Organic foreign body.
- Button battery.
- TM perforation.

**Complications**

- Vertigo if water is not at body temperature, due to caloric stimulation.
- TM rupture if performed too forcefully.
- Reactivation of quiescent otitis media.

### Keratosis obturans

- Mass of keratin accumulating in the external auditory canal.
- **Pathophysiology:** normally, cilia push dead epithelium outside. In keratosis obturans, a ciliary-motility disorder prevents removal; dead epithelium accumulates in layers of keratin/epithelium, described as an “onion-skin” / lamellar arrangement.
- **Effects:** widening, not erosion; widening and pressure may cause seventh-nerve palsy.
- **Clinical features:** severe otalgia; ballooning of the canal.
- **Examination:** white mass with wax in the deep meatus, ulceration, granulations and widening of the EAC; facial nerve palsy is listed.
- History of chronic sinusitis and bronchiectasis.
- Diagnosis: clinical.

## Keratosis obturans — continued; herpes oticus; tympanic-membrane diseases

### Keratosis obturans treatment

- Soften with wax-solytic agents.
- Removal requires sedation or short general anaesthesia because of severe pain.
- Removal by instrumentation.

### Herpes oticus (Ramsay Hunt syndrome)

- **Causative organism:** herpes zoster virus (varicella-zoster virus).
- **Pathophysiology:** virus remains latent in the geniculate ganglion; reactivation occurs due to immunosuppression (the source adds “not low immunity”).
- **Clinical features:** pain and vesicles in the distribution of the facial nerve; LMN-type facial nerve palsy.
- **Treatment:** antivirals and steroids.
- **Prognosis:** poorer than Bell's palsy; recovery is not as good as in facial palsy (as printed).

### Diseases of the tympanic membrane: myringitis bullosa haemorrhagica

- Association: often associated with acute otitis media.
- **Causative organisms:** *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Mycoplasma*.
- **Clinical features:** pain, fever, hearing loss, vesicles/blebs on the TM and sometimes blood-stained discharge if a vesicle ruptures.
- Investigations: not required; examination is diagnostic.
- Treatment: antibiotics against *Streptococcus* and *Mycoplasma*; symptomatic therapy.

### Granular myringitis

- **Definition:** chronic infection of the TM; the TM is replaced by granulation tissue.
- **Causes:** bacterial infection, fungal infection and trauma.

## Granular myringitis and traumatic perforation

### Granular myringitis — continued

- Other cause listed: post-surgery.
- **Clinical features:** blood-stained ear discharge, pain and itching in the ear.
- **Examination:** granulations over the TM.
- **Treatment:** topical + systemic antibiotics; cauterisation (the source marks this as very important to prevent further granulations).

### Traumatic perforation

- **Etiology:** trauma causing a hole/perforation in the TM.
- **Clinical features:** pain, hearing loss and blood-stained discharge.
- Investigations: not required.

# 10. Hearing Loss

## Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSHL)

*Exam references on the page: INI-CET, May 2024; FMGE, January 2025.*

- **Definition:** >30 dB hearing loss at three consecutive frequencies within 3 days (72 hours).
- **Causes listed:** viral, autoimmune, post-infection; tumours such as acoustic neuroma; exposure to loud sounds.
	- Conductive: wax in the ear.
	- Sensorineural: cochlear or retrocochlear.
- **Diagnosis:** audiometry → MRI with contrast, used to rule out intracranial pathology compressing the nerve.
- The source audiogram shows a right-ear drop in hearing level to 50, 60 and 70 dB at 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz, respectively.
- **Treatment:** 
	- the earlier the diagnosis and treatment, the better the prognosis; 
	- **steroids** are the main treatment; 
		- **intratympanic and systemic steroids** are listed.
		- Systemic steroids are generally preferred because of less hassle and same results in both.

## SSHL management; noise-induced hearing loss, ototoxicity and presbycusis

### Management of sudden sensorineural hearing loss

- **Medical treatment:** oral steroids, intratympanic steroids, carbogen therapy and hyperbaric oxygen.
- **Rehabilitation:** if unilateral deafness, BAHA or CROS hearing aids.
- **Imaging:** MRI to exclude acoustic neuroma.

### Important information

- **Cross hearing:** a hearing aid that transmits sound from the ear with severe hearing loss to the opposite normal ear and brain.
- >90 dB is a loud sound (as printed).
- >150 dB causes acoustic trauma and permanent deafness (as printed).

### Conditions of the inner ear causing isolated hearing loss

| Noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) | Ototoxicity | Presbycusis |
|---|---|---|
| History of hearing loss after sudden or prolonged exposure to loud noise. May result in a temporary or permanent threshold shift. | History of exposure to ototoxic agents, such as aminoglycosides, antimalarials, analgesics, cytotoxic drugs, diuretics, vancomycin, erythromycin or azithromycin. | Occurs in individuals older than 50 years. Hearing loss is slowly progressive, bilateral and symmetrical. Most common cause of SNHL in adults, as stated in source. |

### Diagnostic tests and management

- **Pure-tone audiometry:** down-sloping curve indicating high-frequency hearing loss; an acoustic dip at 4 kHz is characteristic of NIHL.
- **High-frequency audiometry:** demonstrates high-frequency hearing loss.
- **Otoacoustic emissions (OAE):** absent in NIHL and ototoxicity due to early involvement of outer hair cells.
- **Management:** hearing aids; cochlear implant.

### Frequently asked PYQ points

- Safe noise exposure limit: 85 dB for 8 hours/day, 5 days/week.
- Prevention of NIHL: earplugs (30 dB protection) and earmuffs (40 dB protection).
- Aminoglycosides and cytotoxic drugs cause irreversible hearing loss.
- Cochleotoxic aminoglycosides (mnemonic **KAN**): kanamycin, amikacin, neomycin (neomycin labelled most toxic in the source).
- Streptomycin, gentamicin and tobramycin are described as predominantly vestibulotoxic.
- Loop diuretics block the Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ channel of the stria vascularis.
- Most common type of presbycusis: neural. Other types listed: sensory, cochlear conductive and strial.

## Childhood hearing loss and auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder

### Etiology of childhood hearing impairment

| Congenital causes (prenatal) — 2/3 | Acquired causes (perinatal and postnatal) — 1/3 |
|---|---|
| **Genetic causes (50%)**: non-syndromic 70%; syndromic 30%. | **Infections:** meningitis (labelled most common), measles, mumps, varicella, herpes, toxoplasmosis and chronic otitis media. |
| **Non-genetic causes (50%)**: maternal infections, including CMV (labelled most common), rubella, syphilis and toxoplasmosis; drug exposure and maternal factors, including ototoxic drugs, substance abuse and endocrine disorders during pregnancy. | **Other causes:** hypoxia; ARDS; prolonged difficult labour; prematurity; hyperbilirubinaemia; ototoxic drugs; noise trauma/head trauma; NICU admission for more than 5 days, associated with a high incidence of auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder. |

The source notes that the listed prenatal factors affect labyrinth development during the first trimester.

### Auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder (ANSD)

- Patients hear sounds but have poor speech discrimination.
- A higher incidence is stated for neonates admitted to the ICU.

**Pathophysiology — important concept (as presented)**

- Dysfunction occurs at the level of cochlear inner hair cells (IHC), resulting in inadequate signal transmission to the auditory nerve.
- Demyelination of auditory-pathway nerves is seen.
- Loss of axons leads to dyssynchrony.
- Sound can travel, reach and be perceived by the auditory cortex; however, speech (complex sounds) cannot be identified because synchronisation is lost.

**Presentation of a child with ANSD**

- The child can hear sounds and responds to sound.
- The child does not understand complex sounds, such as speech, which require synchronisation.
- The condition is usually detected when the child is slightly older.

**Test results pointing toward ANSD**

- PTA: normal or mild-to-moderate SNHL, because the child can perceive sound stimuli.
- Management: hearing aids; cochlear implant.

## ANSD testing, neonatal screening and behavioural observation audiometry

### ANSD — continued test findings

- **Speech audiometry / speech recognition / speech discrimination:** speech intelligibility is disproportionately poorer than expected for the degree of hearing loss.
- **OAE:** present because outer hair cells are normal; therefore, BERA is used for hearing screening in NICU neonates, since OAE may be normal (PASS) and ANSD can be missed.
- **BERA:** abnormal.
- **MLR** (middle-latency response, 12–50 ms) and **LLR / cortical responses** (50–500 ms), assessed by CERA, may be abnormal or absent.

### Neonatal hearing screening

- Every child at birth should undergo an otoacoustic-emission test.
- If OAE is normal, the patient passes and no higher tests are required (as written in the source).

2. If TEOAE is present (**PASS**):
   - No high-risk factors → no further screening.

3. If TEOAE is absent: clean the EAC of meconium / vernix caseosa / wax and repeat TEOAE after 7–10 days.
4. If repeat TEOAE passes, follow the pathway in the source diagram. If absent / **REFER**, perform tympanometry.

   - **Auditory nerve defect:** ABI at 1.5–2 years.

## Behavioural observation audiometry and rehabilitation

### BOA

Behavioural observation audiometry assesses a child's hearing by observing changes in behaviour or response following presentation of a sound stimulus.

**When to perform BOA**

- Per the source, BOA is performed in children older than 6 months who present with complaints of hearing loss.
- This is followed by detailed history-taking and clinical examination.
- If findings are normal up to the TM, BOA is carried out.
- If BOA findings are abnormal or the child is unresponsive, the child is subjected to electrophysiological tests, starting with OAE followed by BERA.

|---|---|---|

## BAHA, cochlear implants and auditory brainstem implant

### BAHA — bone-anchored hearing aid

- A titanium screw is integrated into the squamous temporal bone.
- Once it has integrated with bone (usually in 6 months), the external component is fitted over the screw.
- The processor sends sound to the opposite ear and also to the same ear.
- Hence, the source lists its use in unilateral sensorineural hearing loss.

### Cochlear implants

- **Inventor:** developed by William F. House, described as the Father of Neurotology.
- **Indications:** severe-to-profound hearing loss in both ears with no significant benefit from hearing-aid use; implantation can be considered from 1 year of age onwards.
- **Contraindications listed:** non-formation of the cochlea (Michel aplasia); absence of the cochlear nerve; presence of central auditory-pathway lesions.

**Components of the implant**

- **Internal:** receiver-stimulator unit; electrode array.
- **External:** microphone; speech processor; transmitting coil.
- Has external and internal components. The external component has a microphone and speech processor. It transfers sound to the internal component (receiver / speech stimulator).
- The source places the reference electrode in the squamous temporal region and the internal electrode in the scala tympani of the cochlea.

**Commonly asked PYQs listed in the source**

- The procedure may be performed starting at 1 year of age.
- Entry into the middle ear is achieved via the facial recess.
- Entry into the inner ear occurs through the round window.
- The electrode is positioned in the scala tympani.
- It functions as a substitute for the organ of Corti.
- Neural activation occurs at the level of the cochlear nerve.

### Cochlear implant diagram; auditory brainstem implant

- The source's cochlear-implant diagram labels the external transmitter, microphone/speech processor/battery pack, implanted receiver/stimulator, reference electrode, intracochlear electrodes and electrode array.

### Definition of deafness / calculating hearing disability

**Monaural (one-ear) loss**

1. Perform the calculation for the left and right ears individually.
2. Determine the pure-tone average (PTA): add hearing thresholds at 500, 1000, 2000 and 3000 Hz, then divide by 4.
3. Apply the “Low Fence”: subtract 25 dB from the average, as 25 dB is considered the limit of normal hearing.
4. Multiply by 1.5: every decibel above the 25 dB baseline equals 1.5%.
5. **Formula:** `(PTA - 25) × 1.5 = % monaural impairment`.

**Binaural (both-ear) loss**

- The “better ear” is weighted more heavily because it contributes more to daily functioning.
- Weighted average formula as printed: `[(% better ear × 5) + (% worse ear)] ÷ 6 = % binaural hearing disability`.
