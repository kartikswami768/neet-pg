# 1. Anatomy of the Ear

## Anatomy of the outer ear

### Components of the external ear

- Pinna.
- External auditory canal (EAC).
- Tympanic membrane (TM).

### Pinna

- **Cymba conchae:** cartilaginous landmark for the mastoid antrum.
- **Sites devoid of cartilage:**
  - **Lobule:** site to harvest a fat graft for stapedectomy.
  - **Incisura terminalis:** site of Lempert endaural incision.
  - **Darwin's tubercle:** prominence at the junction of the upper one-third and lower two-thirds of the helix.

### External auditory canal (EAC)

| Cartilaginous EAC | Bony EAC |
|---|---|
| Outer one-third (8 mm) | Inner two-thirds (16 mm) |
| Completely developed at birth | Not developed at birth; continues to develop after birth |
| Directed upward, backward and medially | Directed downward, forward and medially |
| Contains thick, stratified squamous keratinized skin with ceruminous (apocrine) glands and hair follicles | Contains thin, stratified squamous keratinized skin without skin appendages; the narrowest part of the EAC is the isthmus, 5-6 mm lateral to the TM |
| Has an anteroinferior deficiency called the fissures of Santorini | Has a deficiency called the foramen of Huschke |

*NEET PG 2020; FMGE 2021.*

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 3)
> ![[images/chunk01_p003_source_page.jpeg]]

### Viewing the tympanic membrane

- In neonates, because the bony part of the EAC is not developed, the pinna is drawn downward and backward to view the TM.
- To straighten the EAC and visualize the TM in adults, the pinna should be pulled posterosuperiorly.
- The bony-cartilaginous junction, fissures of Santorini (deficiency in the cartilaginous part), and foramen of Huschke (deficiency in the bony part) are potential paths for spread of infections and tumours from the EAC to the base of the skull and parotid, and vice versa.

## Parts of the tympanic membrane

### Pars tensa and pars flaccida

| Feature | Pars tensa | Pars flaccida |
|---|---|---|
| Annulus | Present | Absent |
| Number of layers | 3 | 2 |
| Umbo and cone of light | Umbo in the centre; cone of light in the anteroinferior part | — |

### Other features

- Colour: pearly grey and translucent.
- Angle with EAC: 55 degrees.
- Effective vibratory surface area: 55 mm².
- Total surface area: 90 mm².
- Thickness: 0.1 mm.
- **Pars flaccida:** also called Shrapnell's membrane.
- **Umbo:** point of attachment of the tip of the handle of the malleus to the TM.
- **Side identification (right vs left):** based on the position of the cone of light:
  - Right TM: cone of light is between 3 and 6 o'clock.
  - Left TM: cone of light is between 6 and 9 o'clock.
- Side identification may also be based on the malleolar folds:
  - Short fold = anterior malleolar fold.
  - Long fold = posterior malleolar fold.

## Tympanic membrane perforations

### Classification

- **Central perforation:** located in the pars tensa.
- **Attic perforation:** located in the pars flaccida.
- **Small central:** occupies one quadrant of the pars tensa.
- **Large central:** occupies more than one quadrant but not all; some pars tensa remains circumferentially.
- **Subtotal:** occupies all quadrants; annulus remains intact.
- **Total:** occupies all quadrants and includes destruction of the annulus.
- **Marginal:** occupies one quadrant and includes the annulus.

### Structures visible through a subtotal perforation

- Malleus, specifically the handle.
- Incus, specifically the long process.
- Stapes: head, tendon and crura.
- Oval window, covered by the footplate of the stapes.
- Round window, located inferior to the oval window.
- Eustachian tube opening, seen anteroinferiorly.
- Promontory, a projection on the medial wall formed by the basal turn of the cochlea.
- Facial nerve impression, located above the oval window.

## Classifications of retractions

### Pars flaccida: Toss classification

- **Grade 0:** normal.
- **Grade 1:** small dimpling; does not touch the neck of the malleus.
- **Grade 2:** retracted to touch the neck of the malleus.
- **Grade 3:** adherent to the neck of the malleus and extending beyond the scutum (outer attic wall).
- **Grade 4:** retracted to the neck, extending beyond the scutum, with resorption of the outer attic wall.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 5)

### Pars tensa: Sade classification

- **Grade 1:** membrane slightly retracted.
- **Grade 2:** membrane touching the incus.
- **Grade 3:** membrane touching the promontory but remaining mobile.
- **Grade 4:** membrane touching the promontory, adherent and not mobile.
- **Grade 5:** retraction with an atelectatic membrane and perforation.

## Middle ear

### Middle ear cleft

Air enters the middle ear via the Eustachian tube, then passes through the middle ear proper, attic, aditus and mastoid antrum.

### Middle ear anatomy

| Wall / feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Roof | Tegmen tympani |
| Floor | Bone separating the middle ear from the jugular bulb |
| Anterior wall | Communicates with the Eustachian tube; bone separates it from the carotid; communicates with the canal for tensor tympani |
| Posterior wall | Pyramid and aditus |
| Lateral wall | TM and outer attic wall, called the scutum |

### Medial wall and landmarks

| Structure | Description / clinical significance |
|---|---|
| Impression of lateral semicircular canal | Prominence of the lateral semicircular canal |
| Promontory | Impression of the cochlea; formed by the basal turn of the cochlea (associated in the source with high-frequency sounds) |
| Oval window | Foot process of the stapes transmits sound vibrations to the scala vestibuli |
| Facial canal | Passes between the semicircular canal and the promontory; the tympanic/horizontal/third segment is described as the most commonly dehiscent segment of the facial nerve |
| Round window | Covered by a secondary TM; cochlear implant electrodes are inserted into the inner ear (scala tympani) and drugs may be delivered through this window |
| Processus cochleariformis | Landmark for the first genu of the facial nerve and geniculate ganglion; tensor tympani takes a lateral turn here to attach to the upper end of the malleus handle |

It supplies sensory fibres to the middle ear, mastoid, TM and Eustachian tube; parasympathetic supply reaches the parotid through the lesser petrosal nerve and otic ganglion. |

### Recesses

| Recess | Boundaries | Clinical note |
|---|---|---|
| Facial recess | Medial wall: vertical segment of the facial nerve; lateral wall: chorda tympani nerve; superior wall: fossa incudis | Important surgical corridor (posterior tympanotomy) to access the middle ear without damaging ossicles; posterior tympanotomy is used for cochlear implant surgery |
| Sinus tympani | Superior boundary: ponticulus (bony ridge between oval window and facial canal); inferior boundary: subiculum (bony ridge between round window and facial canal) | Hidden recess behind the facial nerve; a notorious site for residual cholesteatoma |

## Nerve supply of the ear

### Outer ear

- **Lesser occipital nerve:** supplies a smaller portion of the pinna.
- **Auriculotemporal nerve:** upper half anteriorly, roof and anterior TM.
- **Greater auricular nerve:** lower half of the pinna.
- **Auricular branch of the tenth nerve (Arnold's nerve):** supplies the floor, posterior wall and posterior TM.

### Middle ear

- Supplied by Jacobson's nerve, the tympanic branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve.

### Inner ear

- Cochlea: cochlear nerve.
- Vestibule: vestibular nerve.

### Tympanic plexus

- Location: on the promontory.
- Formed by the glossopharyngeal nerve and sympathetic plexus covering the carotid.

### PYQs / one-liners: ossicles

**Malleus**

- Largest ossicle of the middle ear.
- The tensor tympani tendon inserts on the medial surface of the upper handle.
- The chorda tympani passes just above the tendon insertion, posterior to the anterior wall.

**Incus**

- The body articulates with the malleus head, forming the incudomalleolar joint (saddle-type synovial joint).
- The most common ossicle to undergo necrosis in otitis media is the long process of the incus.
- The lenticular process is most vulnerable because of its precarious blood supply.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 8)
> ![[images/chunk01_p008_source_page.jpeg]]

**Stapes**

- Smallest ossicle in the entire body.
- Articulates with the lenticular process of the incus, forming the incudostapedial joint (ball-and-socket type).
- The stapedius tendon inserts on the posterior part of the neck.
- The footplate lies on the oval window and transmits sound to the inner ear.
- Footplate fixation is seen in otosclerosis.

## Anatomy of the inner ear

- The inner ear is a bony labyrinth.
- The central chamber is the vestibule.
- Anteriorly: cochlea.
- Posteriorly: semicircular canals.

### Vestibule

- The spherical recess contains the saccule.
- The elliptical recess contains the utricle, which receives the openings of the semicircular canal ducts.
- The spherical and elliptical recesses are parts of the bony labyrinth; the saccule and utricle are parts of the membranous labyrinth.
- Neurosensory epithelium within the utricle and saccule is called the maculae.

### Semicircular canals

- Responsible for maintaining body posture.
- Each canal has a dilated (ampullated) end and a non-dilated end.
- **Crus commune:** non-dilated ends of the superior and posterior canals join together.
- The ampullated end contains neurosensory epithelium called a **crista**.

### Cochlea

- Spiral structure with a central axis called the **modiolus**, which contains the cochlear artery and cochlear nerve.
- The central chamber is the cochlear duct (scala media), between the scala vestibuli and scala tympani, with the basilar membrane below it.
- Neurosensory epithelium above the basilar membrane is the **organ of Corti**, which helps with hearing.
- Neurosensory epithelium in the scala media is the **stria vascularis**, responsible for production and absorption of endolymph.

*NEET PG 2020, 2022, 2024; FMGE 2023, 2024.*

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 9)
> ![[images/chunk01_p009_source_page.jpeg]]

### Cochlear scalae and inner-ear fluids

- The scala vestibuli and scala tympani are bony labyrinths; the scala media is a membranous labyrinth.
- At the base, the scala vestibuli opens at the oval window and the scala tympani opens at the round window.
- At the apex, the scala vestibuli and scala tympani communicate through the **helicotrema**.
- The scala media lies between the scala vestibuli and scala tympani.

#### Endolymph

- Produced and reabsorbed by the stria vascularis.
- The stria vascularis is vascular epithelium located in the scala media.
- Contains sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺) and chloride (2Cl⁻) channels for regulation.
- The source notes that diuretic use affects Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ channels and may cause hearing loss and vertigo.

#### Perilymph

- Composition: an ultrafiltrate of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
- Flow described in the source: CSF → scala tympani → space between the membranous and bony labyrinths.
- The cochlear aqueduct is the duct facilitating communication between the scala tympani and the subarachnoid space.
- Endolymph is inside the membranous labyrinth; perilymph is between the membranous and bony coverings.

### Endolymphatic duct and membranous structures

- The vestibule (central chamber) has spherical and elliptical recesses in its bony part; its membranous part contains the saccule and utricle.
- Saccular duct + utricular duct → endolymphatic duct.
- The endolymphatic duct opens into the endolymphatic sac.
- The utricle receives the openings of the semicircular canal ducts via five openings.
- The saccule communicates anteriorly with the cochlea via the cochlear duct (scala media).

**Bony coverings of membranous structures**

- Cochlear duct: scala vestibuli above and scala tympani below.
- Saccule: spherical recess.
- Utricle: elliptical recess.
- Semicircular canal ducts: semicircular canals.
- Endolymphatic duct: vestibular aqueduct.

# 2. Physiology of Hearing

## Conductive pathway

### Path of sound (air conduction)

1. External auditory canal.
2. Tympanic membrane.
3. Malleus.
4. Incus.
5. Stapes (footplate).
6. Oval window.

### Sound transmission in the inner ear

- Oval window → scala vestibuli.
- Scala vestibuli → scala tympani (through inner-ear fluid).
- Ends at the round window.

### Phase difference (space difference)

- Sound hits the oval window first and the round window later.
- This phase difference is necessary (time difference).
- If both windows vibrate simultaneously:
  - No phase difference.
  - No proper mechanical vibration.
  - No stimulation of the organ of Corti.
  - No conversion into electrical energy.

## Sensory-neural pathway

- Begins from the cochlea onward.
- Beyond the middle ear = sensory-neural pathway.

## Impedance matching

- Middle ear = air.
- Inner ear = fluid (perilymph + endolymph).
- Sound moving from air to fluid faces resistance.
- The impedance-matching mechanism helps overcome this resistance.

### Impedance-matching mechanisms

#### Area ratio

Condensation of sound travelling from a larger tympanic membrane to a smaller footplate of the stapes.

| Parameter | Value / calculation |
|---|---:|
| Effective vibratory area of the tympanic membrane | 45 mm² |
| Stapes footplate area | 3.2 mm² |
| Calculation | 45 ÷ 3.2 = 14:1 |
| If vibratory area = 55 mm² | 55 ÷ 3.2 = 17:1 |
| Area-ratio range | 14-17:1 |

#### Lever ratio

Condensation of sound travelling from the larger malleus to the smaller incus.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | Malleus (larger) → incus (smaller) |
| Lever ratio | 1.3:1 |

#### Total transformer ratio

Total transformer ratio = areal ratio × lever ratio.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Effective vibratory area | 45 mm² / 55 mm² |
| Footplate area | 3.2 mm² |
| Areal ratio | 14:1 / 17:1 |
| Lever ratio | 1.3:1 |
| Total transformer ratio | 14 × 1.3 = 18:1; 17 × 1.3 = 22:1 |

## Functions of different levels

| Function | Site | Key details |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency (pitch) determination | Cochlea | Tonotopic organization present |
| Amplitude (intensity) detection | Cochlear nerve | Detects loudness of sound |
| Feature detection | Higher auditory centres | Recognition of type of sound (human, animal); human-voice discrimination |

# 3. Assessment of Hearing

## Tuning fork tests

### Air conduction and bone conduction

**Air conduction (AC)**

- Tuning fork placed in front of the pinna.
- Pathway: external auditory canal → tympanic membrane → malleus → incus → stapes → inner ear (organ of Corti).
- The organ of Corti converts mechanical energy to electrical energy → eighth nerve → brain.
- AC = conductive pathway + sensory-neural pathway.

**Bone conduction (BC)**

- Tuning fork placed on the mastoid bone.
- Pathway: vibrations go directly to the cochlea → brain.
- Bypasses the external auditory canal and middle ear.
- BC = sensory-neural pathway alone.

### Rinne test

| Result | Findings | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Rinne positive | AC > BC; sound heard longer and louder via air conduction | Normal hearing or sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) |
| Rinne negative | BC > AC; bone conduction heard longer and louder than air conduction | Conductive hearing loss |
| False-negative Rinne | Transcranial transmission of sound | Unilateral severe-to-profound SNHL (“dead ear”) |

*AIIMS 2020; FMGE 2020, 2021; INICET 2022, 2023; NEET PG 2023.*

### Rinne test and degree of hearing loss

| 256 Hz | 512 Hz | 1024 Hz | Degree of hearing loss | Threshold |
| ------ | ------ | ------- | ---------------------- | --------- |
| −      | +      | +       | Mild (15-30 dB)        | 40-55 dB  |
| −      | −      | +       | Moderate (30-45 dB)    | 55-70 dB  |
| −      | −      | −       | Severe (>45 dB)        | >70 dB    |

### Weber test

- **Method:** tuning fork placed in the midline.
- **Normal:** sound distributed equally to both ears.
- **Lateralization:**
	- Sound goes towards the diseased ear → conductive hearing loss.
	- Sound goes towards the normal/opposite ear → SNHL.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 16)
> ![[images/chunk01_p016_source_page.jpeg]]

- Example: lateralization to the right ear implies right conductive hearing loss or left SNHL.
- Note: combine with Rinne and absolute bone-conduction tests for diagnosis.

### Absolute bone-conduction (ABC) test

- Tests the sensory-neural pathway and identifies SNHL.
- **Method:** patient and examiner occlude the tragus; the test assumes the examiner has normal hearing sensitivity. Compare the duration of BC in the patient and examiner.
- **Inference:**
  - Normal: patient and examiner hear for the same duration.
  - SNHL: patient hears for a shorter duration than the examiner (ABC shortened).

## Pure-tone audiometry (PTA)

- **Type:** subjective test (patient gives a response); the source notes that it is not an objective test despite using a machine.
- Conducted in a soundproof room.
- AC and BC are tested separately, from a higher dB level to a lower dB level.
- The lowest threshold is marked on the audiogram.

### Symbols used in an audiogram

| Test | Right ear | Left ear |
|---|---|---|
| AC, unmasked | O | X |
| AC, masked | △ | □ |
| BC, unmasked | < | > |
| BC, masked | [ | ] |
| No response | Arrow-marked O (as shown) | Cross-marked X (as shown) |

## Impedance audiometry

- Components:
  1. Tympanometry.
  2. Stapedial reflex.

### Tympanometry

- Test used to assess middle-ear pathologies.
- **Type:** objective test (the machine gives the response, not the patient).
- Parameters measured:
  - Middle-ear pressure.
  - Mobility of the tympanic membrane.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 20)
> ![[images/chunk01_p020_source_page.jpeg]]

- **Mechanism:** a probe is placed in the canal; canal pressure is varied to match middle-ear pressure. The TM vibrates maximally when canal pressure equals middle-ear pressure.

### Tympanogram interpretation

- **Type A:** ascending and descending curve with a peak at 0; peak occupies approximately half the length of the Y-axis; seen in a normal middle ear.
- **Type As:** peak at 0 but height is less than half of the Y-axis; seen in otosclerosis.
- **Type Ad:** peak at or near 0 but very high/open (off the chart); seen in ossicular discontinuity, for example after head injury/trauma.
- **Type B:** single flat line with no peak; seen with fluid or effusion in the middle ear (OME).
- **Type C:** curve with peak shifted to negative pressure (−200 to −300 daPa); seen in Eustachian tube dysfunction/negative middle-ear pressure.

### Stapedial reflex

- **Definition:** a loud sound to one ear causes bilateral contraction of the stapedius muscles.
- **Reflex pathway:** loud sound → external/middle/inner ear → eighth nerve → cochlear nucleus → ipsilateral and contralateral superior olivary complex (lateral decussation occurs here) → bilateral facial nerve nuclei → nerve to stapedius (branch of seventh nerve) → stapedius muscle contraction.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 21)
> ![[images/chunk01_p021_source_page.jpeg]]

### Important points about the stapedius reflex

| Point | Source note |
|---|---|
| Afferent nerve | Eighth cranial nerve |
| Centre | Superior olivary complex |
| Efferent nerve | Seventh cranial nerve |
| Nerves tested | Eighth and seventh cranial nerves |
| Loud sound given to right ear | Right ear: eighth and seventh nerves; left ear: seventh nerve |
| Bilateral reflex | Listed as an important result in the source |
| Right seventh-nerve palsy | Reflex absent on the right side |
| Hyperacusis | Normal sounds appear abnormally loud |

**Important stapedius-reflex results**

- CHL (otosclerosis, SOM): absent.
- Sensory/cochlear hearing loss: threshold reduced; reflex may be present at lower loudness due to recruitment.
- Neural/retrocochlear hearing loss: stapedial-reflex decay present; the reflex dies out quickly despite continuous loud sound.
- Malingering: normal.
- The source notes its use when evaluating hearing in infants/young children unable to undergo tuning-fork tests/audiometry.

## Speech audiometry

Two tests are listed: **Speech Reception Threshold (SRT)** and **Speech Discrimination Score (SDS)**.

| Speech Reception Threshold (SRT) | Speech Discrimination Score (SDS) |
|---|---|
| Threshold at which 50% of spoken words are repeated correctly | Ability of a patient to correctly identify phonetically balanced words, such as “fox” and “box” |
| Both SRT and SDS are affected in retrocochlear pathology | SDS <20% strongly suggests retrocochlear pathology |

## Electrocochleography (ECoG)

- **Type:** objective test for inner-ear assessment.
- **Parameters:** measures inner-ear potentials:
  - Cochlear microphonics.
  - Summating potential (SP).
  - Action potential (AP).
- Cochlear microphonics represent activity of hair cells (shown in the source diagram).
- **Method:** electrode placed transtympanically through the tympanic membrane, over the promontory (basal turn of the cochlea).
- **Interpretation:**
  - Normal SP/AP ratio: <30%.
  - Abnormal ratio (>30%): indicates Ménière's disease.
- The source calls ECoG the investigation of choice (IOC) for identifying Ménière's disease.

## Otoacoustic emissions (OAE)

- Emissions coming from outer hair cells of the cochlea.
- **Significance:**
  - Best audiometric screening test for neonates.
  - Non-invasive, repeatable, easily applicable and cost-effective.
  - Screening investigation of choice in the neonatal hearing-screening programme.
- **Uses** (continued on the following page):

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 22)
> ![[images/chunk01_p022_source_page.jpeg]]

- Differentiate cochlear from retrocochlear pathology.
- Early detection of noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) in high-risk professions (factory, airline).
- Testing is done in awake babies using a probe in the external auditory canal.

### OAE result interpretation

- **OAE present (“Pass”):** outer hair cells of the cochlea are normal. If SNHL persists, retrocochlear pathology is considered likely.
- **OAE absent (“Refer”):** indicates an abnormality (cochlear pathology or middle-ear pathology blocking emissions). It does not confirm hearing loss; further testing is required.

### Management protocol for “refer” (absent OAE)

- If OAE is present, it is called a pass: conclusion in the source is normal inner ear/normal outer hair cells of the cochlea.
- If OAE is absent, refer for:
  - **Tympanometry** to rule out middle-ear pathology. The source notes that middle-ear pathology causing >35 dB hearing loss may prevent sufficient sound from reaching the inner ear and returning to the probe in the EAC.
  - **BERA** for three reasons listed in the source: reconfirm cochlear pathology; determine the exact hearing threshold/level; and check whether the nerve is functional.
- The source states that moderate/moderately severe hearing loss requires a hearing aid, while severe-to-profound hearing loss may require a cochlear implant if there is no response to a hearing-aid trial.

## BERA (Brainstem Evoked Response Audiometry)

- Typically done in a sleeping child.
- Assesses the auditory pathway from the eighth nerve up to the brainstem.
- **Type:** non-invasive objective test.
- Wave generation: 5-7 waves are generated; each represents a part of the pathway.

### Clinical uses of BERA

- Best audiometric test (as labelled in the source).
- Screens for hearing loss in neonates in ICU.
- Confirms hearing loss in neonates and infants and helps find the hearing threshold.
- Differentiates cochlear and retrocochlear hearing loss.
- Used for retrocochlear pathology, including acoustic neuroma.
- Used for non-organic hearing loss (malingering).

| Test | What it measures / timing in source |

| LLR (late-latency response / cortical responses) | Electrical activity 50-500 ms after sound stimulus; tested by CERA (Cortical Evoked Response Audiometry) |

The source's “best test for” list includes confirmatory testing for hearing loss, non-organic hearing loss, acoustic neuroma, infants/uncooperative patients, and differentiating cochlear from retrocochlear loss.

## Recruitment: test for cochlear pathology

- A phenomenon in which there is abnormal growth in sound intensity; a low-intensity sound is perceived as loud.
- Seen in cochlear pathology.
- **Type:** subjective test (patient gives a response).

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 24)
> ![[images/chunk01_p024_source_page.jpeg]]

### SISI (Short Increment Sensitivity Index)

- Test for recruitment (cochlear pathology).
- **Method:** sound intensity increased/decreased by 1 dB every 5 seconds.
- Normal: cannot appreciate the small change.
- Cochlear pathology: can appreciate the small change (recruitment present).

### Threshold tone-decay test

- Test for retrocochlear pathology.
- **Type:** subjective test.
- **Method:** sound stimulus given continuously.
- Normal: heard for 60 seconds or more.
- Retrocochlear pathology: nerve undergoes fatigue; tone is heard for <60 seconds.

### Summary: subjective vs objective tests

| Pathology | Subjective tests | Objective tests |
|---|---|---|
| Cochlear pathology | Pure-tone audiometry; speech audiometry; recruitment (SISI) | OAE; electrocochleography |
| Retrocochlear pathology | Speech discrimination score; tone-decay test | BERA (very important); auditory steady-state response (ASSR) |

## Approach to a patient with hearing loss

The source describes a patient with hearing loss, no pain, no discharge and an intact tympanic membrane.

### Step 1: Tuning-fork tests

- **Rinne test:**
  - AC > BC → Rinne positive → normal hearing or SNHL.
  - AC < BC → Rinne negative → conductive hearing loss.
- **Weber test:**
  - Lateralizes to diseased ear → conductive loss.
  - Lateralizes to opposite ear → SNHL.
- **Absolute bone-conduction test:** differentiates conductive from sensorineural hearing loss.

### Step 2: PTA

- Confirms the type of hearing loss.
- Quantifies the degree of hearing loss.
- If conductive hearing loss (CHL) → tympanometry:
  - Assesses middle-ear pressure and compliance.
  - Normal TM + CHL → middle-ear pathology.
- If SNHL is present → BERA / OAE:
  - OAE identifies cochlear pathology.
  - BERA identifies retrocochlear pathology.

# 4. Assessment of the Vestibular System

## Nystagmus

### Approach

1. Identify central vs peripheral components of nystagmus.
2. Determine the rule/direction of nystagmus and what it indicates.
3. Determine how to evoke nystagmus by stimulating the labyrinth.

### Three ways to evoke nystagmus

- Temperature change → caloric test.
- Pressure change → fistula test.
- Position change → Dix-Hallpike test.

### Central vs peripheral nystagmus

| Feature | Peripheral (labyrinthine) | Central (brain) |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | Present | Absent |
| Source description | Problem in balance receptors: macula + crista; utricle/saccule/semicircular canals involved → vertigo | — |
| Fatigability | Fatigable with repeated stimulation | Not fatigable |
| Duration | Limited (1.5-2 minutes) | Long-lasting |
| Direction | Usually fixed | Direction-changing / random |
| Optic fixation | Disappears | Does not disappear |
| Torsion with horizontal/vertical | Present | Pure horizontal / pure vertical / pure torsional |

- **Latency** = time from stimulation to appearance of nystagmus. If stimulation occurs at 0 seconds and nystagmus appears at 10 seconds, latency is present.
- **Peripheral (labyrinthine):** latency is present because the labyrinth must be stimulated; impulses travel via the vestibular nerve → vestibular nucleus → MLF → third/fourth/sixth nerve nuclei → nystagmus appears.
- **Central:** no latency; nystagmus appears immediately after stimulation.

### Direction of nystagmus

- Normal eyes stay in the midline because the pull from both labyrinths is equal.
- “Direction of nystagmus” means the direction of the **fast component**.
- Nystagmus has a fast and a slow component; direction is named by the fast component.
- Example: fast to the right + slow to the left → right-beating nystagmus.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 29)
> ![[images/chunk01_p029_source_page.jpeg]]

## Direction based on lesion

| Type of lesion | Condition listed in source | Direction of nystagmus |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperactive lesion of labyrinth | BPPV | Toward the more active side, i.e. the involved ear |
| Hypoactive lesion of labyrinth | Vestibular neuritis, purulent labyrinthitis | Toward the more active side, i.e. the normal ear |

Examples in the source:

- Right posterior semicircular canal (PSC) BPPV: vertical nystagmus toward the right.
- Right vestibular neuritis: horizontal nystagmus to the left (the active side).

|---|---|---|

| Superior canal | Down-beating | — |
| Posterior canal | Up-beating | — |
| Horizontal SCC | Horizontal nystagmus | Horizontal SCC disease; BPPV |
| Complete involvement of labyrinth (hypoactive) | Horizontal nystagmus with torsion | Vestibular neuritis (complete destruction of vestibular nerve), purulent labyrinthitis, late stages of Ménière's disease |

## Evoking nystagmus

### Position change: Dix-Hallpike test

- Investigation of choice for BPPV. *(AIIMS 2020 in source.)*
- Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo occurs due to displacement of small calcium carbonate crystals (otoliths) into the semicircular-canal area.
- Vertigo in BPPV lasts a few seconds to a few minutes.
- **Principle:** otoliths are normally present in the ampullated end of the semicircular canals. Placing the patient's head in a low position displaces the otoliths and provokes vertigo and nystagmus.

| Inner-ear canal involved | Frequency in source | Primary diagnostic test | What happens in the test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Posterior canal | Most common (~85-90%) | Dix-Hallpike test | Vertigo + upward and twisting eye movements (torsion) |
| Horizontal (lateral) canal | Less common | Supine roll test | Vertigo when head is turned left/right while lying flat |
| Anterior (superior) canal | Rare | Deep head-hanging test | Vertigo when head is deeply extended |

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 30)
> ![[images/chunk01_p030_source_page.jpeg]]

### Thermal changes: caloric test

**Principle**

Warm or cold water is irrigated into the external auditory canal, stimulating the lateral semicircular canal and the vestibular pathway.

**Position**

- If supine → head up 30 degrees.
- If sitting → head down 60 degrees.

**Procedure and inference**

- Test for the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) pathway. *(FMGE 2023 in source.)*
- Unilateral testing is done using the caloric test; each labyrinth can be tested.
- Bithermal Fitzgerald/Hallpike test: 30°C to 44°C. As body temperature is 37°C, the source gives 7° below and 7° above body temperature.
- Semicircular canal stimulated: lateral canal.
- **Normal response: COWS**
  - Cold water → nystagmus to the opposite side.
  - Warm water → nystagmus to the same side.
  - Example: right warm → right nystagmus; right cold → left nystagmus.
- If there is no response/no nystagmus, the source lists a hypoactive labyrinth, vestibular nerve/pathway problem, or a non-functioning inner ear.

**Exceptions**

- **Tympanic membrane perforation:** do not irrigate water; use air instead (Dundas Grant tube test).
- **No pinna and no canal:** Barany's rotational-chair test:
  - Patient sits on a rotating chair with VR glasses.
  - Rotation stimulates the crista (angular/rotational motion).
  - VR glasses record nystagmus.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 31)
> ![[images/chunk01_p031_source_page.jpeg]]

### Pressure change: fistula test

- Pressure stimulation of the EAC leads to labyrinthine stimulation.
- Done by pressure on the tragus or by Siegelization.
- **Siegle's pneumatic speculum:** inflate/deflate the bulb to increase/decrease canal pressure.
- Vertigo may increase with:
  - Cough.
  - Sneeze.
  - Lifting a heavy weight (because middle-ear pressure increases).
- Normally, there is no direct communication between middle and inner ear, so pressure is not transmitted to the inner ear; the fistula test should be negative in a normal person.
- In lateral semicircular canal dehiscence, oval-window rupture or round-window rupture, direct communication permits pressure transmission, stimulating the vestibular nerve and nucleus and resulting in nystagmus.

**Inference**

- Positive fistula test: communication is present.
- Negative fistula test: normal individuals.
- False-positive fistula test: no communication, but test is positive.
- False-negative fistula test: communication is present, but test is negative.

| Fistula-test result | Conditions listed in source |
|---|---|
| Positive (vertigo and nystagmus during the test) | Fistula on medial wall, e.g. perilymphatic fistula, LSC erosion, fenestration operation |
| False positive / Hennebert sign (test positive without fistula on medial wall) | Ménière's disease; hypermobile footplate (congenital syphilis); SCC dehiscence |
| False negative (test negative despite fistula on medial wall) | Dead labyrinth; cholesteatoma covering the fistula |

### Head impulse test

- Test for VOR pathways. *(NEET PG 2024 in source.)*

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 32)
> ![[images/chunk01_p032_source_page.jpeg]]

**Procedure**

- Ask the patient to fixate the eyes on a target (examiner's nose/finger/pen).
- Examiner suddenly rotates the head to the right or left.

**Interpretation**

- Normal: eye remains fixed on the object.
- Abnormal: the eye deflects with head movement and then comes back to catch the object; this corrective movement is a **saccade**.
- Saccade present → labyrinthine/inner-ear abnormality.
- Abnormal on right thrust → right vestibular hypofunction.

## VEMP (vestibular evoked myogenic potential)

- Used for the vestibulospinal reflex, not the VOR.
- VEMP = vestibular system stimulation with muscle contraction recorded by electrodes.

### cVEMP (cervical VEMP)

- Electrode on the sternocleidomastoid (SCM).
- Tests the inferior vestibular nerve.
- Pathway described: saccule → inferior vestibular nerve → vestibular nucleus → spinal cord → spinal accessory nerve → SCM contraction.

### oVEMP (ocular VEMP)

- Records ocular-muscle contraction (the inferior oblique is mentioned in the source).
- Stimulates the utricle.
- Impulses pass via the superior vestibular nerve.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 33)
> ![[images/chunk01_p033_source_page.jpeg]]

### VEMP test results

| Response | Conditions listed in source |
|---|---|
| Hyperactive response; vertigo on loud sounds (Tullio's phenomenon) | Ménière's disease; fistula; canal dehiscence; hypermobile footplate (congenital syphilis) |
| Hypoactive response | Labyrinthitis; vestibular neuritis; acoustic neuroma |

### Common student errors

- Caloric test positive = normal (as stated in the source).
- Fistula test positive = abnormal; Dix-Hallpike test positive = BPPV.
- cVEMP vs oVEMP:
  - cVEMP → cervical muscle (SCM) → inferior vestibular nerve.
  - oVEMP → ocular muscle → superior vestibular nerve.
- Peripheral vs central nystagmus:
  - Peripheral: latency present; fatigable; limited duration.
  - Central: no latency; not fatigable; unlimited duration / direction-changing.

# 5. Acoustic Neuroma

- Benign tumour.
- Arises from the internal auditory canal (IAC).
- Extends toward the cerebellopontine angle (CPA).
- Most common tumour of the CPA → acoustic neuroma (as stated in the source).
- Accounts for approximately 80% of CPA tumours.
- Cell of origin: Schwann cells.

## Anatomy: internal auditory canal (IAC)

### Structures passing through the IAC

- Facial nerve (CN VII).
- Vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII).

### Branches of the vestibulocochlear nerve

- Cochlear nerve.
- Vestibular nerve.

### Branches of the vestibular nerve

- Superior vestibular nerve.
- Inferior vestibular nerve.

## Quadrant arrangement in the IAC

Two bony crests divide the internal auditory canal:

- **Bill's bar:** vertical crest.
- **Falciform crest:** horizontal crest.

### Nerve arrangement in quadrants

| Quadrant | Structure |
|---|---|
| Anterosuperior | Facial nerve (CN VII) |
| Anteroinferior | Cochlear nerve |
| Posterosuperior | Superior vestibular nerve |
| Posteroinferior | Inferior vestibular nerve; most common site of origin of acoustic neuroma |

## Pathology

- Benign tumour, locally invasive.
- Originates from Schwann cells of the vestibular nerve, but may sometimes arise from the cochlear nerve.
- As it expands, it causes widening of the canal and appears in the CPA.

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 36)
> ![[images/chunk01_p036_source_page.jpeg]]

## Clinical features

Symptoms occur due to compression of cranial nerves. *(NEET PG 2023 in source.)*

### Early stage: internal auditory canal

| Nerve involved | Features listed in source |
|---|---|
| Vestibular nerve | Vertigo; imbalance; often compensated by the opposite vestibular system |
| Cochlear nerve | Retrocochlear sensorineural hearing loss |
| Facial nerve | Early involvement affects sensory fibres: loss of sensation over the concha; **Hitzelberger's sign** = loss of conchal sensation, indicating facial-nerve sensory involvement. Late involvement: LMN facial palsy. |

### Late stage: expanding tumour

As the tumour enlarges, additional cranial nerves may be compressed.

| Direction of tumour expansion | Cranial nerves / structures involved | Symptoms listed in source |
|---|---|---|
| Superior | CN V (trigeminal); CN VI (abducens) | Loss of corneal reflex; lateral rectus palsy |
| Inferior | CN IX; CN X; CN XI | Dysphagia; speech difficulty |
| Medial | Brainstem; cerebellum | Ataxia; raised intracranial pressure |

### Key clinical indications

- Most common age: 40-60 years.
- Bilateral tumours are part of neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2).

> [!example]- Source page visual (PDF page 37)
> ![[images/chunk01_p037_source_page.jpeg]]

## Audiometric findings

- Unilateral SNHL.
- Poor speech discrimination.
- Cranial-nerve involvement.
- Brainstem/cerebellar involvement.

### Tuning-fork tests

- **Rinne test:** positive.
  - False positive in severe-to-profound unilateral SNHL (>70-90 dB), as stated in the source.
- **Weber test:** lateralizes to the normal ear.
- **Absolute bone conduction:** shortened.
- **Acoustic-reflex delay:** positive.
- **Speech discrimination:** poor.
- **Tone-decay test:** positive; patient hears the tone for less than 60 seconds.

### Investigations

- **Audiological investigation of choice:** BERA (Brainstem Evoked Response Audiometry); delay in wave V.
- **General investigation of choice:** MRI with gadolinium contrast; “ice-cream cone sign”.
- **Biopsy findings:** Antoni A cells, Antoni B cells, Verocay bodies.

## Treatment

Management depends on tumour size.

## Phase C — P2 verification notes (editorial layer)

The transcription and source-page embeds above preserve the original study-note wording. The following addenda document verification; they do not silently replace the printed values.

> [!warning] ENT-A012 — Air–bone gap thresholds (PDF pp. 17–20)
> The source uses **>20 dB** in detailed audiogram explanations and **>15 dB** in the page 20 summary. Both readings are retained above. An air–bone gap is interpreted in the context of frequency-specific audiometry, test conditions, and the chosen clinical convention; the conflicting source thresholds should not be treated as one internally consistent rule. Do not choose one number solely from the summary table. See [StatPearls: Conductive Hearing Loss](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK578179/) for clinical context.

> [!warning] ENT-A013 — Tympanic-membrane vibratory area (PDF pp. 4, 14)
> Page 4 states **55 mm²**, while page 14 first calculates with **45 mm²** and then also calculates with **55 mm²**. With the commonly quoted stapes footplate area of **3.2 mm²**, 55 ÷ 3.2 ≈ **17.2:1**; 45 ÷ 3.2 ≈ **14.1:1**. The source's 14–17:1 range can be understood as using two different area assumptions, but the page 14 presentation does not clearly distinguish those assumptions. Original figures and calculations remain visible. Reference: [Endoscopic Ear Surgery: Critical Review of Middle-Ear Anatomy and Physiology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4899366/).

> [!note] ENT-A014 — BERA wave mnemonic/table (PDF p. 24)
> The source mnemonic **E-E-C-O-L-A-I** does not align with the accompanying table's letter assignments for wave II and waves VI–VII. In clinical ABR interpretation, waves **I–V** are the principal, most consistently identified components; later components may be less consistent, and later waves should not be treated as a rigid one-wave/one-nucleus map. The printed mnemonic and table are preserved as source material, but their mismatch should not be memorized as a verified mapping. Reference: [StatPearls: Auditory Brainstem Response](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK597358/).
