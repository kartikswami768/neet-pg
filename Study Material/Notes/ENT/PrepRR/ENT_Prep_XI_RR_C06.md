## Cartilages of the larynx

### Page 155 — Cartilages of the larynx

**Cartilages of larynx** *(lecture timestamp 00:02:16)*

- Larynx is made of cartilages.
- Largest: thyroid cartilage.
  - Has two alae, like a butterfly.
  - They meet at an angle of 90° in males — Adam's apple; 120° in females.
  - Muscles attached to the oblique line:
    - Sternothyroid
    - Thyrohyoid
    - Thyropharyngeus
- Below the thyroid cartilage: cricoid cartilage (signet-ring cartilage).
- The source contains the phrase: “Inner surface of thyroid epiglottic cartilage” (retained as printed; its intended relationship is unclear).
- Above the cricoid cartilage, on its superior facets: arytenoid cartilages.
- Above the arytenoid cartilage: cuneiform cartilage.

| Paired cartilages | Unpaired cartilages |
|---|---|
| Arytenoid | Thyroid |
| Corniculate | Cricoid |
| Cuneiform | Epiglottis |

- Some cartilages calcify with age; these are hyaline cartilages:
  - Thyroid
  - Cricoid
  - Arytenoid
- Elastic cartilages open and close the laryngeal inlet:
  - Epiglottis
  - Corniculate
  - Cuneiform
- The only complete cartilaginous ring in the airway is the cricoid cartilage.

**Cricoid cartilage** *(lecture timestamp 00:06:22)*

- Only signet-ring-shaped cartilage.
- The narrow arch is anteriorly; the lamina is wider posteriorly.
- Has two facets on each side:
  - Superior facets: attachment to the arytenoid cartilage.
  - Inferior facets: attachment to the inferior horn of the thyroid cartilage.
- Laryngeal crepitus can be elicited:
  - Movement of the cricoid cartilage against the vertebra produces a gritty sensation.
  - Normally present in everyone.

### Page 156 — Cricoid, epiglottic and arytenoid cartilages

- **Absent laryngeal crepitus (Boccas' sign)** is described in:
  - Post-cricoid cancer
  - Retropharyngeal abscess
  - Prevertebral abscess

**Epiglottic cartilage** *(lecture timestamp 00:08:56; FMGE 2021)*

- Leaf-like cartilage attached to the inner surface of the thyroid cartilage.
- Surfaces/parts:
  - Suprahyoid
  - Infrahyoid
- Surface facing the base of the tongue: lingual surface.
- Surface facing the larynx: laryngeal surface.
- Superiorly free; attached inferiorly to the thyroid cartilage.

**Arytenoid cartilage** *(INI-CET 2020)*

- Pyramid-shaped cartilage.
- Base rests on the superior facet of the cricoid cartilage.
- Apex articulates with the corniculate and cuneiform cartilages, as represented in the source diagram.
- Medially, a vocal process attaches to the vocal cords.
- Muscular process provides attachment to muscles.

**Corniculate and cuneiform cartilages**

- Corniculate cartilage = cartilage of Santorini.
- Cuneiform cartilage = cartilage of Wrisberg.

### Page 157 — Laryngeal subdivisions, development, membranes and cavity

**Subdivisions of the larynx**

- **Glottis:** at the level of the vocal cords and 1 cm below.
- **Supraglottis:** above the level of the vocal cords.
- **Subglottis:** 1 cm below the vocal cords to the lower border of the cricoid cartilage.

**Development** *(lecture timestamp 00:12:44)*

- Supraglottis develops from the 4th arch.
- Glottis and subglottis develop from the 6th arch.

**Membranes of the larynx**

*Extrinsic membranes* — connect the laryngeal structure to an external structure:

- Thyrohyoid membrane — site for superior laryngeal nerve block.
- Cricotracheal membrane.

*Intrinsic membranes* — connect one laryngeal structure to another:

- Cricothyroid membrane — emergency airway access.
- **Quadrangular membrane**
  - Has four sides.
  - Extends between the epiglottis, thyroid cartilage and arytenoid cartilages.
  - Lower fibres condense to form the false cords; the vestibular ligament originates here.
- **Cricovocal membrane**
  - Cone-shaped; also called the *conus elasticus*.
  - Gives rise to the vocal ligament.
  - Upper fibres condense to form the true cords.

**Laryngeal cavity** *(lecture timestamp 00:18:37; FMGE 2019)*

- Superiorly begins at the inlet of the larynx.
- The quadrangular membrane forms its superior/false-fold boundary; the cricovocal membrane is below.
- Space between the two quadrangular membranes: vestibule of the larynx.
- Space between the two false cords: **rima vestibuli**.
- Space between the two true cords: **rima glottidis**.

The source diagram labels the hyoid bone, thyrohyoid membrane, epiglottis, quadrangular membrane, vestibular ligament, vocal ligament, cricovocal membrane, cricoid cartilage, vestibular folds, vocal folds and trachea.

### Page 158 — Cavity, epithelium, lymphatics and anatomical summary

- Space between the false and true cords: **ventricle of the larynx**.
- Below the rima glottidis: subglottis.

**Epithelium of the larynx** *(lecture timestamp 00:21:45)*

- Lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, except at three sites:
  - Vocal cords
  - Aryepiglottic fold
  - Superior surface of the epiglottis

**Lymphatics** *(lecture timestamp 00:22:35)*

- Supraglottis: drains to upper deep cervical lymph nodes.
- Subglottis: drains to pre-laryngeal and pre-tracheal lymph nodes, then to lower deep cervical or mediastinal nodes.
- Glottis: no lymphatics.

**Summary by laryngeal subsite**

| Feature | Supraglottis | Glottis | Subglottis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structures | Epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, arytenoids; together form the laryngeal inlet, also called the epilarynx; false vocal cords and ventricle | True vocal cords; anterior and posterior commissures | Narrowest part of the larynx in children |
| Epithelium / notes | Drains to upper and middle deep cervical lymph nodes | True vocal cords lined by stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium; no lymphatics, acting as a watershed for lymphatics | Lined by pseudostratified columnar epithelium; drains into lower deep cervical and anterior lymph nodes |

**Anatomy of the larynx**

- Cavity, in sequence from above downwards: inlet of larynx (also called epilarynx), vestibule, rima vestibuli, ventricle (sinus of Morgagni of the larynx), saccule (site of origin of a laryngocele), rima glottidis and subglottis.
- Spaces:
  - **Pre-epiglottic space / space of Boyer:** upper boundary is the hyoepiglottic ligament; lies between the thyroid/thyrohyoid membrane and epiglottis.
  - **Paraglottic space / space of Tucker:** lateral to the quadrangular membrane and conus elasticus.
  - **Reinke's space:** submucosal space of the true vocal cord; oedema, nodules and polyps arise here.

## Laryngeal muscles, spaces and one-liners

### Page 160 — Muscles and spaces of the larynx

**Muscles of the larynx** *(lecture timestamp 00:30:37; FMGE 2019)*

- Abductor of the vocal cord / safety muscle of the larynx: **posterior cricoarytenoid**.
- Tensor of the vocal cord / secondary adductor: **cricothyroid**.
  - Has two bellies.
  - Supplied by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN).
- All muscles of the larynx adduct the vocal cord except the posterior cricoarytenoid.
- Adductor + tensor: cricothyroid and vocalis (as stated in the source).
- Increases tension, pitch and length: cricothyroid (the only intrinsic muscle lying outside the larynx).
- Decreases tension, pitch and length: vocalis.
- Only unpaired muscle: interarytenoid / transverse arytenoid.
  - Receives dual innervation.

**Spaces of the larynx** *(lecture timestamp 00:40:13)*

- Pre-epiglottic space of Boyer.
- Paraglottic space of Tucker.
- Reinke's space.

The source page includes labelled muscle illustrations for the cricothyroid, thyroarytenoid, lateral and posterior cricoarytenoid, and interarytenoid/oblique arytenoid muscles.

### Page 161 — Laryngeal spaces, functions and infant/adult differences

**Pre-epiglottic space of Boyer**

- Space in front of the epiglottis.
- Boundaries:
  - Anteriorly: thyrohyoid membrane.
  - Superiorly: hyoepiglottic ligament.
  - Posteriorly: epiglottis.

**Paraglottic space of Tucker**

- Lies on the sides of the larynx.
- Boundaries:
  - Laterally: thyroid cartilage.
  - Medially: quadrangular membrane.
  - Below: cricovocal membrane and ventricles.

**Reinke's space**

- The source lists four layers of the vocal cord:
  - Epithelium
  - Superficial lamina propria
  - Vocal ligament
  - Vocalis muscle
- Space between the epithelium and superficial lamina propria: Reinke's space.

**Functions of the larynx** *(lecture timestamp 00:44:44)*

- Protection of airways.
- Phonation.
- Respiration.
- Fixation of the chest.

| Position / feature | Infants | Adults |
|---|---|---|
| Vertebral position | C2–C3 | C3–C6 |
| Epiglottis | Curled | Leaf-shaped |
| Thyroid cartilage | Flat | Angulated |
| Narrowest part | Subglottis | Glottis |
| Submucosal tissue | More | Less |
| Cartilages | Soft | Ossified |

### Page 162 — One-liners and common errors

**One-liners**

- Larynx develops from the 4th and 6th arches.
- Supraglottis develops from the 4th arch; glottis and subglottis develop from the 6th arch.
- Larynx lies against C3–C6.
- **==Laryngeal crepitus occurs due to movement of the cricoid against the vertebrae.**==
- ==**Laryngeal crepitus is absent in post-cricoid cancer, prevertebral abscess and retropharyngeal abscess.==**
- Hyaline cartilages of the larynx: thyroid, cricoid and arytenoid.
- Elastic cartilages: epiglottis, cuneiform and corniculate.
- Largest laryngeal cartilage: thyroid.
- Signet-ring cartilage: cricoid.
- Angle at which the thyroid cartilage alae meet:
  - Male: 90°.
  - Female: 120°.
- **Oblique line of thyroid cartilage** gives attachment to sternothyroid, thyrohyoid and thyropharyngeus.
- Two surfaces of the epiglottis: lingual and laryngeal.
  - Parts: suprahyoid and infrahyoid.
- Only complete cartilaginous ring in the airway: cricoid cartilage.
- Narrowest portion of the adult larynx: glottis; in the paediatric larynx: subglottis.
- Cartilage of Santorini: corniculate.
- Cartilage of Wrisberg: cuneiform.
- ==Membranous portion: 2/3; cartilaginous portion: 1/3.==
- **Sinus of Morgagni of the larynx**: ventricles.
- Rima vestibuli: space between the false cords.
- Rima glottidis: space between the true cords.
- **==Space of Tucker==**: paraglottic space.
- **==Space of Boyer:==** pre-epiglottic space.
- Only unpaired laryngeal muscle: interarytenoid / transverse arytenoid.
- Only muscle said here to receive **==dual innervation:==** **interarytenoid / transverse arytenoid.**
- Only abductor of the vocal cord: posterior cricoarytenoid.
- Tensor: cricothyroid.

**Common errors** *(lecture timestamp 00:49:55)*

- Nerve supply:
  - Sensory above vocal cords: internal branch of SLN.
  - Sensory below vocal cords: recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN).
  - Motor supply: all muscles by RLN except cricothyroid, supplied by the external branch of SLN.
- Muscles:
  - Cricothyroid: only muscle with two bellies, lying outside; adducts and lengthens the vocal cord (as stated in the source).
  - Vocalis: shortens and adducts the vocal cord. (relieving the tension of mucosa and effectively the vocal cord)
  - Posterior cricoarytenoid: only abductor of the vocal cord.

## Congenital anomalies of the larynx

### Page 163 — Laryngomalacia

**Congenital anomalies of the larynx**

- Most common congenital anomaly of the larynx:
  1. 1st most common: Laryngomalacia.
  2. 2nd most common: Congenital vocal cord paralysis.
  3. 3rd. most common: Subglottic stenosis.

**Laryngomalacia** *(lecture timestamp 00:51:44; FMGE 2020)*

- Definition: condition with <u>excessive flaccidity of the epiglottis or supraglottis.</u>
- Supraglottis: part of the larynx above the vocal cords.

**Mechanism of obstruction**
- During inspiration:
	- Air is sucked in.
	- Epiglottis falls onto the airway and obstructs it.
	- Inspiratory stridor results.
- During expiration:
	- Air comes from below (from the trachea) and pushes the epiglottis away.
	- No stridor during expiration.

**Clinical findings and presentation**

- Inspiratory stridor at birth.
- No expiratory stridor.
- Crying increases stridor:
	- ==Crying causes negative intrathoracic pressure and pulls air in; the epiglottis is sucked in, increasing stridor.==
	- ==Cry/voice at birth is normal because the epiglottis, not the vocal cords, is involved.==
- Position:
	- Supine: epiglottis collapses over the laryngeal inlet, obstructing the glottis and worsening stridor.
	- Prone: epiglottis falls away from the larynx, relieving stridor.

**Laryngoscopic examination**

- Done under short general anaesthesia or sedation.
- Finding: collapsed, **omega-shaped epiglottis.**

> ![[images/chunk06_p163_source_page.jpeg]]


**Laryngomalacia treatment**

- Conservative: wait and watch.
- Spontaneous resolution occurs at 1–2 years of age.
- Epiglottic surgery may be needed if it does not resolve.

### **Congenital vocal cord palsy**

- Most common congenital CNS abnormality causing vocal cord palsy: **Arnold–Chiari malformation.**

*Clinical presentation*

- **Unilateral vocal cord paralysis:** usually asymptomatic or may cause a weak cry.
- **Bilateral vocal cord paralysis:**
	- Stridor at birth; **biphasic stridor** because the lesion is at the level of the vocal cords.
	- Difficulty breathing, cyanosis and respiratory distress at birth.
	- Weak cry or aphonia/no cry.
	- **Crying increases stridor.**

*Treatment*

- Unilateral: wait and watch; one cord is paralysed while the other still works.
- Bilateral:
	- Tracheostomy to access the airway.
	- Vocal cord lateralisation to create airway space for respiration.

### **Subglottic stenosis**

- Definition: excessive thickening of the cricoid cartilage, narrowing the subglottic lumen.
- The subglottis lies at the level of the cricoid cartilage and is the narrowest part of the larynx in children.
- Abnormal subglottic **diameter**:
	- Less than ==4 mm== in full-term neonates.
	- Less than ==3 mm== in premature neonates (NEET PG 2021).

**Cotton–Myer classification**

- Used for acquired subglottic stenosis; also applicable to congenital stenosis.
	- Grade 1: obstruction up to 50%.
	- Grade 2: 51–70% obstruction.
	- Grade 3: 71–99% obstruction.
	- Grade 4: complete obstruction (100%).

**Clinical presentation**

- Does not present at birth (as stated in the source).
- The airway is narrowed but not completely blocked, leading to minimal or very minor symptoms, which may include noisy breathing.
- Crying is essentially normal.
- Crying does not increase stridor.

| Acute epiglottitis              | increases / worsens           |
| Congenital vocal cord paralysis | increases / worsens           |
| Subglottic stenosis             | No effect                     |
| Bilateral choanal atresia       | Improves                      |

**Treatment based on grade**

| Grade | Management | Key concept / procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Grade 1 | Observation | Usually resolves with growth; cartilages enlarge/expand with age |
| Grade 2 | Endoscopic dilatation | Dilatation of narrowed airway using endoscopic techniques |
| Grade 3 | Laryngotracheal reconstruction | Rib cartilage graft used to expand the subglottic region |
| Grade 4 | Partial cricotracheal resection | Remove cricoid cartilage and first tracheal ring; connect larynx with trachea |

*Post-surgery*

- Montgomery T-tube (stent): used in Grade 3 and Grade 4 reconstructions.
	- Prevents further stenosis after surgery.
- Mitomycin-C (antiproliferative drug): prevents fibrosis and adhesions.

### **Laryngeal web**

- Embryological cause: incomplete canalisation of the larynx at the level of the vocal cords.
- Most common site: glottis.
- **Partial web:** occupies only the anterior 2/3 of the vocal cord; weak cry, noisy/difficult breathing.
- **Complete web:** occupies the vocal cord completely; **presents with no cry; crying increases stridor.**

> [!quote]- Original source page (PDF p. 166)
> ![[images/chunk06_p166_source_page.jpeg]]

#### Laryngeal web treatment
- **Partial web:** not an emergency; eventually release the web.
- **Complete web:** emergency; tracheostomy to access the airway, followed by release of the web.

**Common error files** *(lecture timestamp 01:05:08)*

- Conditions presenting at birth:
	- Laryngomalacia.
	- Congenital vocal cord paralysis.
	- Complete laryngeal web.
- Stridor types:
	- Inspiratory: laryngomalacia.
	- Biphasic: congenital vocal cord paralysis and laryngeal web.
- Cry status:
	- Normal: laryngomalacia, subglottic stenosis.
	- Weak or absent: congenital vocal cord paralysis, laryngeal web.
- Effect of crying on stridor:
	- Increases: laryngomalacia, vocal cord paralysis, laryngeal web, epiglottitis.
	- No effect: subglottic stenosis.
	- Improves: croup/laryngotracheitis.
- Effect of position (laryngomalacia): supine worsens; prone improves.
- Cotton–Myer's classification is used for subglottic stenosis.

### **Inflammatory conditions of the larynx: acute epiglottitis** *(lecture timestamp 01:09:54)*

- Most common age group: 3–7 years.
- Causative organisms:
	- *Streptococcus pneumoniae* — most common (as stated).
	- *Haemophilus influenzae* — incidence reduced due to vaccination.
- Clinical presentation:
	- Dysphagia.
	- Odynophagia with drooling of saliva and dehydration.
	- Absence of cough / hoarseness of voice.
	- Dyspnoea and **inspiratory stridor due to supraglottic obstruction.**
	- Increased chance of laryngospasm on examination.
	- **Child sits in the tripod position.**

#### **Acute epiglottitis: investigations and treatment**

- Investigation: lateral X-ray view — **thumb sign**, indicating inflammation of the epiglottis.
- Treatment:
	- Secure the airway.
	- Antibiotics against the specific organisms.
	- Steroids may be given to reduce oedema and prevent airway obstruction.
	- Voice rest and cough suppressants are not required (as stated).
	- **Racemic adrenaline nebulisation or injection** — bronchodilator (as stated).
	- Hydration is important.
	- Intubation or tracheostomy may be required.

### **Croup** *(also called laryngotracheobronchitis)*
- Most common age group: 3 months–3 years.
- Causative organisms:
	- Parainfluenza virus types I and II (most common); the notes add “secondary infection by bacteria”.
	- Respiratory syncytial virus.
	- Rhinovirus.
	- Influenza virus.
- Clinical presentation:
	- History of cold / URTI.
	- Barking, seal-like cough.
	- Hoarseness of voice / change in cry.
	- **Sore throat absent.**
	- **Dyspnoea and biphasic stridor** due to ==subglottic obstruction==.
	- Increased chance of laryngospasm on examination.
- Investigations: AP X-ray — **steeple sign**, narrowing of the subglottis.
- Treatment (as listed in the source):
	- Secure the airway.
	- **Antibiotics** for secondary infections.
	- Steroids may be given to reduce oedema.
	- **Racemic adrenaline nebulisation** with **==helium and oxygen==** — bronchodilator.
	- Voice rest and cough suppressants are required.
	- Hydration is important.
	- **Intubation** or **tracheostomy** may be required 
		→ BUT not effective because of mucosal edema in bronchus and trachea
		- Intubation can be tried if necessary
		- Tracheostomy is not preferred as bleeding during tracheostomy (as this tissue is already inflamed) can cause aspiration

> [!danger] Phase B clinical-safety verification — epiglottitis and croup (ENT-A005)
> These source treatment bullets must not be used as a current standalone protocol. For suspected **epiglottitis**, current paediatric guidance prioritises minimising distress, early senior/ENT/anaesthetic/ICU support, deferring unnecessary examination or imaging until an airway-capable team is available, and airway management plus antibiotics; dexamethasone may be considered. “Racemic adrenaline nebulisation or injection” is **not listed as core epiglottitis treatment** in the references below, and “injection” is unclear in this context.
> 
> For **typical viral croup**, guideline treatment is severity-dependent: corticosteroids are used according to severity, and nebulised adrenaline is used for more severe disease with observation. Antibiotics have no role in uncomplicated viral croup, and antitussives such as codeine have no proven benefit and may cause respiratory depression. Heliox has not been shown superior to nebulised adrenaline. Thus the source's blanket “voice rest and cough suppressants are required” and adrenaline/heliox wording needs correction in a study-safe erratum; the original source wording remains above for fidelity. ### **Benign lesions of the larynx** *(lecture timestamp 01:18:33)*

#### **Vocal nodule**
- Also called teacher's nodule / singer's nodule.
- Bilateral, symmetrical lesions on the vocal cords.
- Location: junction of the anterior 1/3 and middle 1/3.
- Occurs due to gradual or chronic voice abuse.
- Treatment: voice rest; microlaryngeal surgery (MLS), as listed.

#### **Vocal polyp**
- Unilateral lesion on the vocal cords.
- Location: junction of the anterior 1/3 and middle 1/3.
- Occurs due to sudden voice abuse.
- Presents with diplophonia.
- Treatment: microlaryngeal surgery.

#### **Reinke's oedema**
- Bilateral, symmetrical swelling of the vocal cords.
- Location: membranous or anterior 2/3 of the vocal cords.
- Cause: smoking and GERD.
- Presents with diplophonia and low-pitched voice.
- Appears as a bag of water.

**Treatment:**
- Voice rest.
- Antireflux therapy.
- Decortication / stripping of the vocal cord epithelium.
	- Initially, one vocal cord is treated; the other is treated after 4–6 weeks.
	- Stripping of both vocal cords is not done simultaneously because it can lead to adhesions.

#### **Contact ulcer** *(also called pachyderma laryngitis)*
- Cause: trauma, reflux and voice abuse.
- Hypertrophy of epithelium on one side with depression of the opposite epithelium.
- “Pseudoulcer”.
- Affects the posterior part of the vocal cord.

#### **Keratosis of the larynx** *(also called leukoplakia)*
- Smoking / tobacco chewing → chronic irritation → transformation of epithelium to white plaque-like lesions.
- Treatment:
	- Stop offending agents.
	- Decortication / stripping of the vocal cord or excisional biopsy of the vocal cord.

#### **TB larynx** *(lecture timestamp 01:24:37)
- Can occur along with pulmonary tuberculosis.
- Can also occur as a low-grade, indolent form: lupus larynx.
- Symptoms:

- Normally, in males during puberty, the voice transforms to produce an adult, low-pitched quality.

- Means weakness of the phonatory muscle.

- Keyhole appearance of the glottis:

## Laryngeal nerve palsies

### Page 179 — Unilateral and bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy

**Unilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) palsy** *(lecture timestamp 02:00:00)*

*Example: right RLN palsy*

- Sensory loss:
  - Right side of larynx below the vocal cords (RLN is sensory below the vocal cords).
  - Left side has no sensory loss because palsy is unilateral.
- Motor loss:
  - Right side: all muscles paralysed except cricothyroid, supplied by the external branch of SLN.
  - Left side: all muscles functional.
- Vocal cord position:
  - Normal position: intermediate (cadaveric) position.
  - In RLN palsy, posterior cricoarytenoid is paralysed, so abduction is lost; adduction and tension are retained; cord lies in median or paramedian position.
- Phonation: no major change because the paralysed cord is already median/paramedian, the opposite cord can reach the midline, both cricothyroids remain functional, and adduction occurs; mild voice change may arise from paralysis of other adductors.
- Respiration: paralysed cord cannot move away from midline, but normal cord can; no stridor, although dyspnoea on exertion may occur.
- Treatment: wait and watch.

**Bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy**

- Sensory loss: bilateral loss below the vocal cords.
- Motor loss: both posterior cricoarytenoids are paralysed; all muscles except cricothyroid are paralysed bilaterally.
- Vocal cord position: both cords lie in the midline or paramedian position because both abductors are paralysed.
- Phonation: no major change in voice; change may occur due to paralysis of other adductors (thyroarytenoid, interarytenoid). Continued on page 180.

### Page 180 — Bilateral RLN palsy and unilateral SLN palsy

**Bilateral RLN palsy (continued)**

- Respiration: both vocal cords cannot move away from the midline → stridor, because both posterior cricoarytenoids are paralysed.
- Stridor is a feature of bilateral RLN palsy.
- Treatment:
  - Immediate tracheostomy.
  - Afterwards create space for air by lateralising the cord / resecting tissue.
  - Type II thyroplasty procedures listed:
    - Arytenoidectomy: remove only the arytenoid to create space.
    - Cordectomy: remove only the posterior 2/3 of the vocal cord.
    - Kashima operation: remove arytenoid + posterior 2/3 of the cord.

**Unilateral superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) palsy**

- The SLN gives rise to the internal laryngeal nerve, which supplies sensation above the vocal cords.
- Example: right SLN palsy.
- Sensory loss: right side above the vocal cords; left side normal.
- Motor loss: only the right cricothyroid is paralysed; remaining muscles are functional, supplied by RLN.
- Vocal cord position:
  - Adductor/tensor (cricothyroid) paralysed; posterior cricoarytenoid has more power and pulls the cord into an abducted position.
  - **Askew position:** normal cord in intermediate position + paralysed cord in abducted position.
- Phonation: definite voice change; cannot increase pitch or tone because the paralysed cord cannot reach the midline or perform the tensor function.
- Respiration: no effect because the paralysed cord is already abducted and the normal cord can abduct.

### Page 181 — Bilateral SLN palsy and combined palsy

**Unilateral SLN palsy: treatment (continued)**

- Moderate risk of aspiration.
- Aim: reduce aspiration.
- Procedure: medialise the cord using Type I thyroplasty; place a fat graft in the paraglottic space to push the cord medially; fat injection in the vocal cord.

**Bilateral superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) palsy**

- Sensory loss: bilateral loss above the vocal cords.
- Motor loss: both cricothyroid muscles paralysed; other muscles functional.
- Vocal cord position: posterior cricoarytenoid has greater power → cords abducted.
- Phonation: cords cannot come to the midline or stretch; aphonia is a feature.
- Respiration: no problem because both cords are abducted.
- Highest risk of aspiration because sensation is lost above the cords and the cords are 7 mm from the midline on each side (total 14 mm gap, as stated).
- Treatment:
  - Initially, tracheostomy to protect the airway.
  - For intractable aspiration: tracheoesophageal diversion + permanent tracheostomy.
  - Divert the first tracheal ring with the upper oesophagus; aspiration through the larynx passes through the first tracheal ring into the oesophagus; the second tracheal ring is brought out as a permanent tracheostomy.

**Unilateral combined palsy (SLN + RLN on one side)**

- Example: right SLN + RLN palsy.
- Sensory loss: right hemilarynx anaesthetised; left hemilarynx completely functional.
- Motor loss: all muscles on the right paralysed; left muscles functional.
- Vocal cord position: affected cord intermediate with bowing; opposite cord normal/intermediate.
- Effects on phonation continue on page 182.

### Page 182 — Combined palsy and anatomical relations

- Phonation: difficulty speaking; mild voice change.

- Lateral neck dissection removes lymph nodes II, III and IV.
- Anterior-compartment neck dissection removes level VI (Delphian node).

- Level Va: posterior triangle.
- Level Vb: supraclavicular.
- Level VI: anterior-compartment lymph nodes.
- Level VII: superior mediastinal.

**Types of neck dissection**

- **Radical neck dissection:** nodes I–V removed; non-lymphatic structures removed include spinal accessory nerve, internal jugular vein, sternocleidomastoid muscle, tail of parotid, submandibular salivary gland and omohyoid.
- **Modified radical neck dissection:** nodes I–V removed; structures preserved as specified in the source:
  - Spinal accessory nerve — MRND I.
  - Spinal accessory nerve + IJV — MRND II.
  - Spinal accessory nerve + IJV + SCM — MRND III, also called functional/comprehensive neck dissection.

### Page 189 — Laryngeal cancer errors and foreign bodies

**Error files — laryngeal cancer**

- Best prognosis: glottic malignancies (early presentation + no lymphatic spread).
- “T3 > 3 rules”:
  - Vocal-cord fixation.
  - Spaces involved.
  - Inner perichondrium involved.
- First investigation: contrast-enhanced CT scan.
- Role of MRI: only when cartilage or space involvement is suspected.
- Total laryngectomy indications:
  - Bilateral arytenoid involvement in a primary patient with a large malignancy.
  - Patient cannot tolerate chemoradiotherapy.
  - Stage T3 or T4a with recurrence.

**Foreign body in airway and oesophagus**

**Foreign body in air passage: presentation**

- Common in younger age group (1–4 years).
- Most common foreign body: nuts and peanuts.
- Earliest symptoms: sudden-onset paroxysmal coughing, choking and gagging.
- Later: hoarseness, haemoptysis and stridor.

**Management of foreign body in the larynx — source flowchart**

- If there is no respiratory distress and the person can speak: back blows and encourage coughing.
- If conscious with the universal choking sign (inability to speak, breathe or cough): Heimlich manoeuvre / abdominal thrusts.
- Listed complications: gastric and oesophageal perforation, rib fracture and injury to abdominal organs.
- Contraindications/alternatives as printed: age under 1 year (the source image reads “Age <1 yr”); if unconscious, give sternal compressions; in pregnancy, give chest thrusts (over the sternum).

> [!danger] Phase B clinical-safety verification — foreign-body airway obstruction and CICO (ENT-A006)
> The original flowchart is preserved, but it should **not** be used as a current standalone first-aid algorithm. The transcription error “age under 4 years” was corrected to “age under 1 year” because the source image says “Age <1 yr.” More importantly, current 2025 AHA/AAP paediatric guidance differentiates: (1) mild obstruction/effective cough — encourage coughing and observe; (2) severe obstruction in an infant — cycles of 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts; (3) severe obstruction in a child — cycles of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts; and (4) unresponsive infant/child — start CPR with chest compressions and remove only a **visible** object when opening the mouth (no blind finger sweeps). Pregnancy and adult protocols are age/context-specific. The source's cricothyrotomy/minitracheostomy material is clinician-level emergency airway management, not lay first aid; invasive procedures must be performed by appropriately trained clinicians with urgent senior airway support. ## Phase C — P2 verification notes (editorial layer)

> [!note] ENT-A023 — Thyroid-cartilage interlaminar angle (PDF pp. 155, 162)
> The classic anatomy teaching value is approximately **90° in males and 120° in females**, consistent with the source. These are conventional population-level measurements, not fixed values for every individual. Reference: [StatPearls: Adam’s Apple / Thyroid Cartilage](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK535354/).

> [!note] ENT-A024 — Botulinum-toxin target in abductor spasmodic dysphonia (PDF p. 174)
> The specific target is the **posterior cricoarytenoid (PCA)**, the only intrinsic laryngeal muscle that abducts the vocal folds. “Cricoarytenoid muscle” alone is imprecise because the cricoarytenoid group includes muscles with different functions. Reference: [StatPearls: Laryngeal Botulinum Toxin Injection](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK567733/).

> [!note] ENT-A025 — Recurrent laryngeal nerve laterality (PDF pp. 177–178)
> There is no universal side-specific rule across all thyroidectomy populations. In one study of 5,334 nerves at risk, palsy was more common on the **right in bilateral procedures** but more common on the **left in unilateral procedures**. The statements in the source may therefore refer to different denominators/context; retain their original context and avoid merging them into a universal ranking. Reference: [Differential recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy rates after thyroidectomy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25444315/).

> [!note] ENT-A026 — Surgical triangles/eponyms (PDF p. 183)
> **Lore’s triangle** is a recognized inferior approach area for identifying the recurrent laryngeal nerve. A commonly described boundary set uses the trachea/esophagus medially, laterally retracted strap muscles (some descriptions also refer to the carotid artery), the inferior pole of the thyroid as the superior/base boundary, and the thoracic inlet as the apex. The source labels “Lowry’s” and “Simon’s” could not be mapped to equivalent definitions confidently; these labels remain preserved and unresolved rather than being silently normalized. Reference: [Laryngeal Nerves and Voice Change in Thyroid Surgery](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8986921/).

> [!warning] ENT-A027 — Laryngeal-cancer TNM staging (PDF pp. 186–187)
> The NCI’s laryngeal-cancer PDQ reproduces **AJCC 8th edition** stage groups. For stage IVB, it specifies **T4b, any N, M0** and **any T, N3, M0**. The source summary on page 187 omits the T4b-any-N group if it restricts this to T4b N0, and the source’s condensed T category material should not be treated as a complete site-specific staging reference: supraglottic, glottic and subglottic criteria differ. The source table is preserved; use the cited staging reference for precise staging. Reference: [NCI: Laryngeal Cancer Treatment (PDQ)](https://www.cancer.gov/types/head-and-neck/hp/adult/laryngeal-treatment-pdq).
