## Facial nerve anatomy and lesions

### Introduction

- **Is it a sensory, motor, or mixed nerve?**
	- Mixed nerve (sensory, motor and secretomotor root).
- **What is the sensory root called?**
	- ==Nerve of Wrisberg (*nervus intermedius*).==
- **Where is the facial nerve nucleus present?**
	- Pons.
- **What is the meaning of supranuclear and infranuclear palsy?**
	- **Supranuclear:** lesion in the cerebral cortex above the pons.
	- **Infranuclear:** lesion below the level of the nucleus in the pons.
- **Why is the forehead spared?**
	- Due to bilateral innervation from the cerebral cortex.
	- The source then states: **UMN palsy → forehead involved** and **LMN palsy → forehead spared**. These statements are retained as printed and flagged in the QA report because they conflict with the preceding explanation and appear internally inconsistent.

**Facial nerve in the brainstem — facial colliculus**

- The facial nerve winds around the nucleus of the abducens, forming the facial colliculus, and exits at the cerebellopontine junction.
- A brainstem lesion is associated in the notes with:
  - 7th and 6th nerve palsy.
  - Contralateral hemiplegia.
  - This condition is also called **Millard–Gubler syndrome**.

### Page 193 — Intracranial course; intratemporal, meatal and labyrinthine segments

**Intracranial course of the facial nerve**

- Pons → internal auditory meatus.
- The source illustration labels the longitudinal fracture plane, greater superficial petrosal nerve, carotid artery, cochlea, internal auditory canal, jugular vein, external auditory canal (EAC), geniculate ganglion, facial nerve, labyrinthine segment, perigeniculate region, tympanic segment, vertical segment and related temporal-bone structures.
- The facial-nerve-lesion diagram lists 15 cases and labels the labyrinthine segment (3), geniculate ganglion (4), tympanic segment (7) and vertical segment (1). The visual layout of the percentages is ambiguous in the source; see the embedded image.

**Intratemporal segment of the facial nerve**

- The intratemporal segment is the longest.
- It is the only cranial nerve to run most of its course through a bony canal (Fallopian canal).
  - The bony canal is described as dehiscent in 50%.
  - The most common site of dehiscence is the horizontal part.
- It is divided into:
  - Meatal segment.
  - Labyrinthine segment.
  - Tympanic segment.
  - Mastoid segment.

**Meatal segment**

- Length: 8–10 mm.
- Seen in the IAM, with the facial nerve travelling with the cochlear nerve.
- A vertical crest on the bone in the superior part is **Bill’s bar**.
- The horizontal crest is the **falciform crest**.
- Anterosuperior quadrant: facial nerve.
- Inferior quadrant: cochlear nerve.

**Labyrinthine segment**

- Inner-ear segment.
- Length 3–4 mm and diameter 0.7 mm; described as the shortest and narrowest segment and therefore prone to ischaemia and oedema in Bell’s palsy.
- Travels over the vestibule in the inner ear:
  - Anteriorly: cochlea.
  - Posteriorly: semicircular canals.

### Page 194 — Tympanic and mastoid segments

**Tympanic segment**

- Horizontal segment: 11 mm; vertical segment: 13 mm, as stated in the source.
- From the inner ear, the nerve enters the medial wall of the middle ear, making a turn at the **first genu / geniculate ganglion**.
  - Landmark for the first genu: **cochleariform process**.
- The horizontal segment runs above the oval window and below the lateral semicircular canal (SCC).
- It goes to the mastoid to make the second turn, the **second genu**.
  - Landmark: **pyramidal process**.

**Mastoid segment**

- The nerve runs vertically; this is also called the mastoid segment.
- Landmark: **tympanomastoid suture**.

The source diagrams label the facial canal, geniculate ganglion, stapes/oval window, lateral semicircular canal, nerve to stapedius, internal acoustic meatus, greater and lesser petrosal nerves, tympanic plexus, chorda tympani, carotid artery and adjacent middle-ear structures.

### Page 195 — Extracranial course and subsegments/features table

**Extracranial segment of the facial nerve** *(lecture timestamp 00:10:07)*

- Exits through the stylomastoid foramen.
- Passes between the superficial and deep lobes of the parotid gland and gives five terminal branches:
  - Temporal.
  - Zygomatic.
  - Buccal.
  - Marginal mandibular.
  - Cervical.

### Page 196 — Branches, topodiagnosis and surgical landmarks

**Branches of the facial nerve** *(lecture timestamp 00:12:46)*

- Greater superficial petrosal nerve (GSPN): lacrimation; assessed by Schirmer’s test.
- Nerve to stapedius: stapedial reflex.
- Chorda tympani: taste from anterior 2/3 of tongue.
- Sensory auricular branch to pinna and EAC.
- Branches to submandibular and sublingual glands.
- Five terminal branches: motor supply to muscles of facial expression.

**Topodiagnosis**

A. **Lesion above the first genu — suprageniculate lesion**
   - Loss of lacrimation, stapedial reflex and taste from the anterior 2/3 of the tongue; dry mouth and LMN-type facial palsy.
B. **Lesion at the horizontal segment / infrageniculate / suprastapedial level**
   - Lacrimation is preserved and the rest of the functions are lost.
C. **Lesion at the vertical / mastoid segment**
   - Schirmer’s test and acoustic reflex are preserved; the rest are lost.
D. **Lesion outside the temporal bone**
   - Motor paralysis of the face, along with sparing of all the nerves/branches, as stated in the source.

**Surgical landmarks** *(lecture timestamp 00:16:38)*

- Processus cochleariformis: the geniculate ganglion lies in front of it.
- Oval window and horizontal canal: the facial nerve lies between them.
- Pyramid and fossa incudis.
- Digastric ridge.

### Page 197 — Bell’s palsy, Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome and Ramsay Hunt syndrome

**Bell’s palsy** *(lecture timestamp 00:16:59)*

- Idiopathic facial nerve palsy.
- Peripheral facial lesions are more common than central lesions; about 2/3 of peripheral lesions are Bell’s palsy, according to the source.
- Risk is stated to be three times greater in pregnancy, especially in the third trimester.
- Most commonly associated with HSV or EBV.
- Increased risk with diabetes.

**Clinical features**

- Sudden onset, usually over hours, with maximal weakness within 48 hours.
- Unilateral.
- Eyebrow sagging.
- Inability to close the eye.
- Loss of the nasolabial fold.
- Decreased tearing.
- Hyperacusis.
- Loss of taste from the anterior 2/3 of the tongue.
- Mouth droop.

**Diagnosis and management**

- Diagnosis is by exclusion.
- Supportive treatment: eye care and physiotherapy.
- Medical management: prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day).
  - 10 days for recovery.
  - 20 days if recovery is incomplete.
- Surgical management: decompression of the vertical, tympanic, with or without labyrinthine, segments.
- Prognosis: 90% recover; good recovery is noted in incomplete Bell’s palsy.

**Merkelson Rosenthal Syndrome** *(spelling as printed on page 197)*

- Recurrent facial nerve palsy.
- Swelling of the lips.
- Fissured tongue.

**Recurrent facial nerve palsy — listed causes**

- Melkersson syndrome.
- Diabetes.
- Sarcoidosis.
- Tumours.

**Ramsay Hunt syndrome / herpes zoster oticus**

- Treatment: acyclovir/valacyclovir plus steroids.
- Prognosis is poorer than Bell’s palsy.

### Page 198 — Temporal-bone fractures and electrophysiological tests

**Fractures of the temporal bone** *(lecture timestamp 00:19:38)*

- Which is common? — Longitudinal.
- Tympanic-membrane perforation occurs commonly in — Longitudinal.
- Facial nerve palsy is higher in — Transverse. The source adds the parenthetical phrase “Facial nerve runs parallel to the longitudinal,” which is retained and flagged because the phrasing is difficult to reconcile with the answer.
- Conductive hearing loss (CHL) and CSF otorrhoea: longitudinal.
- Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL): transverse.
- CSF rhinorrhoea: both longitudinal and transverse.

**Electrophysiological tests** *(lecture timestamp 00:21:30)*

| Electroneuronography | Electromyography |
|---|---|
| If there is a >90 decrease in CMAP within 14 days of paralysis, the chance of spontaneous recovery is described as negligible; consider surgical decompression. | If motor-unit potentials are present, the source says the nerve is regenerating. If fibrillation potentials are present, the chance of spontaneous recovery is described as negligible. If there is no electrical output, facial reanimation may be considered. |

**Tests for facial nerve palsy** *(lecture timestamp 00:22:35)*

- **Topodiagnostic tests** — used to determine the site of the nerve lesion:
  - Schirmer test: assesses lacrimation (dry eye).
  - Stapedial reflex: the source associates its absence with hyperacusis.
  - Evaluation of taste and salivation.
- **Electrophysiological tests** — helpful in assessing prognosis:
  - Electroneuronography (ENoG): should not be performed before 3 days, as Wallerian degeneration begins after this period.
  - Electromyography (EMG): usually performed after more than 3 weeks.

**One-liners**

- First branch of facial nerve → GSPN.
- Nerve involved in hyperacusis → nerve to stapedius.
- Lacrimation is affected when the facial nerve is injured at the 1st genu.
- Taste is affected when the facial nerve is injured at the vertical segment.
- Most common cause of facial nerve palsy → idiopathic/trauma.
- Iatrogenic facial palsy occurs most commonly in mastoid surgery.
- Segment affected first in ischaemia and oedema → labyrinthine segment.
- Segment affected first in iatrogenic trauma → 2nd genu/mastoid.
- Petrous-bone fracture responsible for facial-nerve injury → transverse.
- Crocodile tears occur due to faulty reinnervation, with gustatory lacrimation described as faulty innervation of GSPN fibres with the chorda tympani nerve.

### Page 199 — Traumatic facial nerve palsy, syndromes and PYQ

**Important PYQs on traumatic facial nerve palsy**

- Most common iatrogenic cause — parotid surgery.
- Second most common iatrogenic cause — mastoid surgery.
- Most common site injured in mastoid surgery — 2nd genu, followed by the vertical segment.
- Non-iatrogenic trauma — more common in a transverse fracture of the temporal bone.
- Overall incidence of longitudinal fracture is greater than transverse fracture.
- Ecchymosis behind the ear following basilar skull fracture — **Battle’s sign**.
- Best investigation for petrous temporal-bone fracture — HRCT.
- Following trauma, late-onset palsy management — steroids.
- Following trauma, sudden-onset palsy management — re-exploration and repair.
- Aberrant regeneration of the facial nerve can lead to crocodile tears and synkinesis.

**Syndromes associated with the facial nerve**

- Millard–Gubler syndrome.
- Melkerson-Rosenthal syndrome.
- Heerfordt syndrome.
- Ramsay Hunt syndrome.

**Faulty innervation**

- **Crocodile tears:** injury is before the geniculate ganglion; reinnervating fibres anastomose with the chorda tympani (CT) nerve.
- **Frey’s syndrome:** gustatory sweating; faulty reinnervation with the AT nerve, as stated in the source.

# 19. Instruments

## Page 200 — Instruments used in tonsillectomy

**Boyle Davis mouth gag with tongue blade** *(lecture timestamp 00:00:19)*

- Self-retracting mouth gag.
- Stabilised by Draffkin’s bipod on Magaurens plate.
- The upper end with two blades retracts the upper jaw.
- The lower end with the tongue blade retracts the lower jaw.
- The illustration labels the Draffkin’s bipod and Magaurens plate.

**Mollison tonsillor dissector**

- The tonsil is pulled medially with tonsil-holding forceps and dissected from the tonsillar bed using the Mollison tonsillor dissector.
- It also has an anterior-pillar retractor to retract the anterior pillar and visualise the tonsillar bed to check for a bleeder.

**Waugh’s tenaculum forceps**

- Used to hold tissue.
- Can be toothed or untoothed.

**Eve’s tonsillar snare**

- Used to dissect the lower pole of the tonsil.
- The pedicle is engaged in the snare and pulled so that the base of the tonsil is crushed and cut.

**Yonkos suction**

- Used to suction the tonsillar bed to clear bleeding.

## Page 201 — Adenoidectomy and ear examination instruments

**Instruments used in adenoidectomy** *(lecture timestamp 00:02:40)*

**St Clair Thompson adenoidectomy curette**

- Has a detachable cage.
- Held like a dagger; it passes from the oropharynx to the nasopharynx above, and the adenoid is churned off its bed.
- Initially used with a cage to prevent chunks of adenoid from being aspirated.
- Later used without a cage to smoothen the bed of the tonsil, as stated in the source.

**Bull’s lamp and head mirror**

- The head mirror is concave.
- Reflects light from the Bull’s lamp onto the area of interest.

**Freers elevator and Kellians elevator**

- Used to palpate or elevate tissue.

**Jobson Horne probe with ring curette**

- Used to remove wax or a foreign body.

**Siegels Pnuematic Speculum**

- Used to assess the mobility of the tympanic membrane.
- Used to increase pressure in the EAC to elicit the fistula sign.
- The speculum end goes into the EAC.
- The cuffed end increases and decreases the pressure.

## Page 202 — Otoscope, syringe, myringotome, grommet and mastoid retractors

**Otoscope**

- Used to visualise the outer ear and tympanic membrane.
- Magnification of 2–2.5 times is obtained.

**Simpson’s aural syringe**

- Used in syringing to remove a non-hygroscopic foreign body.
- Directed posterosuperiorly to avoid hitting the tympanic membrane directly.
- Water temperature should be at body temperature.
- Water 7°C above or below body temperature may be used, but elicits a caloric reaction in the caloric test, as stated in the source.

**Myringotome**

- Used to make a hole in the tympanic membrane to drain pus in ASOM or CSOM.

**Grommet**

- Ventilating tube used in serous otitis media.

**Mastoid retractors**

- **Mollison self-retaining hemostatic mastoid retractor:** equal number of prongs on both blades; examples: 4 and 4, or 3 and 3.
- **Withlaner self-retaining hemostatic mastoid retractor:** unequal number of prongs on both blades; examples: 4 and 3, or 3 and 2.

## Phase C — P2 verification notes (editorial layer)

> [!note] ENT-A028 — Facial-nerve lesion diagram percentages (PDF p. 193)
> The full-resolution diagram resolves the arithmetic: labyrinthine segment **3**, geniculate ganglion **4**, and tympanic segment **7** sum to **14 of 15 cases (93.3%)**, grouped as the perigeniculate region; the vertical segment is **1 of 15 (6.6%)**. The percentages therefore correspond to regional grouping/counts rather than a separate unexplained statistic. Source diagram retained.

> [!note] ENT-A029 — Facial-nerve lesion-level topodiagnosis (PDF p. 196)
> Interpret preserved/lost functions relative to the order of branches, not segment names alone: the greater superficial petrosal nerve carries preganglionic parasympathetic fibres for lacrimation; the nerve to stapedius mediates the stapedius reflex; the chorda tympani carries taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue and secretomotor fibres to submandibular/sublingual glands. Lesions proximal/distal to these branches produce different combinations, and anatomical variation matters. The source’s simplified levels are retained. Reference: [Facial nerve: anatomical course and lesion localisation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4314567/).

> [!note] ENT-A030 — Temporal-bone fracture MCQ (PDF p. 198)
> The classic teaching is that transverse fractures more often involve the otic capsule and are associated with facial palsy/sensorineural loss, whereas longitudinal fractures more often spare it and produce conductive loss or tympanic-membrane injury. The printed explanation (“facial nerve runs parallel to the longitudinal”) does not by itself adequately justify the key. Modern literature favours **otic-capsule-violating versus otic-capsule-sparing** classification because it better stratifies complications than the traditional longitudinal/transverse labels. The source key is preserved, with this caveat. References: [Temporal Bone Fractures: Otic Capsule Sparing vs Violating](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10608536/) and [2024 systematic review/meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38168743/).

> [!warning] ENT-A031 — ENoG degeneration threshold (PDF p. 198)
> The source reads **“If >90 decrease in CMAP within 14 days”**, with no percentage sign. The intended expression is likely **>90% degeneration**, but it should not be silently inserted into the original transcription. A review describes potential benefit from decompression in selected patients with complete facial paralysis, >90% ENoG degeneration, and absent voluntary EMG within 14 days; this is not a universal stand-alone surgical indication. Reference: [Facial Nerve Decompression review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30138146/).

> [!note] ENT-A032 — Water-caloric test temperatures (PDF p. 202)
> With a nominal body temperature of 37°C, water at **30°C** and **44°C** is 7°C below/above body temperature and corresponds to a common bithermal water-caloric protocol. Actual protocols and technical conditions should follow the testing service’s validated standard. Reference: [Bárány Society: caloric testing consensus/criteria](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/VES-170619).

> [!warning] ENT-A059 — Forehead involvement in UMN versus LMN facial palsy (PDF p. 192)
> The source line reverses the usual exam teaching and conflicts with its own explanation. A unilateral supranuclear/UMN lesion typically causes contralateral lower facial weakness with **relative forehead sparing** due to bilateral corticobulbar input; a peripheral/nuclear LMN facial palsy typically causes ipsilateral weakness involving both upper and lower face. This is the standard pattern, with clinical exceptions (e.g. bilateral lesions) possible. The source transcription remains unchanged; this note is the correction layer. Reference: [StatPearls: Facial Nerve Palsy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK549815/).
