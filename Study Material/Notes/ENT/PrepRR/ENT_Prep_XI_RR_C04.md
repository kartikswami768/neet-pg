## Choanal atresia (00:00:01; FMGE 2023)

- Due to <strong>persistence of the bucconasal membrane.</strong>
- Associated with **CHARGE syndrome**:
  - **C** — Coloboma.
  - **H** — Heart defect.
  - **A** — Atresia.
  - **R** — Retardation (growth and mental retardation).
  - **G** — Genital malformation.
  - **E** — Ear abnormalities.
- Can be unilateral or bilateral.
- Bilateral atresia is an emergency.
###### Illustrations
![[p107_choanal-atresia-ct-imaging-axial-and-sagittal.jpg]]
![[p106_choanal-atresia-nasal-endoscopy-clinical-images.jpg]]
### Bilateral choanal atresia

- Presents as an emergency at birth.
- Infants are obligate nasal breathers until 3 months of age, as stated in the source.
- The choana fails to communicate with the nasopharynx; the patient is unable to breathe.
- **Cyclical cyanosis:** cyanosis is present at rest and disappears on crying.
	- Mechanism: at rest, the nose and mouth are blocked, causing cyanosis.
	- On crying, negative intrathoracic pressure opens the mouth; air enters the lungs and cyanosis improves.
- Crying improves stridor.

### Unilateral choanal atresia

- Not an emergency; presents later in life.
- A cold or upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) may trigger difficulty breathing and stridor.
- Constant unilateral rhinorrhea:
	- Secretions on the normal side drain into the nasopharynx.
	- Secretions on the atretic side cannot drain posteriorly and therefore come anteriorly.
	- This results in constant unilateral rhinorrhea, crusting and skin excoriation.
- Crusting and excoriation of the skin may be present.

### Diagnosis

- **Simple bedside test:** pass a Foley catheter through the nose.
	- Normal: it passes into the nasopharynx and down to the oropharynx.
	- Resistance or inability to pass it indicates choanal atresia.
- Endoscopic examination under anaesthesia.
- CT scan confirms the diagnosis by showing no communication between the nose and nasopharynx.

### Treatment of choanal atresia

#### Bilateral choanal atresia
1. **McGovern's technique:** provide a feeding nipple (McGovern's nipple) to allow simultaneous breathing and feeding; this temporarily restores the airway.
2. **Tracheostomy:** bypass the upper airway and secure it for a longer period.
3. **Definitive repair:** at 1 to 1.5 years of age.

#### Unilateral choanal atresia
- Requires no intervention at birth.
- Direct surgical repair is performed at 1 to 1.5 years of age.

### Error files

- Choanal atresia:
	- Cyclical cyanosis — cyanosis that gets better on crying.
	- Crying improves stridor.
- ==Crying worsens stridor in:==
	- ==Acute epiglottitis.==
	- ==Croup.==
	- ==Laryngomalacia.==

## Septal diseases

### Deviated nasal septum (DNS) (NEET PG 2022; FMGE 2023)
- The septum does not lie in the midline and fails to divide the nasal cavities into equal halves.
- Described in the source as extremely common, affecting approximately 95% of patients.

#### Indications for surgery
- Presence of symptoms not resolving with medical therapy.
- Symptoms include:
	- Nasal blockade.
	- Headache.
		- Sinusitis
		- Anterior Ethmoidal Syndrome / Sluder's Neuralgia
	- Smell abnormalities.
	- Facial pain.
	- External deformities of the nose.
- Types of surgery:
	- Septoplasty — described as a very conservative type of surgery.
	- ==Submucous resection (SMR).==
	- <u>No septal surgery is preferred until 17 years of age, according to the source.</u>

#### Cottle's line
![[IMG_1276.jpeg]]

- An imaginary line drawn from the frontal spine to the anterior nasal spine.
- <strong>Anterior deviations (anterior to the line)</strong> → treated only with <u>septoplasty.</u>
- <strong>Posterior deviations (posterior to the line)</strong> → treated with ==either septoplasty or SMR.==

### Septal abscess versus septal haematoma

Both present with:
1. bilateral septal swelling, 
2. nasal blockade, 
3. pain and 
4. breathing difficulty.

**Septal haematoma**
- History of trauma.
- Accumulation of blood between cartilage/bone and the overlying perichondrium/mucosa.

**Septal abscess**
- History of infection or <u>untreated septal haematoma</u>.
- Accumulation of pus between the septum and overlying mucosa.

## Septal perforation

- A hole in the septum allowing communication between the left and right nasal cavities.

### Cartilaginous portion perforations

- Most common site.
- Causes:
  - Trauma (finger, nose rings).
  - ==Inhalation of fumes== (asbestos, mustard gas).
  - Cocaine sniffing.
  - Diseases (==tuberculosis, leprosy==, sarcoidosis).

### Bony portion perforations

- Typically seen in <strong><mark style="background-color: #ffd1e4; color: #5c203d;">syphilis.</mark></strong>

### Total septal perforations

- Typically seen in <mark style="background-color: #ffd1e4; color: #5c203d;"><strong>Wegener's granulomatosis</strong>.</mark> (granulomatosis with polyangitis)

### Presentation

- Small perforation — <em>whistling</em> sound during respiration.
- Large perforation — airflow dries the edges → <em>crusting;</em> removal of crusts leads to *bleeding*.

### Treatment

- Most are asymptomatic and <strong>require no intervention</strong>.
- If symptomatic, insert a <u>Silastic button.</u>

## Nasal polyps (00:20:07; FMGE 2023)

- **Definition:** chronically inflamed, oedematous tissue that prolapses out. *(FMGE 2023)*

### Antrochoanal polyp

- Origin: maxillary antrum.
- Grows into the nasal cavity, reaching the nasopharynx.
- Single, unilateral mass.
- Aetiology listed: infection (commonly *Streptococcus pneumoniae*).
- Young age group; male predominant.

#### Presentation

- Unilateral nasal blockade.
- Nasal obstruction and discharge.
- Widening of the nose.
- Hyposmia.

#### Diagnosis

- On examination: greyish-white polypoidal mass ==growing posteriorly.==
- Next step: CT scan — shows unilateral haziness.

#### Treatment

- No role for medical therapy, according to the source.
- Surgery → FESS (functional endoscopic sinus surgery).

#### FESS (Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery)

- The bulla ethmoidalis lies behind the uncinate process.
- Sequence of FESS:
	1. Uncinectomy.
	2. Maxillary sinusotomy.
	3. Anterior ethmoidectomy.
	4. Posterior ethmoidectomy.
	5. Sphenoidotomy.
	6. Frontal sinusotomy.
- FESS has largely replaced the Caldwell–Luc procedure.


> [!note]- Why do ethmoidectomy when we are only doing "sinusotomy" for other sinuses?
>Imagine the ethmoid labyrinth as a honeycomb.
Each cell has its own walls, and the cells communicate through relatively narrow openings.
>
>In chronic rhinosinusitis, inflammation may involve multiple cells, producing:
>- Edematous mucosa that narrows the drainage pathways.
>- Polyps or inflammatory tissue obstructing the labyrinth.
>- Persistent disease within multiple interconnected cells.
>Simply enlarging one entrance would not necessarily address disease in the individual cells behind it.
>
>Therefore, ethmoidectomy opens the affected ethmoid compartments by removing selected intercellular bony partitions and diseased tissue, creating a more accessible, interconnected cavity.
>
>Importantly, the surgeon does not routinely remove every ethmoid air cell in every patient. The extent of ethmoidectomy depends on disease distribution and the intended surgical outcome.
>![[IMG_1280.webp]]
### Ethmoidal polyp (NEET PG 2022; FMGE 2023)

- Arises from ethmoidal air cells.
- Occurs secondary to **allergy**.
- Affects **both sides of the nose** → **==Bilateral==** → Both sides of ethmoid air cells are connected
- Multiple masses/polypoidal tissue grow anteriorly to the vestibule of the nose.
	- Remember that Ethmoidal sinus itself has multiple cells, so its polyp will also be like that. 
- Bilateral.
- **Bunch-of-grapes appearance** on examination.
- Associated syndromes/conditions:
	- Chronic rhinosinusitis.
	- Asthma.
	- Cystic fibrosis.
	- **==Kartagener's syndrome==:** bronchiectasis, sinusitis, situs inversus.
	- **==Young syndrome==:** bronchiectasis, sinusitis and obstructive azoospermia.
	- **Churg–Strauss syndrome**: asthma, fever, eosinophilia, vasculitis and granuloma.
	- ==**Samter's triad:**== asthma, nasal polyps and aspirin intolerance.
		- Not only aspirin but other COX1 inhibitors also.

1. Medical therapy:
	- Steroid — primary treatment.
		- Since Ethmoid polyp is due to allergy sometimes steroid can cause *Medical Polypectomy*
	- Antiallergics.
	- Mast-cell stabiliser.
2. If medical therapy fails → CT scan → FESS → if even FESS fails → Ethmoidectomy

### Inverted Papilloma

- **Definition:** Benign epithelial tumour of the sinonasal tract with endophytic (inward) growth into the underlying stroma, arising from Schneiderian epithelium.
- **Common site:** Lateral nasal wall, especially middle meatus; commonly involves the maxillary and ethmoid sinuses.
- **Clinical features:** Unilateral nasal obstruction, unilateral nasal discharge and epistaxis; typically occurs in middle-aged males.
- **CT:** Focal hyperostosis indicates the site of tumour attachment.
- **MRI:** Convoluted cerebriform pattern.
- **Histopathology:** Inward proliferation of epithelium into the underlying stroma.
- **Malignant potential:** Associated with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).
- **Recurrence:** High risk if incompletely excised.
- **Treatment:** Endoscopic surgical excision with complete removal of the tumour attachment site.

## Granulomatous diseases of the nose (00:34:08)

- Rhinosporidiosis.
- Rhinoscleroma.

### Rhinosporidiosis (NEET PG 2018)

- Caused by the protozoan *Rhinosporidium seeberi*.
	- Aquatic protozoan.
	- Endemic in South India.
	- Transmission: waterborne route (contact with contaminated ponds).
- Presentation: nasal blockade and bleeding.
- On examination: <u>red hanging mass</u> with whitish dots on the surface — <strong>“strawberry” mass</strong>.
- CT scan can be done.
- Treatment: c<u>auterise the base and excise the mass.</u>
- ==<strong>HPE: shows multiple sporangia with spores inside.</strong>==

![[p113_rhinosporidiosis-clinical-nasal-mass-and-sporangia-histopathology.jpg]]![[p112_rhinoscleroma-nasal-lesion-and-histopathology.jpg]]

### Rhinoscleroma

- Granulomatous condition caused by the bacterium ***Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis*** (Frisch bacillus).
- Endemic in ==North India.==
- Transmission: air droplets.

**Stages of disease**

1. **Atrophic stage:** bacteria destroy mucin-producing cells → nose becomes dry, with crusting and foul smell.
2. **Granulomatous stage:** immune response creates a central necrotic area surrounded by inflammatory cells → <strong><mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">“woody hard granuloma”</mark></strong> → <mark style="background-color: #c6e3ff; color: #173653;"><strong>“Hebra nose” deformity.</strong></mark>
3. **Stage of cicatrisation:** healing by fibrosis with stenosis/narrowing of the nose → ==“Tapir nose” deformity.==

'**Complications**'
- can cause Sugglottic Stenosis (Laryngoscleroma)

- Histopathology: **Mikulicz cells** and **Russell bodies**.
- Culture on **MacConkey agar**.
- **Management:**
	- Medical therapy: ==streptomycin, tetracycline and **rifampicin**== for *4–6 weeks*.
	- ==Therapy is stopped only after two consecutive cultures, 4 weeks apart, are negative.==

### Comparison between Rhinosporidiosis and Rhinoscleroma
| Feature                   | Rhinosporidiosis                                                  | Rhinoscleroma                                                                |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Mode of transmission      | Water exposure                                                    | Air droplets                                                                 |
| Common region             | South India                                                       | North India                                                                  |
| Symptoms                  | Nasal blockage and bleeding                                       | Crusting, foul smell, granuloma, narrowing/stenosis of the nose              |
| Characteristic appearance | Strawberry-like mass                                              | Woody hard granuloma, Hebra nose, Tapir nose                                 |
| Nasal deformity           | —                                                                 | Hebra nose, Tapir nose                                                       |
| Diagnosis                 | HPE — final diagnosis                                             | Culture positive on MacConkey agar; Mikulicz cells and Russell bodies on HPE |

## Rhinitis (00:45:22)

### Allergic rhinitis versus non-allergic/vasomotor rhinitis

| Feature | Allergic rhinitis | Non-allergic rhinitis/vasomotor rhinitis |
|---|---|---|
| Serum IgE | Elevated | Normal |
| Presentation | Sneezing; itching; watery discharge; nasal blockade | Sneezing; itching; watery discharge; nasal blockade |
| Other findings/pathophysiology | Mucosa is pale, boggy and oedematous | Imbalance of the autonomic nervous system (parasympathetic hyperactivity + sympathetic hypoactivity); on examination, mulberry turbinate or hypertrophic turbinate |
| Management | First line: intranasal corticosteroids + antihistamines + decongestants | First line: intranasal corticosteroids + antihistamines + decongestants |
| If unresolved | Skin-prick test/radioallergosorbent test to identify the specific allergen → immunotherapy (subcutaneous or sublingual); immunotherapy types: SCIT, SLIT | If medical therapy fails → surgical reduction of inferior turbinates; final surgical option → Vidian neurectomy |
| Specific signs | Allergic shiners: dark circles around the eyes. Allergic salute: constant upward rubbing of the nose. Darier's line/crease: dark transverse crease on the nasal dorsum. | — |

### Atrophic rhinitis
- Atrophy of the entire sinus mucosa, nasal mucosal glands and olfactory epithelium.
- Results in a dry nose, greenish foul-smelling crusts and merciful anosmia.
- **Merciful anosmia:** the patient does not perceive the foul smell because of olfactory epithelium atrophy, but people around them do.
- **Paradoxical symptom:** the patient complains of nasal blockade despite wide, roomy chambers (loss of sensation of airflow).

**Causes — mnemonic HENIA + R (as printed)**
- H → Hereditary.
- E → Endocrinological problems (↓ Estrogen)
- R → Racial.
- N → Nutritional deficiencies (vitamins A, **==D==** and E).
- I → Infective causes (***Klebsiella ozaenae***, most common cause, also called ==Perez bacillus==).
- A → Autoimmune causes.

**Treatment**

1. **Medical therapy:**
	- Irrigate the nose to keep it clean and moist.
	- Solution: <strong><u>alkaline nasal douching solution</u></strong>
		- Composition, 
			- mixed in 280 mL water
			- 2 parts sodium chloride 
			- 1 part sodium bicarbonate 
			- 1 part sodium biborate.
	- Other options: 
		- placental extracts, 
		- oestrogen/estradiol spray, 
		- ==**Kemecetine** anti-ozaena solutions.==
2. **Surgery if medical therapy fails:**
   - **Young's operation:** complete surgical closure of the nasal cavity (elevating mucosa on the septum, lateral wall and floor).
   - **Modified Young's operation:** leaves a 10 mm gap for breathing.
   - **Lautenschlager's operation:** medialisation of the lateral wall of the nose.
### Other rhinitis conditions

#### **Rhinitis sicca**
- <u>Similar to atrophic rhinitis but limited to the anterior one-third of the nose</u>.
- **Aetiology**: <u>constant exposure to dry air currents, hot/humid environments.</u>
- Symptoms: nasal crusting, foul smell and nasal blockade.
- Treatment: saline irrigation spray and changing profession.

#### **Rhinitis caseosa**
- Accumulation of cheesy material within the sinuses.
- Aetiology: chronic obstruction of the nose/sinuses (e.g., polyp, foreign body, DNS) → retention of secretions.
- Symptoms: cheesy, thick, mucoid, foul-smelling discharge with crusting.
- Treatment: surgical removal of the obstruction to clear secretions and restore a patent airway.

#### **Tuberculosis**
- Primary TB: occurs directly inside the nose.
- Secondary TB: occurs secondary to an active pulmonary TB focus.
- Presentation: granuloma, usually affecting the cartilaginous portion of the nose.
- Complication: septal perforation.
- Diagnosis: GeneXpert or growth on Lowenstein–Jensen (LJ) medium.
- Treatment: antitubercular therapy (ATT).

#### **Lupus vulgaris**
- Low-grade, <u>indolent ==tubercular== infection of the nos</u>e.
- Usually ==not associated with active pulmonary TB.==
- Specific sign: ==apple-jelly nodule==; <u>the nodule blanches on application of pressure.</u>
- Treatment: ==ATT==.

#### **Sarcoidosis**
- Non-caseating granuloma inside the nose or sinuses.
- Specific sign: “**strawberry mucosa**”.
	- ==Differential note: “**strawberry polyp**” indicates rhinosporidiosis.==
- Complications: destructive lesions affecting the cartilaginous septum → saddle-nose deformity and chronic sinusitis.
- Can be associated with systemic sarcoidosis manifestations.

## Epistaxis (01:01:49)

- Any bleeding from the nose.

### Blood supply of the nasal septum

- **Upper half:** internal carotid artery (ICA) circulation.
  - ICA → ophthalmic artery.
  - Ophthalmic artery → anterior ethmoidal artery (supplies the anterosuperior septum) + posterior ethmoidal artery (supplies the posterosuperior septum).
- **Lower half:** external carotid artery (ECA) circulation.
  - ECA → facial artery + internal maxillary artery.
  - Facial artery → superior labial artery.
  - Internal maxillary artery → greater palatine artery + sphenopalatine artery (both supply the posteroinferior septum).
- **Vascular plexuses:**
  - Little's area/Kiesselbach's plexus:
    - Anastomosis at the anteroinferior part of the nose.
    - Any bleed → anterior epistaxis.
  - Woodruff's plexus:
    - Venous plexus at the posterior end of the inferior turbinate, according to the text on this page.
    - Any bleed → posterior epistaxis.
- **Artery of epistaxis:** sphenopalatine artery.

### Course of the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries

- The internal carotid artery gives a branch → ophthalmic artery.
- The ophthalmic artery crosses the optic nerve (lateral to medial) and enters the medial wall of the orbit.
- Divides into anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries along the medial wall.
- **Rule of 24–12–6** (using the anterior lacrimal crest as landmark):
  - Anterior ethmoidal artery: 24 mm behind the crest.
  - Posterior ethmoidal artery: 12 mm behind the anterior ethmoidal artery.
  - Optic canal: 6 mm behind the posterior ethmoidal artery.
- The anterior ethmoidal artery passes through the anterior ethmoidal foramen and ethmoidal air cells to supply the septum.
  - Surgical risk: may be traumatised during ethmoidectomy.

### Course of the sphenopalatine and greater palatine arteries

- Internal maxillary artery branches run within the pterygopalatine fossa (behind the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus).
- Ligation of the internal maxillary artery is done in the pterygopalatine fossa.
- <strong>Sphenopalatine artery:</strong> <u>ligated approximately 8–10 mm</u> behind the <u>middle turbinate.</u>
- <strong>Greater palatine artery:</strong> dips down to the palate; <u>ligated near the third molar</u>.

### Causes of epistaxis

- **Local causes:** trauma, tumour, granulomatous diseases, rhinosporidiosis.
- **Systemic causes:**
  - Hypertension (most common systemic cause).
  - Blood disorders: haemophilia, von Willebrand disease, sickle-cell anaemia.
  - Coagulation/bleeding disorders.
  - Blood-vessel disorders: hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia.
  - Medications: oral anticoagulants, thrombolytics, antiplatelets, OCPs.
  - Liver disease (e.g., cirrhosis due to alcoholism or drugs).
  - Renal disease.

### Protocol for epistaxis (01:10:20)
![[p117_epistaxis-assessment-and-management-algorithm.jpg]]
The original flowchart is preserved in the source-page embed. The accompanying steps are:

1. ==**Trotter's method:**== pinch the nose and <u>bend forward</u> for approximately 15 minutes.
2. **Initial medical review and resuscitation.**
3. **Nasal preparation for endoscopy:** spray ==Xylocaine (numbing) and adrenaline (reduces bleeding).==
4. **Endoscopic cauterisation:** if a single bleeding point is seen, use ==chemical cautery (silver nitrate solution==) or electrical cautery (monopolar/bipolar).
5. **Anterior nasal packing:** done if no bleeding point is seen, endoscopy is unavailable, or cautery fails.
6. **Posterior nasal packing:** done if anterior packing fails.
7. **Surgical ligation of an artery (last resort):**
   8. Sphenopalatine artery.
   9. Internal maxillary artery.
   10. External carotid artery (if bleeding is from the lower half).
   11. Ethmoidal artery (if bleeding is from the upper half).

**Posterior nasal packing — procedure**
- Pass a Ryle's/infant feeding tube through the nose to the mouth.
- Attach threaded ribbon gauze.
- Retract the tube from the nose; the gauze sits in the nasopharynx, then block the anterior nose.

### One-liners

- Most common cause of epistaxis: trauma.
- Most common cause of epistaxis in children: finger trauma.
- Most common cause of epistaxis with foul-smelling unilateral nasal discharge in children: ==foreign body.==
- <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Most common cause of recurrent, unprovoked, profuse epistaxis in an adolescent male:</mark> ==JNA==.
- Most common cause of epistaxis in the elderly: HTN.
- <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Most common site/source of epistaxis listed</mark>: ==sphenopalatine artery.==
- Anterior epistaxis, if venous, occurs from the ==Reterocolumella vein== (source spelling is retained in the image; see QA note).

## Fractures of the face (01:20:58)

- Source wording: “Middle third and lower thyroid mostly”. This wording is unclear in the scan and is not silently replaced.
- Fracture frequency sequence as printed: ==nasal fracture (most common)== > zygomatic fractures > maxillary fractures > ==floor-of-orbit fractures.==

### Nasal fractures

- **Class 1 — Chevallet fracture:**
	- Vertical fracture line.
	- Direction of injury from below.
- **Class 2 — Jarjaway fracture:**
	- Horizontal fracture line.
	- Direction of injury from the front.
- **Class 3 — naso-orbito-ethmoid fracture:**
	- Entire component gets crushed and rotated; nasal part points upwards → **pig-nose deformity.**
	- ==High risk of CSF leak.==

### Zygomatic fracture

- Three fracture lines:
  - Zygomaticomaxillary suture.
  - Zygomaticofrontal suture.
  - Zygomaticotemporal suture.
- Tripod fracture.
- Crush of zygoma → step deformity.

### Floor-of-orbit fracture

- Floor of the orbit has no support inferiorly because of the maxillary sinus.
- Called a **blow-out fracture**.
- Orbital contents herniate into the maxillary sinus → ==tear-drop sign.==
- Inferior rectus and inferior oblique may become entrapped → diplopia.

### Maxillary fracture — Le Fort fractures
![[p120_le-fort-midface-fracture-lines.jpg]]
**Le Fort I**
- Fracture through the floor of the nose and floor of the maxilla.
- Also called transverse fracture/**Guerin's fracture**.
- **Greater palatine artery rupture** → bluish discolouration of palate called **==Guerin's sign==**.
- ==Hanging palate and hanging teeth== are seen.

**Le Fort II**
- Pyramidal or pyramid-shaped fracture.
- Course: nasion → lacrimal bone → medial wall of orbit → floor of orbit → maxilla.
- Key feature: ==hanging maxilla.==

**Le Fort III**
- Course: nasion → lacrimal bone → medial wall of orbit → floor of orbit → lateral wall of orbit → zygoma.
- Key feature: <u>disconnects the cranial cavity from the facial skeleton.</u>
- Also called **craniofacial disjunction.**
- Associated with a **==high risk of CSF leak (CSF rhinorrhea).==**

> ![[images/chunk04_p120_source_page.jpeg]]

### Fracture of mandible

- Most common site: **==subcondylar region.==**
- Fractures rarely occur at the direct site of trauma; impact forces are transmitted to the opposite side (counter-coup effect), resulting in an opposite subcondylar fracture.

**Guardsman fracture/parade-ground fracture**
- Patient falls forward directly on the symphysis menti (common in guards/marching personnel).
- Fracture at the symphysis menti plus bilateral subcondylar fractures.

**Management of mandibular fractures**
- Closed reduction with internal fixation or open reduction with internal fixation.
- <u>Do not immobilise the mandible for more than 3–4 weeks.</u>
- <u>Prolonged immobilisation (>4 weeks) can lead to permanent <strong><mark style="background-color: #ffd1e4; color: #5c203d;">TMJ ankylosis</mark></strong> and inability to open the mouth.</u>

### One-liners

- Most common site of mandibular fractures: subcondylar region.
- Subcondylar fractures occur due to trauma to the body of the mandible from the opposite side.
- When closed reduction is done, the mandible should not be immobilised for 3–4 weeks.

## CSF rhinorrhea (01:34:40)

- Leakage of cerebrospinal fluid into the nasal cavity.
- Most common cause: trauma (95%).
- Rare cause: increased intracranial pressure (e.g., benign intracranial hypertension) → thinning of the bone separating the cranial and nasal cavities → leakage.

### Clinical presentation

- History of trauma followed by watery nasal discharge.

### Diagnosis

**Clinical bedside tests** (to differentiate CSF from nasal mucin):

- **Sniff test:** nasal fluid can be sniffed back; CSF cannot be sniffed back.
- **Handkerchief test:** nasal fluid stiffens the cloth on drying; CSF does not stiffen the cloth.
- **Reservoir sign:** bending forward in a gravity-dependent position → increased watery discharge.
- **Halo test/double-ring sign/target sign** (for traumatic cases with mixed blood): filter paper shows a central red dot surrounded by a clear peripheral ring.
- **Valsalva manoeuvre/coughing:** increases intracranial pressure → increased CSF flow.
- Note: all clinical tests are non-specific.

### Confirmatory laboratory investigations

- <strong>Investigation of choice (IOC):</strong> ==beta-2 transferrin test== (protein specific to CSF, aqueous humour and perilymph).
- Newer highly selective test: beta-trace protein (specific only to CSF).

### Radiological investigations

- HRCT: identifies the exact site of the bony defect.
- CT cisternography: identifies active leaks.
- MRI: compares fluid intensity (CSF versus nasal fluid).

### Management

- Transnasal endoscopic repair of the defect.

## Tumours of the nose and paranasal sinuses (PNS) (01:41:02)

### Osteoma

- Most common benign tumour of the PNS, as stated in the source.
- Site: frontal > frontoethmoid region.
- HPE: dense lamellar bone lacking Haversian canals.
- Associated condition: Gardner syndrome (osteomas + colorectal polyps).
- Management: <strong>Lynch–Howarth external frontoethmoidectomy.</strong>
  - The Lynch–Howarth incision starts at the medial edge of the eyebrow, curves down midway between the nasal dorsum and medial canthus, and extends downwards to access the frontal and ethmoid sinuses.
- ![[p123_danger-triangle-of-face-nasal-infection-spread.jpg|Lynch Howarth Incision]]

### Sinonasal malignancies

- Aetiology: exposure to carcinogens (smoke, alcohol, tobacco).
- Most common histopathological type: squamous cell carcinoma.
- Woodworkers inhaling wood dust are at high risk for adenocarcinoma.

**Prognostic classifications**

- **==Ohngren's line==:** imaginary line from the medial canthus to the angle of the mandible.
  - Tumours below the line → good prognosis.
  - Tumours above the line → poor prognosis.
- ==**Lederman's classification:**== two imaginary lines from the medial wall of the orbit and two from the floor of the maxillary sinus.
  - Higher tumour location → poorer prognosis.
  - Lower tumour location → better prognosis.

- Dull pain over the maxillary sinus.
- Ipsilateral epiphora.
- Blood-stained nasal discharge.
- Nasal obstruction.
- Toothache.

**Diagnosis**
- Investigation of choice: biopsy.
- Radiological studies are done prior to biopsy.

# 14. Sinusitis and Complications

## Sinusitis (00:00:10; FMGE July 2023; INI CET July 2021/May 2023)

- Inflammation of the mucosa of the paranasal sinuses is called sinusitis.
- Duration less than 3 weeks → **acute sinusitis**.
- Duration 3 to 12 weeks → **subacute sinusitis**.
- Duration more than 12 weeks → **chronic sinusitis**.
- **Recurrent sinusitis:**
  - <u>Four or more episodes per year</u>.
  - <u>Each episode lasts 7–10 days</u>.
- Most common sinus affected in <strong>adults: maxillary sinus.</strong>
- Most common sinus affected in <strong>children: ethmoid.</strong>
- Least common sinus affected: sphenoid sinus.

### Aetiology

**Exciting factors**
- Nasal infection: <u>most acute sinusitis starts as viral infection followed by bacterial invasion.</u>
- Swimming and diving.
- Trauma.
- <u>Dental infection</u> (especially because the maxillary sinus is close to the first and second molars).

**Predisposing factors**
- Obstruction to ventilation of sinuses (polyp, oedema, rhinitis).
- Stasis of secretion in the nasal cavity (cystic fibrosis).
- Allergy.

**Systemic factors**
- Attack of ==exanthematous fever== (measles, chickenpox, whooping cough).
- Nutritional causes.
- Immune deficiencies.

### Bacteriology

- Bacteria listed:
	- *Streptococcus <u>pneumoniae</u> (most common organism).
	- H. influenza (spelling as printed in the source).
	- *<u>Moraxella</u>*.
	- *Streptococcus <u>pyogenes</u>*.
	- *Staphylococcus aureus*.
- <u>Anaerobic organisms and mixed infections</u> are seen in sinusitis of dental origin.

### Clinical features of acute sinusitis

| Major                                                                                                                                                                       | Minor                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Nasal obstruction<br><u>Facial pain or pressure</u><br>Purulent nasal discharge<br>Fever<br>Nasal congestion<br>Hyposmia or anosmia<br><u>Facial congestion or fullness</u> | <u>Headache</u><br>Cough<br>Halitosis<br><u>Ear pain and pressure</u><br><u>Dental pain</u> |

- For diagnosis: **2 major symptoms OR 1 major + 2 minor symptoms.**

## Treatment of sinusitis

### Acute sinusitis (NEET PG 2022)

- Mainly medical treatment: antibiotics + decongestants + analgesics + hot fomentation.
- Surgery is listed if:
  - There is no response to medical treatment.
  - It is progressing to a complication.
  - FESS (functional endoscopic sinus surgery), ethmoidectomy or trephination of the frontal sinus may be performed.

### Chronic sinusitis (FMGE July 2023)

- Maximum medical therapy: antibiotics with antral irrigation are tried, along with intranasal corticosteroids for 3 months and nasal wash.
- If symptoms persist after this → FESS.

### FESS

- First open the maxillary sinus.
- Uncinate process → boomerang-shaped bone in the anterior part of the nose.
- Behind the uncinate process is the bulla ethmoidalis.
- Space between the uncinate process and bulla → hiatus semilunaris/infundibulum.
- Opening of the maxillary sinus is in the infundibulum.

**Steps as shown in the source flowchart**

1. Uncinectomy.
2. Middle meatal antrostomy → Widening of ostia 
3. Remove anterior ethmoidal cells.
4. Remove posterior ethmoidal cells.
5. Sphenoidotomy.
6. Frontal sinusotomy.

## Complications of sinusitis

### Local complications

- Retention cyst:
  - Cystic transformation of mucosa.
- Mucocele:
  - Due to blockage of the ostium.
  - Retention of secretion in an epithelial-lined sac.
- Pyocele:
  - Infected mucocele.
- Orbital osteomyelitis:
  - If infection spreads to adjacent bone/orbit.
- Frontal bone osteomyelitis → **Pott's puffy tumour**.

### Orbital complications

- Preseptal cellulitis.
- Orbital cellulitis.
- Subperiosteal abscess:
  - Pus accumulates between bone and periosteum.
- Orbital abscess:
  - Infection breaks the periosteum and enters the orbit.
- Cavernous sinus thrombosis:
  - Infection spreads from the orbit via ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus.

### Intracranial complications

- Meningitis.
- Extradural abscess — pus between bone and dura.
- Subdural abscess — pus between dura and brain.
- Brain abscess — infection enters brain parenchyma.

### Descending infections

Nasal infection can spread to:
- Pharynx → pharyngitis.
- Larynx → laryngitis.
- Adenoids → adenoiditis.
- Tonsils → tonsillitis.

### Mucocele (00:19:50)

- Most commonly involves the frontal sinus.
- Least commonly involves the sphenoid sinus.
- Site: superomedial quadrant of the orbit.
- Symptoms: eye displaced forwards, downwards and laterally.
- Swelling: cystic, non-tender, “egg-shell cracking”.
- X-ray: loss of scalloped margins.
- Treatment: frontoethmoidectomy to promote drainage of the frontal sinus.

### Cavernous sinus thrombosis

- Aetiology: infection of the ethmoid or sphenoid sinuses is the most common cause.
- Infection can also spread from the nose, ear, throat or orbit.
- Orbital cellulitis can secondarily lead to cavernous sinus thrombosis.
- Treatment:
  - Immediate drainage of abscess and start IV antibiotics.
  - Role of anticoagulants is not clear.

> [!warning] ENT-A060 — Sinusitis investigation and management (PDF pp. 128–129)
> These pages reproduce an older, compact management pathway and should not be read as a universal current pathway. The 2025 AAO-HNS adult guideline states that imaging is not routine for uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis unless complications or an alternative diagnosis are suspected; clinicians should offer watchful waiting for adults with uncomplicated acute bacterial rhinosinusitis when follow-up is assured; and if antibiotics are selected, amoxicillin with or without clavulanate is first-line for most adults for 5–7 days. Chronic rhinosinusitis should be objectively confirmed, and “three months of antibiotics/antral irrigation then FESS” is not a universal rule. This external guideline applies to adults and does not replace separate assessment of children, complications, or specialist-specific cases. The source pathway above is retained, with this contextual caution. Reference: [AAO-HNS Clinical Practice Guideline: Adult Sinusitis Update (2025)](https://aao-hnsfjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ohn.1342).
