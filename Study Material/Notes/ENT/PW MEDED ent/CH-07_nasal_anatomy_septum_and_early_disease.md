# Anatomy and Physiology of the Nose, Sinuses and Septum

## Functions of the Nose

### Protection
- Vibrissae (nasal hair) trap macro-particles, while sticky mucus produced by goblet cells traps micro-particles.

### Respiration
- The nose humidifies and warms air to approximately 37°C and enables it for gas exchange.
- Temperature regulation is performed by the turbinates inside the nasal cavity.
- In a cold environment, the turbinates enlarge to increase surface area and warm the cold air. Therefore, in colder environments people tend to have nasal blockage due to enlarged turbinates.
- In warm and humid environments, the turbinates shrink because the air is already warm.
- In a deviated nasal septum, the inferior turbinate enlarges on the opposite side of the deviation to compensate for the other side and to meet the increased space between the septum and lateral wall.

**Notes**
- Gas exchange is not done in the nose.
- Length of the nasal cavity: 5–5.5 cm (2–2.2 inches).

## Embryology of the Nose

- Development of the nose occurs between the 4th and 8th weeks of gestation.
- Ectoderm around the frontal eminence invaginates and forms the nasal placode; nasal pits develop from it.
- The nasal pits approach each other in the midline and fuse to form the nasal septum and nasal cavity.
- The nasobuccal membrane is present posteriorly and divides the nasal cavity from the pharynx. This membrane disappears at birth. If it does not disappear at birth, it leads to choanal atresia.

## External Nose

- Parts of the external nose are the dorsum, tip and bridge.
- **Rhinoplasty:** corrective surgery of the external nose.
- **Endoscopic sinus surgery:** surgery to correct internal parts of the nose.
- **Nasion:** point where the frontal bone connects with the nasal bone.
- **Rhinion:** point where the nasal bone connects with the upper lateral cartilage of the nose.
- The external nose is made of nasal bone and cartilage.

### Cartilages of the Nose

- **Paired:**
  - Upper lateral cartilage
  - Lower lateral cartilage (greater alar cartilage)
  - Lesser (minor) alar cartilage (sesamoid cartilage)
- **Unpaired:** septal cartilage.

![[images/nasal-cartilages-and-valves.png]]

### Nasal Valve and Vestibule

- The junction between the upper lateral cartilage and lower lateral cartilage forms the **internal nasal valve**, also called the **limen nasi**. It is the narrowest part of the nasal cavity.

**Boundaries of the limen nasi**
1. **Medial wall:** nasal septum.
2. **Lateral wall:** anterior part of the inferior turbinate, fibrofatty tissue and alar cartilage.

- The **external nasal valve** is present at the entrance of the nasal cavity.
- The space between the external and internal nasal valves is called the **vestibule**.
- The vestibule is part of the “dangerous area” of the nose. Ophthalmic veins drain into the vestibule. Infection or inflammation of the vestibule (**vestibulitis**) can spread to the cavernous sinus and cause cavernous sinus thrombosis.

![[images/cottles-test.png]]

### Cottle’s Test

- Used to check the function of the nasal valve.
- Hold the side of the nose and pull it laterally. If breathing improves, the nasal valve is not functioning properly.

## Nasal Mucosa and Mucociliary Clearance

- The lining epithelium of the nose is **pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium**.
- It drains secretions towards the nasopharynx.
- Approximately **1–1.5 L of nasal secretions are produced in 24 hours** to lubricate the nasal cavity and humidify the air.

![[images/nasal-stratified-squamous-epithelium.png]]

![[images/respiratory-epithelium-cilia-and-goblet-cells.png]]

There are two mucus layers:

1. **Gel layer**
   - A sticky layer exposed to air.
   - Nanoparticles, microparticles, dust and allergens in inspired air become trapped in the gel layer, making it thicker.
2. **Sol layer**
   - A non-sticky solution layer present below the gel layer.

**Conveyor-belt mechanism:** cilia move towards the nasopharynx and push the gel layer. The cilia then bend and move down into the sol layer, where the stickiness goes away. The cilia re-enter the gel layer and push it towards the nasopharynx. This continuous movement is called the conveyor-belt mechanism.

- During dehydration, the solution in the sol layer is lost and only the gel layer remains. The cilia cannot work properly, become stuck, and the material comes out as a dry, sticky crust.
- In heat, the crust expands and causes bleeding. Therefore, dryness in the nose is described as the most common cause of epistaxis.
- Saline nose drops or salt-water drops are used to restore the sol layer.

## Nasal Septum

### Septal Anatomy and Blood Supply

- **Anterior two-thirds:** cartilage (septal cartilage).
- **Posterior one-third:** bone (perpendicular plate of the ethmoid and vomer).

![[images/nasal-septum-anatomy-cutaway.png]]

**Blood supply of the septum**

- Anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries: branches of the ophthalmic artery.
- Sphenopalatine and greater palatine arteries: branches of the maxillary artery; these supply the majority of the nasal septum.
- Superior labial artery: branch of the facial artery; supplies the front part of the nasal cavity.
- Together, these arteries form **Kiesselbach’s plexus (Little’s area)**.

### Lateral Wall of the Nose

- The lateral wall of the nose has three bony projections called **conchae**. The bony part is covered by mucosa; concha and mucosa together are called **turbinates**.
- The three turbinates are:
  1. Superior turbinate
  2. Middle turbinate
  3. Inferior turbinate (the longest turbinate)
- The superior and middle turbinates are projections of the ethmoid bone.
- The inferior turbinate is a separate bone. It is part of the maxillary bone.
- Very rarely, a fourth turbinate called the **supreme turbinate** is seen.

![[images/nasal-cavity-and-turbinates.png]]

**Meatus:** space under the turbinates.

1. Superior meatus
2. Middle meatus
3. Inferior meatus

**Nasolacrimal duct**

- Connects the lacrimal sac to the nasal cavity.
- Opens into the inferior meatus.
- Has a **valve of Hasner**.
- Drains excess tears into the nasal cavity.

![[images/lateral-nasal-wall-anatomy.png]]

### Osteomeatal Complex and Sinus Drainage

- The ethmoidal, frontal and maxillary sinuses open into a small area in the middle meatus called the **osteomeatal complex (OMC)**.
- The posterior ethmoidal sinus opens in the superior meatus.
- The sphenoid sinus opens in the sphenoethmoidal recess.
- The largest first ethmoidal cell is called the **bulla ethmoidalis**. Beside it is a plate of bone called the **uncinate process**, described as perpendicular.
- Air enters the sinuses during expiration; air hits the uncinate process and creates eddy currents.

**Components of the osteomeatal complex**
1. Uncinate process
2. Bulla ethmoidalis
3. Infundibulum (funnel-shaped space between the uncinate process and bulla ethmoidalis)
4. Middle meatus

- A curve-like structure called the **hiatus semilunaris** is present between the bulla ethmoidalis and middle turbinate. It is not a part of the osteomeatal complex.
- About 40% of the population has the first ethmoidal cell called **agger nasi**.
- In dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR), access to the nasolacrimal duct is gained through the agger nasi via a Kerrison’s punch.

![[images/osteomeatal-complex-anatomy.png]]

![[images/nasal-cavity-sagittal-section.png]]

![[images/paranasal-sinus-location-face.png]]

Labels shown in the sinus-drainage figures include the frontal sinus, posterior ethmoid cell, sphenoid sinus, middle turbinate, ethmoid bulla, uncinate process, anterior and posterior ethmoid cells, agger nasi, nasolacrimal duct, superior and middle meatuses, basal lamina, unciform groove (semilunar hiatus), maxillary sinus and inferior turbinate.

- **Concha bullosa:** an air pocket in the middle turbinate. It blocks sinus drainage and air passage and causes sinusitis.
- **Haller cells** are connected to the lamina papyracea of the ethmoid; **Onodi cells** are present in the sphenoid.

## Paranasal Sinuses

There are four paranasal sinuses:

1. Frontal sinus
2. Maxillary sinus
3. Ethmoidal sinus
4. Sphenoid sinus

- **Sinus:** blind pouch.
- **Fistula:** opens on both sides.
- All sinuses are present at birth except the frontal sinus. The frontal sinus becomes clinically evident at 6–7 years of age and has a volume of 7 mL.
- The sphenoid sinus is the last sinus to develop fully, at 15 years of age.
- The maxillary sinus is the largest sinus. It has a volume of 15 mL and is called the **sinus of Highmore** or **maxillary antrum**.
- The ethmoidal sinus is a grape-like cluster.
- The sphenoid sinus opens in the sphenoethmoidal recess. Issues in the sphenoid sinus can directly affect the pituitary. In skull-base surgery, access to the pituitary for a pituitary adenoma is gained through the sphenoid sinus.

![[images/paranasal-sinus-anatomy-side-view.png]]

![[images/paranasal-sinus-axial-imaging.png]]

### Sinusitis and Pain Patterns

- Air enters the sinuses during expiration and each sinus has a programmed ciliary pathway.
- When sinuses become blocked and sinusitis occurs, they cause pain.
- **Frontal sinusitis:** forehead headache/“office headache”.
- **Maxillary sinusitis:** cheekbone pain/“morning headache” and tooth pain, because the maxillary sinus lies over the second premolar and first molar.
- **Ethmoidal sinusitis:** pain with eye movement.

## Anatomy and Physiology of the Nasopharynx and Eustachian Tube

### Nasopharynx Anatomy

- The nasopharynx is the part of the pharynx behind the nasal cavity or choana.
- **Boundaries of the nasopharynx:**
  - Superior: base of the skull.
  - Inferior: superior border of the hard palate.
- There is an opening of the Eustachian tube on both sides of the nasopharynx.
- **Torus tubarius:** bulge around the opening of the Eustachian tube, produced by the muscle supplying the tube.
- **Fossa of Rosenmüller:** depression behind the torus tubarius.
- Nasopharyngeal carcinoma originates from the fossa of Rosenmüller.
- The space between the skull base and the superior constrictor is called the **sinus of Morgagni**.
- When nasopharyngeal carcinoma invades the sinus of Morgagni, it produces a triad of symptoms known as **Trotter’s triad**.

![[images/nasopharynx-and-eustachian-tube-anatomy.png]]

**Trotter’s triad**
1. Ipsilateral conductive hearing loss
2. Ipsilateral palatal palsy
3. Temporoparietal neuralgia

### Eustachian Tube Physiology

**Functions**
1. Ventilation of the middle ear.
2. Drainage of secretions from the middle ear.

- The Eustachian tube is a patulous tube, so it is normally closed. It opens when the salpingopharyngeus muscle contracts.
- The levator veli palatini and tensor veli palatini elevate the Eustachian tube.
- The levator veli palatini and tensor veli palatini are supplied by the trigeminal nerve (mandibular V3 and maxillary V2 divisions).

![[images/eustachian-tube-anatomy.png]]

### Eustachian Tube Anatomy

- The Eustachian tube is 36 mm long.
- **Medial two-thirds:** cartilaginous (24 mm).
- **Lateral one-third:** bony (12 mm).
- In adults it makes an angle of 45°. In children the angle is around 0°, which is why children are more prone to middle-ear infection.
- In chronic Eustachian tube blockage or inflammation, **Eustachian tuboplasty** is performed. Balloon dilation is used to reverse chronic inflammation and is based on microscopic pressure necrosis of the Eustachian tube mucosa.

### Tympanic Membrane Retraction and Eustachian Tube Function Tests

- Tympanic membrane retraction occurs when the Eustachian tube is blocked due to negative pressure.

![[images/tympanic-membrane-endoscopic-view-one.png]]

![[images/tympanic-membrane-endoscopic-view-two.png]]

![[images/tympanic-membrane-endoscopic-view-three.png]]

**Grades of tympanic membrane retraction**

1. **Grade 1:** slight retraction onto the handle of the malleus and distortion of the cone of light.
2. **Grade 2:** retraction over the incudomalleal joint.
3. **Grade 3:** retraction over the incudostapedial joint.
4. **Grade 4:** retraction over the promontory.
5. **Grade 5:** atelectasis.

### Tympanometry

![[images/tympanometry-principle.png]]

- Tympanometry or audiometry is used to check middle-ear pressure and evaluate Eustachian tube dysfunction.
- A **Type C curve** on tympanometry confirms negative pressure in the middle ear due to Eustachian tube obstruction.
- Other treatment options or Eustachian tube function tests include the **Valsalva** and **Toynbee** manoeuvres. Both manoeuvres are subjective.
- Pure-tone audiometry shows mild to moderate conductive hearing loss.

| Tympanogram type | Readings | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Type A** | Middle-ear pressure (MEP): −100 to +100 daPa; compliance: 0.3–1.6 mL; ear-canal volume: adults 1.0–1.5 mL, children 0.7–1.0 mL | Normal |
| **Type B** | MEP: not definable; compliance: less than 0.3 mL. Ear-canal volume may be smaller than normal, within the normal range, or significantly larger than normal (>2.5 mL in adults; >2.0 mL in children) | Smaller volume: wax; normal volume: middle-ear effusion; significantly larger volume: perforation or grommet |
| **Type C** | MEP: more negative than −100 daPa; compliance: normal; ear-canal volume: normal | Eustachian tube obstruction |
| **Type Ad** | MEP: normal; compliance: greater than 1.6 mL; ear-canal volume: normal | Healing perforation; possible ossicular-chain discontinuity; atrophy of the eardrum |
| **Type As** | MEP: normal; compliance: less than 30 mL; ear-canal volume: normal | Scarring or adhesions; possible ossicular fixation |

| Septoplasty | Submucosal resection (SMR) |
|---|---|
| Only cartilage is removed. | Bone and cartilage are removed. |
| Freer’s incision is given. | Killian’s incision is given. |
| Flap is raised on one side. | Flaps are raised on both sides. |

- Inferior turbinate hypertrophy.
- Infection and saddle-nose deformity.

## Epistaxis

Bleeding from the nose is called **epistaxis** (epi = up; taxis = bleeding).

### Blood Supply of the Septum

- **Internal carotid artery → ophthalmic artery → anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries.**
- **External carotid artery:**
  1. Facial artery → superior labial artery (septal branch).
  2. Maxillary artery → sphenopalatine artery and greater palatine artery.
- All these arteries together form **Kiesselbach’s plexus**, also called **Little’s area**. The posterior ethmoidal artery is a watershed area and makes only a very small contribution to Kiesselbach’s plexus.
- Anterior bleeding occurs largely because of Kiesselbach’s plexus.
- Posterior bleeding is described as mostly due to the sphenopalatine vein, which forms **Woodruff’s plexus**.

![[images/nasal-septal-blood-supply.png]]

### Types and Aetiology of Epistaxis

| Feature | Anterior epistaxis | Posterior epistaxis |
|---|---|---|
| Amount | Mild bleeding (10–30 mL) | Profuse bleeding |
| Source | Kiesselbach’s plexus | Woodruff’s plexus |
| Vessel type | Arterial bleed | Venous bleed |
| Causes | Dryness, trauma, dehydration | Hypertension |
| Other feature | Anterior bleeding | Posterior drip into the mouth leads to cough |
| Treatment | Anterior packing, Trotter’s method | Posterior packing |

![[images/epistaxis-endoscopic-bleeding-site.png]]

![[images/nasal-mucosa-vessel-cautery-views.png]]

### Management of Epistaxis

- First aid for epistaxis is **Trotter’s method** or an ice pack.
- **Trotter’s method:** bend forward and pinch the nose firmly for 10 minutes.
- Bending backwards to stop bleeding is not the recommended method because blood is swallowed. When blood mixes with acid in the stomach, it forms **acid haematin**, described in the notes as corrosive. Acid haematin is associated in the notes with gastric/peptic reactions such as nausea, vomiting, ulcers and perforation.
- Trotter’s method works only in anterior epistaxis. It does not work in posterior epistaxis or in systemic disease in which bleeding factors or the coagulation cascade are not functioning.

![[images/trotters-method-for-epistaxis.png]]

- If bleeding is profuse, the next line of management is anterior or posterior nasal packing, depending on the cause.
- For anterior epistaxis, anterior nasal packing is performed with a roller gauze using Tilley’s packing forceps or a Netcell/Merocel pack.

![[images/anterior-and-posterior-nasal-packing.png]]

- For posterior epistaxis, posterior nasal packing is performed with a **Foley catheter**. Inflate the balloon of the Foley catheter and tug it.

![[images/foley-catheter-posterior-packing-position.png]]

![[images/foley-catheter-posterior-packing-inflated.png]]

- If bleeding does not stop, perform electrical bipolar cautery or chemical cautery with AgNO₃ (silver nitrate) of Little’s area, or ligate the sphenopalatine artery.

- In recurrent epistaxis, evaluate for deficiency of clotting factors, haemophilia, thalassaemia, another bleeding disorder or liver failure.
- Check bleeding time (BT), clotting time, PT, aPTT and INR.

## Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma and Carcinoma

### Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma (JNA)

**Introduction**

- Benign tumour.
- Most common in young males aged 14–16 years.
- Cell of origin: myofibroblast.
- Described as exclusively seen in adolescent males and androgen-dependent.
- In females, the notes state that it spreads first in the maxillary sinus.
- Usually unilateral.
- Originates from the upper lip of the sphenopalatine foramen.
- Most common blood supply: sphenopalatine artery or internal maxillary artery.

**Symptoms**

- Profuse, recurrent epistaxis.
- Swelling in the cheeks.

**On examination**

- Reddish polypoid mass.
- **FROG:** frog-face deformity.

![[images/frog-face-deformity-jna.png]]

**Diagnosis**

- **CT scan:** anterior bowing of the posterior maxillary wall (**Holman–Miller sign**).
- **Nasal endoscopy:** red, beefy appearance.
- Angiography of the maxillary vessels.
- Labels shown in the source figure: SS, MA, SPF and SPA.

![[images/jna-imaging-and-staging.png]]

### Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC)

- Age: 50–60 years; it may also occur at a young age.
- Squamous cell carcinoma.
- The notes state that 85% of NPC is linked to tobacco use (smoking/cigarettes).
- Lymph-node enlargement.
- Originates from the upper part of the Eustachian tube region (fossa of Rosenmüller).

**Trotter’s triad (sinus of Morgagni)**

1. Ipsilateral trigeminal neuralgia.
2. Ipsilateral palatal palsy.
3. Unilateral SOM.

- **Investigation of choice (IOC):** endoscopic biopsy.
- **Treatment:** radiation therapy and chemotherapy (cisplatin, 5-FU).

### JNA versus Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma

| Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma | Juvenile nasopharyngeal carcinoma |
|---|---|
| Adolescent (around 14 years of age) | Bimodal (50–70 years of age) |
| Frog face | Trotter’s triad |
| Holman–Miller sign | Fossa of Rosenmüller |
| Originates from the sphenopalatine foramen | Fossa of Rosenmüller |
| Treatment: surgery | Treatment: radiotherapy and chemotherapy |
