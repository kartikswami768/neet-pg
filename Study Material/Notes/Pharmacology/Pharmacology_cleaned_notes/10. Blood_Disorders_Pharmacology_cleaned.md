# Blood Disorders - Pharmacology Study Document
# 1. ANTIPLATELET DRUGS
## 1.1 Clinical indications
Antiplatelet drugs are used when excessive platelet deposition occurs in blood vessels.
| Location | Disease/example |
|---|---|
| Coronary artery | MI (myocardial infarction) |
| Brain/cerebral artery | Ischemic stroke |
| Limb arteries/leg arteries | PVD (peripheral vascular disease), including Buerger disease |
**Clinical distinction:** Platelet-rich **arterial** thrombosis is the central pharmacologic setting for antiplatelet therapy. Venous thromboembolism is primarily a coagulation/fibrin-driven process and is treated with anticoagulation.
## 1.2 Physiology of platelet attachment, recruitment and aggregation
The first page contains a platelet diagram linking COX-1/TXA2, ADP/P2Y12, cAMP and GP receptors. The logical sequence is:
```text
Platelet adhesion/attachment
 |
 | COX-1 in platelets → thromboxane A2 (TXA2)
 v
Platelet activation / recruitment
 |
 | ADP → P2Y12 receptor
 | cAMP opposes platelet activation/recruitment
 v
Platelet aggregation
 |
 | GP IIb/IIIa activated
 | fibrinogen cross-links adjacent platelets
 v
Stable platelet aggregate / primary hemostatic plug
```
### The three steps presented
### Step 1 - Platelet attachment
- COX-1 in platelets produces **TXA2**.
- TXA2 promotes platelet activation/attachment.
The earliest adhesion of circulating platelets to exposed subendothelial matrix is classically mediated by **GP Ib-IX-V binding von Willebrand factor (vWF)**. TXA2 is an important platelet agonist/amplifier but is not the sole molecular mechanism of initial adhesion.
### Step 2 - Platelet recruitment
- Activated platelets release **ADP**.
- ADP acts on platelet **P2Y12 receptors**.
- This recruits/activates additional platelets.
- **cAMP inhibits platelet activation/recruitment** simplified schema.
### Step 3 - Platelet aggregation
- Recruited platelets are cross-linked by a fibrin-like "cement".
- The labels the relevant receptor as **GP 2B3A**.
The standard terminology is **glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GP IIb/IIIa; integrin alphaIIb beta3)**. It binds **fibrinogen** (and also vWF under some conditions), allowing platelet-to-platelet cross-linking.
## 1.3 Classification of antiplatelet drugs
| Class | Key action | Drugs |
|---|---|---|
| Irreversible COX-1 inhibitor | ↓ TXA2 synthesis | **Aspirin** |
| P2Y12 (ADP receptor) blockers | ↓ platelet recruitment/activation | Ticlopidine, clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, cangrelor |
| PDE-3 inhibitors | ↑ intracellular cAMP; inhibits platelet activation | Pentoxifylline, dipyridamole, cilostazol |
| GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors | Inhibit platelet aggregation | Abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban |
| Other/newer agents | Other platelet activation pathways ↓orapaxar; dazoxiben is mentioned as investigational |
## 1.4 Aspirin
### Mechanism
- Irreversible inhibitor of **cyclooxygenase (COX)**.
- Described as a **"suicide inhibitor" / "hit-and-run" drug**.
- ↓ platelet TXA2 production → ↓ platelet activation/attachment.
- Because the platelet cannot synthesize new COX-1, the effect persists for the platelet lifespan.
### Clinical use
- Presented as an overall drug of choice for MI, stroke and Buerger disease/PVD.
- Antiplatelet dose range : **42-325 mg**.
Current antiplatelet use depends on the clinical syndrome and treatment phase. Aspirin remains a foundational antiplatelet agent in acute coronary syndromes and in secondary prevention, but modern ACS practice commonly uses **dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)** with aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor when indicated. Modern stroke care also uses short-course DAPT in selected minor stroke/TIA scenarios rather than aspirin alone in every patient. See the current ACS and stroke references in Appendix C.
### Dosing table preserved
| Setting | dose | duration |
|---|---:|---|
| Acute MI / acute stroke | **325 mg orally** | Single dose |
| Secondary prophylaxis | **75 mg**, may be increased up to 100 mg | Lifelong |
These doses should not be read as universal dosing instructions for every contemporary stroke/ACS scenario; clinical dosing depends on the specific indication.
### Adverse effects
- Increased bleeding time/bleeding risk.
- Peptic ulcer/gastrointestinal injury.
### Aspirin-associated peptic ulcer management
- Drug of choice listed: **proton-pump inhibitor (PPI)** such as omeprazole or pantoprazole.
- Alternative listed: **misoprostol**.
### Surgery
- "Stop aspirin 7 days before major surgery."
- Rationale given: platelet lifespan about 7 days and irreversible COX-1 inhibition.
A fixed 7-day stop rule is not appropriate for every patient. Perioperative aspirin decisions depend on the indication for aspirin, bleeding risk procedure, cardiovascular risk of interruption, and whether aspirin is being used for secondary prevention or another reason. Platelet function recovery follows new platelet production over several days, but whether aspirin should be stopped is a clinical risk-benefit decision.
### Breastfeeding / Reye syndrome - correction of a error
that aspirin is "absolutely contraindicated" during lactation because of risk of liver damage/Reye syndrome neonate.
This is too absolute and conflates two different issues. Current LactMed information states that **low-dose aspirin (75-325 mg/day) produces low salicylate levels in breast milk**, although monitoring for bruising/bleeding is advised if it is used. Continuous high-dose maternal aspirin is less desirable. Reye syndrome is associated with **aspirin administration to infants/children with viral infections**; the risk from low-dose salicylate exposure through breast milk is unknown rather than established as a neonatal Reye syndrome mechanism.
## 1.5 P2Y12 (ADP receptor) blockers
### Mechanism
Inhibit platelet recruitment by blocking the platelet **P2Y12 ADP receptor**.
### Classification
| Type | Drugs | wording/notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Irreversible** | Ticlopidine, clopidogrel, prasugrel | Prodrug/inactive until metabolized (for these thienopyridines) |
| **Reversible** | Ticagrelor, cangrelor | Active drugs; not prodrugs |
### Indications and special points
| Drug | use | Important note |
|---|---|---|
| **Clopidogrel** | MI + stroke | Add-on to aspirin in DAPT; synergistic/additive effect |
| **Ticlopidine** | MI + stroke | Older P2Y12 drug |
| **Prasugrel** | "Only MI" | states contraindicated in stroke due to bleeding/ICH risk |
| **Ticagrelor** | "Only MI" | Reversible P2Y12 blocker |
| **Cangrelor** | IV only | Used around stent placement/PCI |
Contemporary indications are broader and depend on ACS, PCI, stroke/TIA subtype and bleeding risk. Prasugrel is specifically used in selected ACS patients undergoing PCI and is **contraindicated in patients with a prior stroke or TIA**. Ticagrelor has indications beyond MI alone, including other ACS settings and selected stroke/TIA strategies.
### Clopidogrel as a prodrug
- Clopidogrel is a **prodrug**.
- Requires conversion to its active metabolite via **CYP2C19**.
### PPI interaction
- Omeprazole is presented as the strongest CYP inhibitor and therefore an undesirable combination with clopidogrel.
- Rabeprazole is given as preferred; pantoprazole as second choice.
- is essentially: "Clopidogrel → avoid omeprazole."
The clinically important point is that **omeprazole and esomeprazole can inhibit CYP2C19 and reduce formation of clopidogrel's active metabolite**. When gastroprotection is necessary, a PPI with less CYP2C19 inhibition is commonly preferred. Do not interpret The rabeprazole/pantoprazole ordering as a universal guideline hierarchy.
### Adverse effects
- Common: diarrhea.
- Serious: immune-mediated thrombocytopenia/TTP .
- **Ticlopidine:** bone-marrow suppression/agranulocytosis.
**Clinical note:** Ticlopidine is now rarely used because of hematologic toxicity and safer alternatives.
## 1.6 Phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE-3) inhibitors
### Mechanism
- Inhibit platelet **PDE-3**.
- This increases **cAMP**.
- cAMP inhibits platelet activation/recruitment.
### Drugs / mnemonic
> **PDE-3 memory:**
> - **P** = Pentoxifylline
> - **D** = Dipyridamole
> - **E-3** = Cilostazol
### clinical framing
- Described as add-on drugs to aspirin.
- **Cilostazol:** specifically emphasized for Buerger disease/intermittent claudication.
- **Pentoxifylline:** described as a rheological agent intended to improve blood flow in limb arteries.
- Headache is listed as the most common adverse effect due to vasodilation.
- these agents are avoided in MI because PDE-3/cAMP is also relevant to cardiac rate and oxygen demand.
The generalized rule "only stroke/Buerger" and its blanket "absolute contraindication in MI" should not be applied as a universal clinical rule. **Cilostazol** is primarily used for intermittent claudication and is **contraindicated in heart failure** in US labeling because of increased mortality in patients with class III-IV heart failure. **Dipyridamole** has a separate role in stroke prevention, commonly in combination with aspirin. **Pentoxifylline** has limited contemporary evidence and is not a routine first-line drug for peripheral artery disease.
## 1.7 GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors
### Mechanism
- Also called anti-aggregating or anti-integrin drugs.
- Inhibit platelet aggregation by blocking **GP IIb/IIIa**.
### Drugs - mnemonic
> **E-A-T**
> - **E** = Eptifibatide
> - **A** = Abciximab
> - **T** = Tirofiban
### Use
- All three are IV drugs.
- Given during stent placement/PCI (PTCA ).
These drugs are highly selected adjuncts in PCI rather than routine therapy for every patient undergoing stent placement. Abciximab is a monoclonal antibody Fab fragment against GP IIb/IIIa; eptifibatide and tirofiban are smaller molecules.
### Abciximab special property
- Called a "renal safe" drug because it is a protein/peptide and not expected to be cleared by glomerular filtration same way as small molecules.
- also describes immunosuppressive effects through effects on leukocyte integrins.
Renal function still matters when choosing and dosing antiplatelet therapy, and the simple rule "all monoclonal antibodies are renal safe" is not a clinically reliable general pharmacology rule.
## 1.8 Newer antiplatelet drugs
### Vorapaxar
- **PAR-1 antagonist**.
- PAR = protease-activated receptor.
- Platelet PAR-1 activation promotes aggregation.
- Presented as an add-on to aspirin and described as approved for MI.
Vorapaxar is a **PAR-1 antagonist used to reduce thrombotic cardiovascular events in selected patients with a history of MI or peripheral arterial disease**. It is not an acute-MI treatment drug sense implied by The, and its use is limited by major bleeding risk, including intracranial hemorrhage; it is contraindicated in patients with a history of stroke/TIA/ICH in US labeling.
### Dazoxiben
- Thromboxane A2 synthase inhibitor.
- Presented as investigational/not currently approved.
# 2. ANTICOAGULANT DRUGS
## 2.1 Why anticoagulation is used
Anticoagulants are defined as drugs that prevent **fibrin deposition**.
The discusses:
- **DVT** → excessive fibrin deposition in leg veins.
- A detached venous thrombus can cause **pulmonary embolism (PE)**.
- Common provoking settings listed include immobility, ICU/unconscious state and recent knee/hip surgery.
- **Atrial fibrillation (AF) + mitral stenosis** → left-atrial thrombus → embolization → ischemic stroke/TIA.
- **DIC** → systemic activation of coagulation and fibrin deposition.
### DVT → PE flow
```text
Venous stasis / provoking factor
 → venous thrombus (fibrin-rich)
 → thrombus embolizes
 → pulmonary artery obstruction
 → pulmonary embolism
```
### AF + mitral stenosis → embolic stroke flow
```text
AF +/- significant mitral stenosis
 → ineffective atrial contraction / blood stasis
 → left atrial thrombus
 → embolization
 → cerebral arterial occlusion
 → ischemic stroke or TIA
```
that TIA represents reversible neuronal dysfunction/damage whereas stroke produces permanent neuronal injury.
Modern definitions are based on tissue injury and transient symptoms, not simply a blanket reversible-versus-permanent rule.
It advises ECG/echo evaluation in stroke patients to look for AF/mitral stenosis.
Cardiac rhythm evaluation is important when cardioembolic stroke is suspected, but the work-up is individualized and often includes prolonged rhythm monitoring; echocardiography is used when indicated by the clinical scenario.
## 2.2 Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
- Fibrin deposition occurs throughout the circulation.
- Triggers include activation coagulation pathway by systemic disease.
- Causes listed: sepsis (most common ), abruptio placentae, malignancy and mechanical/artificial valves.
- Source says DIC from sepsis/abruption/cancer is an emergency and gives IV heparin as the drug.
The central treatment principle in DIC is **rapid treatment underlying cause plus supportive blood-component therapy when bleeding is significant**. Therapeutic-dose heparin is **not routinely given to every patient with DIC**; it may be considered in selected patients when thrombosis predominates or when there is a specific thrombotic indication.
### Mechanical heart valve
- Lifelong oral anticoagulation.
- Drug named: **warfarin**.
Mechanical prosthetic valves require **lifelong vitamin K antagonist anticoagulation** unless an individualized specialist plan dictates otherwise. Direct oral anticoagulants should not replace warfarin for mechanical valves.

factor Xa as the key step converting prothrombin (factor II) to thrombin (IIa), followed by fibrin formation.
## 2.4 Classification of anticoagulants
### Oral anticoagulants
1. **Vitamin K antagonist (VKA):** warfarin, acenocoumarol, dicoumarol/bishydroxycoumarin.
2. **Direct factor Xa inhibitors:** rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban (and other drugs in this class exist).
3. **Direct thrombin inhibitor:** dabigatran.
### Parenteral anticoagulants
1. **Indirect thrombin inhibition via antithrombin:** heparins.
2. **Direct thrombin inhibitors:** argatroban, bivalirudin and other historical/less commonly used agents.
3. **Indirect factor Xa inhibitor:** fondaparinux.

# 4. WARFARIN
## 4.1 Basic identity
- Warfarin is a **coumarin derivative**.
- Historical mnemonic: **WAR-F** = Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation.
- "-arin" suggests a coumarin-derived drug.
- Historically developed from compounds related to rodenticide use.
## 4.2 Mechanism of action
- Acts liver.
- Inhibits **VKORC1** (vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1).
- This reduces regeneration of active vitamin K.
- Production of vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors falls.
```text
Warfarin
 |
 v
Inhibits VKORC1
 |
 v
Less reduced/active vitamin K
 |
 v
Reduced gamma-carboxylation / functional synthesis of
Factors II, VII, IX, X + Protein C + Protein S
 |
 v
Anticoagulant effect
```
### Factor effects
- Clotting factors reduced: **II, VII, IX, X**.
- Anticoagulant proteins reduced: **Protein C and Protein S**.
## 4.3 Sequential reduction and the paradoxical early phase
1. Factor VII falls first.
2. Protein C falls next.
3. Factor II (thrombin) falls last.
4. Full anticoagulant effect takes about **5 days**.
This underlies the classic early **hypercoagulable/paradoxical phase**.
```text
Start warfarin
 |
 +-→ Factor VII falls quickly → PT/INR changes
 |
 +-→ Protein C falls → temporary loss of anticoagulant protection
 |
 +-→ Factors II/other clotting factors decline more slowly
 |
 +-→ Full therapeutic anticoagulation develops over several days
```
### Complication
- **Dermal vascular necrosis / limb necrosis**.
- **Purple toe syndrome** is also mentioned.
### Prevention / bridging - correction of oversimplification
- Combine warfarin with heparin for the initial 5 days.
- Stop heparin after 5 days.
Heparin overlap is classically used when initiating warfarin for **acute VTE**, but routine heparin bridging is **not required for every patient starting warfarin for every indication**. Bridging decisions are indication- and thrombosis-risk-specific.
## 4.4 Pharmacokinetics
### Protein binding / volume of distribution
- Very low volume of distribution.
- Approximately **99% albumin bound**.
- Extensive protein binding contributes to poor removal by dialysis.
### Enantiomers
- Warfarin is a racemic mixture of R- and S-warfarin.
- S-warfarin is described as the active form and R-warfarin as inactive.
**Both R- and S-warfarin are pharmacologically active**, but **S-warfarin is more potent** and is primarily metabolized by CYP2C9.
### Metabolism
- Warfarin metabolism is associated with **CYP2C9**.
- The links variability to CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genetics.
CYP2C9 genotype and VKORC1 genotype can influence warfarin dose requirements, but routine pre-treatment genotyping is not universally required in clinical practice.
## 4.5 Monitoring warfarin
### Test
- **PT with INR**.
- PT = prothrombin time.
- INR = international normalized ratio.
Simplified formula :
```text
INR ~ Patient PT / Control PT
```
Actual INR incorporates the reagent's **ISI (international sensitivity index)**; the simple ratio is a teaching approximation, not the exact formula used by laboratories.
### INR values
| Situation | INR |
|---|---:|
| Normal, no anticoagulant | ~1.0 |
| normal range | 0.8-1.1 |
| Common therapeutic target | **2.0-3.0** |
INR targets vary by indication. 2.0-3.0 is common for AF and VTE, but some mechanical valves require a higher target depending on valve type and patient factors.
## 4.6 Warfarin interactions
### table - preserved
| Factor               | examples                    | effect on INR         | Better current interpretation                                                                                                               |
| -------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| CYP inhibitors       | Ciprofloxacin, ketoconazole | Increase INR          | Many CYP inhibitors can increase warfarin exposure; effect is drug-specific.                                                                |
| CYP inducers         | Rifampicin                  | Decrease INR          | Correct direction: rifampicin strongly induces metabolism and can reduce effect.                                                            |
| Vitamin K deficiency | Antibiotics, aspirin        | Increase INR          | Antibiotics can increase INR through several mechanisms; aspirin primarily increases **bleeding risk** rather than predictably raising INR. |
| Liver disease        | -                           | Increase INR/toxicity | Can reduce clotting-factor synthesis and alter warfarin handling; clinical context matters.                                                 |
| Estrogens/OCPs       | -                           | says lower INR        | Estrogen-containing therapy can alter thrombotic/bleeding balance; INR direction is not a universal rule.                                   |
| Kidney disease       | Albumin loss                | says lower INR        | Hypoalbuminemia can change free warfarin and bleeding risk; INR response is not reliably predictable from albumin loss alone.               |
### High-yield warfarin interaction principles
- **Rifampicin → decreased warfarin effect.**
- Many CYP inhibitors and interacting antibiotics → **increased INR**.
- Changes in vitamin K intake can alter INR.
- Antiplatelet drugs/NSAIDs may increase bleeding **without needing to raise INR**.
- Always interpret interaction clinical context rather than memorizing a one-direction table.
## 4.7 Management of raised INR / warfarin overdose
### Scenario 1 - raised INR without bleeding
| INR | action |
|---|---|
| **3-10** | Stop warfarin; no other treatment |
| **>10** | Oral vitamin K **2.5-5 mg** |
Exact management depends on the actual INR, bleeding status, thrombotic risk and dose history. The simplified thresholds are useful for exam revision but should not replace a contemporary reversal protocol.
### Scenario 2 - raised INR with clinically significant bleeding
- IV vitamin K.
- Immediate replacement of clotting factors:
 - **4-factor PCC** = preferred .
 - **FFP** if PCC unavailable.
### Why 4-factor PCC is important
It contains the vitamin-K-dependent coagulation factors:
- II
- VII
- IX
- X
**Key principle:** Vitamin K restores the body's own factor synthesis but **does not act instantly**; PCC provides immediate factor replacement.

# 6. PARENTERAL ANTICOAGULANTS
## 6.1 Direct thrombin inhibitors
### classification
Drugs ending in **"-rudin"
- Hirudin
- Lepirudin
- Desirudin
- Bivalirudin
- Argatroban (does not follow the suffix mnemonic)
### Administration/monitoring
- IV administration.
- aPTT monitoring for the older parenteral direct thrombin inhibitor framework.
- Indication emphasized: use when **heparin cannot be given**, especially heparin allergy/HIT.
Current HIT management recommends discontinuing heparin and using a **non-heparin anticoagulant**. Options include argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, danaparoid and selected DOACs. Argatroban and bivalirudin are particularly useful when a short-acting parenteral agent is desired.
### HIT
> **HIT = heparin-induced thrombocytopenia**
HIT is a **prothrombotic** immune-mediated adverse drug reaction, usually involving antibodies to PF4-heparin complexes.
The names hirudin/lepirudin/argatroban as drugs of choice for HIT and says there is no antidote for hirudin/lepirudin.
In current practice, the specific agent depends on availability, organ function, clinical stability and bleeding/procedure risk. **Argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux and selected DOACs** are accepted options in contemporary ASH guidance.
# 7. HEPARINS
## 7.1 Core mechanism
- Heparin activates **antithrombin (AT)**.
- Antithrombin inhibits thrombin and other activated clotting factors.
- Therefore heparin is described as an **indirect thrombin inhibitor**.
### Types of heparin
| Feature | UFH / high-MW heparin | LMWH | Synthetic pentasaccharide/ULMW group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | Unfractionated heparin | Enoxaparin, dalteparin, tinzaparin | Fondaparinux; idraparinux is historical/investigational context |
| Predominant activity | Anti-Xa + Anti-IIa | Anti-Xa > Anti-IIa | Anti-Xa only |
| Typical route | IV (also SC in some settings) | SC | SC |
| source | Animal-derived heparin preparations | Animal-derived LMWHs | Synthetic |
| Monitoring | aPTT commonly used for therapeutic UFH | Routine aPTT generally not useful | No routine aPTT |
| HIT risk | Yes | Yes, lower than UFH | Very low/none compared with heparins |
| Clearance | RES plus other pathways | Predominantly renal | Renal |
| Antidote | Protamine | Protamine gives **partial** reversal | No fully established specific antidote |
**Terminology note:** Fondaparinux is a **synthetic selective indirect factor Xa inhibitor**, not simply a chemically smaller heparin molecule.
## 7.2 UFH vs LMWH - exam table, corrected
| Feature | UFH | LMWH |
|---|---|---|
| aPTT monitoring | **Required for therapeutic IV UFH** traditional protocol | **Not routinely required** |
| HIT | Risk present | Risk present, generally lower |
| Renal impairment | Often preferred when rapid reversibility/independence from renal clearance is important | Dose adjustment/caution required in renal impairment |
| Antidote | Protamine | Protamine gives incomplete/partial reversal |
| Typical route | IV for therapeutic inpatient anticoagulation | SC |
### "Which heparin is not excreted in urine?"
UFH/high-MW heparin.
### "Which heparin is safest in severe renal failure?"
UFH/high-MW heparin.
The practical principle is sound: **UFH is often preferred when renal clearance is severely impaired** because it is less dependent on renal elimination and is rapidly reversible. This does not make it universally "renal safe" in every circumstance.
# 8. ANTICOAGULATION IN PREGNANCY
## 8.1 General principle
Heparins are presented as the safest anticoagulants in pregnancy, with a preference for LMWH/ULMW preparations.
For **acute VTE in pregnancy, LMWH is preferred over UFH** by ASH guidance. DOACs are generally avoided in pregnancy because of inadequate safety data and concerns about fetal exposure.
## 8.2 Warfarin and fetal effects
- Warfarin crosses the placenta.
- Vitamin K is important in fetal bone/cartilage formation.
- First-trimester exposure can cause **fetal warfarin syndrome / chondrodysplasia punctata**.
- Features listed:
 - Saddle nose.
 - Limb hypoplasia.
 - Cranial defects/microcephaly.
Warfarin embryopathy is particularly associated with exposure during the critical early gestational period, but fetal/neonatal bleeding and central nervous system abnormalities can occur with exposure later in pregnancy as well. Management of pregnancy in a patient who requires chronic anticoagulation - especially a patient with a **mechanical valve** - is highly individualized and cannot be reduced to a single universal week-by-week schedule.
## 8.3 Pregnancy protocol
| Time period | drug | notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0-12 weeks | Heparin injection only | Warfarin contraindicated |
| After 12 weeks | Warfarin can start/continue; heparin also listed | "Either option acceptable" |
| After 36 weeks | Stop warfarin; give heparin | Preparing for delivery |
| 1 day before delivery | Stop all anticoagulants | Prevent postpartum hemorrhage |
| Postpartum | Heparin | Heparin as drug of choice postpartum |

# 10. FIBRINOLYTIC / THROMBOLYTIC DRUGS
## 10.1 Difference from anticoagulants
- Anticoagulants **prevent further fibrin deposition**.
- Fibrinolytic/thrombolytic drugs **remove already deposited fibrin**.
### Core mechanism
```text
Plasminogen
 |
 | fibrinolytic activator
 v
Plasmin (active)
 |
 v
Fibrin degradation
 |
 v
Clot breakdown
```
## 10.2 Natural enzymes
### Streptokinase
- Produced by **group C beta-hemolytic streptococci**.
- Activates plasminogen.
- Antigenic: antibodies develop after exposure.
- Repeat administration may be less effective.
- the memory rule: **"streptokinase once in a lifetime."**
Streptokinase is now used far less commonly than recombinant/fibrin-specific agents in many modern systems.
### Urokinase

|---|---|---|

### Indications
- **ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)**
- **Acute ischemic stroke**
- **Massive pulmonary embolism / life-threatening PE**
### Acute ischemic stroke
Must be given within **4.5 hours** and is contraindicated in hemorrhagic stroke.
The 2026 AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline endorses **either alteplase 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg) or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg (max 25 mg)** for eligible adults within 4.5 hours. Selected patients with unknown onset or 4.5-9 hours may receive thrombolysis using advanced-imaging selection.
### STEMI
Fibrinolysis is discussed for STEMI and says it is contraindicated in non-STEMI.
Fibrinolysis is a reperfusion strategy for **selected STEMI patients when timely primary PCI cannot be achieved and no contraindication exists**. It is not a treatment for NSTEMI/unstable angina.
### Pulmonary embolism
Describes massive/saddle embolism with right-heart failure as a thrombolysis setting.
Systemic thrombolysis is mainly considered for **high-risk PE with hemodynamic instability or impending decompensation**. The anatomic description "saddle embolus" alone does not automatically mandate thrombolysis.
## 10.4 Major adverse effect
- Sudden removal of fibrin can cause serious/internal bleeding.
- Intracranial bleeding is particularly feared.
- The drugs are described as highly dangerous.
This is the central toxicity of thrombolytic therapy.
# 11. ANTIFIBRINOLYTIC DRUGS
### Drugs
- **Epsilon-aminocaproic acid (EACA) / aminocaproic acid**
- **Tranexamic acid (TXA)**
### Mechanism
They inhibit fibrinolysis by interfering with plasminogen/plasmin binding and activation at lysine sites, thereby stabilizing fibrin clots.
EACA is described as an "antidote" to fibrinolytics and TXA as useful to stop bleeding in:
- Menorrhagia/heavy menstrual bleeding.
- Dental bleeding.
- Other bleeding conditions.
There is no universal "antidote" to thrombolysis; antifibrinolytics can be used in selected severe bleeding scenarios as part of a broader reversal/supportive strategy. Tranexamic acid has well-established indications in trauma, postpartum hemorrhage and other bleeding contexts.
# 12. HYPOLIPIDEMIC DRUGS
## 12.1 Core lipid concepts
Major lipid disturbances can be divided into:
- **Cholesterol** → atherosclerosis → vessel occlusion → MI/stroke.
- **Triglycerides** → severe elevation can injure the pancreas → acute pancreatitis.

### 13.2 indications/examples
statins the drug of choice for hypercholesterolemia associated with:

- Nephrotic syndrome.
- Certain drugs.
- Alcohol use.
The contemporary indication is based on ASCVD risk, LDL-C level and specific conditions such as clinical ASCVD or familial hypercholesterolemia rather than a simple etiologic list.

#### CYP metabolism
- Most statins: CYP3A4.
- Exceptions highlighted:
	- Pravastatin: not metabolized by CYP.
	- Rosuvastatin: not metabolized by CYP.
	- Fluvastatin: CYP2C9.

The commonly tested broad pattern is:
- **CYP3A4:** simvastatin, lovastatin, ==atorvastatin==.
- **CYP2C9:** fluvastatin.
- **Minimal CYP metabolism:** pravastatin.
- **Rosuvastatin:** limited CYP metabolism, including CYP2C9/2C19 contributions, but substantially different CYP3A4 statins.
### 13.5 Adverse effects
- Hepatotoxicity/transaminase elevation.
- **Myopathy**, which may progress to **rhabdomyolysis**.
- Rhabdomyolysis clues:
	- Increased **CK/CPK**.
	- Myoglobin release.
	- **Myoglobinuria → cola-colored urine**.
#### Pregnancy
=="Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy."==
The ==FDA removed the former blanket pregnancy contraindication in 2021==, but **most pregnant patients should still discontinue statins**, with continued use reserved for selected very-high-risk situations after individualized specialist assessment. The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline also generally recommends avoiding statin/nonstatin lipid-lowering therapy during pregnancy/lactation except when the clinical situation justifies an exception.

## 16. NIACIN (NICOTINIC ACID / VITAMIN B3)
### 16.1 Mechanism/effects

- Lowers lipoprotein(a) .
- Hypolipidemic doses are much higher than nutritional vitamin doses; gives **2-6 g/day**.
### 16.2 Adverse effects

# 18. IRON PREPARATIONS
## 18.1 Indication
Iron preparations are used for **iron-deficiency anemia (IDA)**.
**oral ferrous sulfate** the most commonly used standard preparation.
The 2024 AGA Clinical Practice Update states that no single oral iron formulation has a proven general advantage over others, while **ferrous sulfate is preferred because it is inexpensive**.
## 18.2 Oral iron preparations
| Preparation | special property |
|---|---|
| **Ferrous sulfate** | Standard / common |
| **Ferrous gluconate** | - |
| **Ferrous fumarate** | "Maximum iron content" |
| **Ferrous ascorbate** | Contains vitamin C; called "best absorption" in |
Elemental iron content varies by formulation, so the clinically relevant number is the amount of **elemental iron**, not simply tablet mass. There is not enough evidence to call ferrous ascorbate universally "best absorbed".
## 18.3 Factors affecting oral iron absorption
### Increase absorption
- Acidic environment.
- Empty stomach.
- Vitamin C/ascorbic acid (lemon juice, amla examples).
### Decrease absorption
- Food.
- Tea/coffee.
- PPIs/antacids by reducing gastric acidity.
- Tetracyclines through chelation.
### Current practical dosing point
AGA 2024 advises oral iron **once daily at most**; **every-other-day dosing may be better tolerated** for some patients while maintaining good absorption. Vitamin C can be added to improve absorption.
## 18.4 Adverse effects of oral iron
- **Nausea/vomiting** - listed as most common.
- **Black stools**.
- If oral iron is not tolerated → consider parenteral iron.
Other common oral-iron problems include abdominal discomfort and constipation/diarrhea depending on formulation.
# 19. PARENTERAL / IV IRON
## 19.1 Iron dextran
- Iron dextran can be given IV and IM.
- Allergy/anaphylaxis risk.
- "Mandatory" allergen testing before use.
- Z-track/Z-technique for IM administration.
Modern IV iron practice is broader than iron dextran alone. Common modern preparations include iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose, ferumoxytol and iron dextran. **Routine mandatory test dosing is not a universal current requirement for all iron dextran products**, and true anaphylaxis to IV iron is rare. ### When to use IV iron
AGA 2024 recommends IV iron when:
- Oral iron is not tolerated.
- Ferritin does not improve adequately with oral therapy.
- Oral iron is unlikely to be absorbed.
- A rapid or more reliable replacement strategy is needed in an appropriate clinical setting.
## 19.2 Earliest response to iron therapy
- Hemoglobin does not rise immediately.
- The **earliest response is an increase in reticulocyte count**.
This is a useful hematology exam pearl.

**OCR/visual correction:** The OCR renders the macrophage receptor as **"FYC"**. The biologically correct terminology is **Fc gamma receptor (Fc-gamma receptor; FcgammaR)**.
## 25.2 Treatment concepts
### treatment hierarchy
1. **Corticosteroids** - reduce immune-mediated platelet destruction/autoantibody production.
2. **Rituximab** - used for steroid-resistant disease.
3. **Fostamatinib** - inhibits a tyrosine kinase pathway in splenic macrophages.
4. **Romiplostim / eltrombopag** - increase platelet production.
5. **IVIG** - listed as the "best and most effective" treatment for refractory ITP.
6. **Plasmapheresis** - severe cases .
7. **Splenectomy** - when medical therapy fails.
### Current 2026 ITP update
The ASH 2026 focused guideline for adults with primary ITP updates the older steroid-only hierarchy. It conditionally recommends **initial corticosteroids combined with either rituximab or a thrombopoietic agent** rather than corticosteroids alone in relevant adults, and after failure of first-line steroids it strongly recommends a **thrombopoietic agent** as one preferred second-line option and conditionally recommends rituximab as another.
### IVIG
IVIG can produce a **rapid temporary increase in platelet count** and is important when a rapid response is needed, but it should not be memorized as a universal "most effective" treatment for every ITP patient.
### Plasmapheresis
Plasma exchange is **not routine treatment for typical primary ITP**. Consider it only in unusual life-threatening refractory scenarios under specialist management.
### Splenectomy
Splenectomy remains an option for selected chronic/refractory ITP but is generally a later-line strategy after medical therapies, with timing individualized.
## 25.3 Fostamatinib correction
- Tyrosine kinase inhibitor.
- Said to block "Fyc tyrosine kinase" in macrophages.
The target is **spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK)**. Fostamatinib inhibits SYK signaling involved in Fc-receptor-mediated platelet phagocytosis.
# 26. CHELATORS FOR HEAVY-METAL POISONING
## 26.1 General principle
Chelators bind metal ions to form complexes that can be eliminated body. **Chelation is not interchangeable across metals**; the correct agent depends on the toxic metal, severity, organ function and timing.
## 26.2 Dimercaprol (BAL; British Anti-Lewisite)
### mnemonic: "BAM BAM"
- **B** = Bismuth
- **A** = Arsenic
- **M** = Mercury
### contraindications
- Cadmium poisoning.
- Iron poisoning.
- Reason: toxic complexes can form.
Dimercaprol is a recognized chelator for **acute arsenic poisoning** and has uses in selected heavy-metal intoxications. The ATSDR specifically discusses dimercaprol in acute arsenic exposure and notes the need for specialist toxicology input. It should not be used indiscriminately for all metals.
**Important:** Iron poisoning is not treated with dimercaprol.
## 26.3 Calcium disodium EDTA (CaNa2EDTA)
Mnemonic "MLC":
- M = manganese
- L = lead
- C = cadmium
The key clinical indication to remember is **lead poisoning**, where calcium disodium EDTA may be used in appropriate severe cases. The blanket manganese/cadmium "drug of choice" wording is not reliable and should not be used as a current clinical rule.
**Critical nomenclature point:** **Calcium disodium EDTA (CaNa2EDTA)** is the appropriate EDTA formulation for lead poisoning. Disodium EDTA is not interchangeable with it and can cause dangerous hypocalcemia.
## 26.4 Penicillamine
- Chelator for **copper**.
- Classic association: **Wilson disease**.
This is a useful exam association, although modern Wilson disease treatment can also involve zinc and other chelation strategies depending on the clinical situation.
## 26.5 Iron chelators
- **Deferoxamine**
- **Deferiprone**
- Both contain "FE" in their names.
- Used for iron poisoning/iron overload, with thalassemia and repeated transfusions mentioned.
- **Deferoxamine** is a major chelator for **acute iron poisoning** and for chronic iron overload in selected patients.
- **Deferiprone** is an oral iron chelator primarily used for **chronic transfusional iron overload**, not the routine first-line antidote for acute iron poisoning.
- **Deferasirox** is another important oral chelator for chronic iron overload.
### Thalassemia
Repeated transfusions can cause **secondary iron overload**, which is an important indication for chronic chelation therapy.
