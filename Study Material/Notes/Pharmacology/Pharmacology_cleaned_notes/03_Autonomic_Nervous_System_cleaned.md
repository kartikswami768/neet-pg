# Autonomic Nervous System — Topic-Organized Pharmacology Notes
# 1. AUTONOMIC NERVOUS SYSTEM (ANS)
## 1.1 Functional divisions
### Sympathetic nervous system
- Classically associated with the **fight-or-flight** response.
- Major effects:
 - ↑ heart rate and cardiac activity
 - Bronchodilation
 - Mydriasis
 - Reduced bladder emptying
 - Reduced gastrointestinal motility
 - Increased lipolysis
- Sexual function involves coordinated sympathetic and parasympathetic activity.
**Sexual function is coordinated rather than exclusively sympathetic or parasympathetic:**
- **Erection:** predominantly **parasympathetic / NO-mediated**.
- **Emission/ejaculation:** predominantly **sympathetic**, with an important somatic component for expulsion.
### Parasympathetic nervous system
- Classically associated with **rest-and-digest** activity.
- Major effects:
 - ↓ heart rate
 - Bronchoconstriction
 - Miosis
 - Bladder contraction → urination
 - ↑ gastrointestinal motility and secretion
 - Salivation/lacrimation
The commonly tested association is:
**Parasympathetic → erection**
**Sympathetic → emission/ejaculation**
The two systems cooperate with the somatic nervous system during sexual activity.
## 1.2 ANS anatomy
### Sympathetic system — thoracolumbar outflow
- Origin: **T1–L2**.
- Preganglionic fibers release **acetylcholine (ACh)**.
- ACh acts on **Nn (neuronal nicotinic)** receptors in autonomic ganglia.
- Most sympathetic postganglionic fibers release **norepinephrine (NE)**.
- The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion:
 - Preganglionic ACh → **Nn** receptors on chromaffin cells.
 - Adrenal medulla releases mainly **epinephrine** into the circulation.
- End-organ adrenergic receptors are mainly **α and β** receptors.
- this the **adrenergic nervous system**.
- It also notes that dopamine is a minor transmitter at selected sites.
### Parasympathetic system — craniosacral outflow
- Cranial nerves: **III, VII, IX, X**
- Sacral outflow: **S2–S4**
- Preganglionic neurotransmitter: **ACh**
- Ganglionic receptor: **Nn**
- Postganglionic neurotransmitter: **ACh**
- End-organ receptor: **muscarinic (M)**
### General neurotransmitter rule
| Location | Neurotransmitter | Receptor |
|---|---|---|
| All autonomic preganglionic fibers | ACh | Nn |
| Parasympathetic postganglionic | ACh | Muscarinic |
| Most sympathetic postganglionic | NE | α/β |
| Sympathetic sweat glands | ACh | Muscarinic |
| Adrenal medulla | ACh from preganglionic fiber | Nn → catecholamine release |
This receptor/transmitter map is more useful than the simplistic statement “ACh = parasympathetic, NE = sympathetic,” because sympathetic cholinergic fibers to sweat glands are an important exception.
# 2. CHOLINERGIC DRUG CONCEPTS
## 2.1 High-yield terminology
- **Cholinergic drugs / parasympathomimetics:** increase cholinergic effects.
- **Anticholinergic drugs:** decrease muscarinic effects.
**Important distinction**
- “Anticholinergic” in clinical pharmacology generally means **antimuscarinic**.
- Nicotinic blockade is a separate pharmacologic category.
# 3. ACETYLCHOLINE (ACh) METABOLISM
## 3.1 Synthesis
- Components:
 - **Choline**
 - **Acetyl-CoA**
- identifies **choline uptake** as the rate-limiting step.
### Reconstruction
```text
Extracellular choline
 ↓ high-affinity choline transporter
Choline enters presynaptic terminal
 +
Acetyl-CoA
 ↓ choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)
Acetylcholine
 ↓
Vesicular storage via VAChT
```
### Pharmacologic inhibitors
- **Hemicholinium** → inhibits high-affinity choline uptake.
- **Vesamicol** → inhibits vesicular ACh transport (VAChT).
## 3.2 Release
```text
Nerve depolarization
 ↓
Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open
 ↓
Ca²⁺ influx
 ↓
SNARE-dependent vesicle fusion
 ↓
ACh release into synaptic cleft
```
- Botulinum toxin interferes with ACh release.
- mentions a puffer-fish toxin blocking Na⁺ channels.
- **Botulinum neurotoxin** cleaves SNARE proteins and thereby prevents presynaptic ACh vesicle release at the neuromuscular junction.
- **Tetrodotoxin (TTX)** from pufferfish blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; it is a neurotoxin, not a direct inhibitor of ACh release machinery.
## 3.3 Termination of action
### Endogenous neuronal ACh
- Rapidly hydrolyzed by **acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** in the synaptic cleft.
- Hydrolysis produces:
 - **Choline**
 - **Acetate**
- Choline is taken back into the nerve terminal and reused.
### Exogenous ACh / plasma metabolism
- Plasma butyrylcholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase) contributes to breakdown of some exogenous choline esters/drugs.
# 4. BOTULINUM AND TETANUS TOXINS
## 4.1 Botulinum toxin
**Source organism:** *Clostridium botulinum*
**Mechanism**
- Blocks presynaptic ACh release at cholinergic nerve terminals by cleaving SNARE proteins.
- Produces **flaccid paralysis**.
### Clinical uses retained from
- Spasticity / focal muscle overactivity
- Blepharospasm
- Strabismus
- Cervical dystonia / torticollis
- Hyperhidrosis
- Cosmetic treatment of dynamic facial lines
- Selected gastrointestinal indications such as achalasia
**Preparation highlighted in :** Botulinum toxin type A.
### Clinical pearl
**Botulinum toxin = blocks ACh release → flaccid paralysis**
## 4.2 Tetanus toxin
**Source organism:** *Clostridium tetani*
**Mechanism**
- Blocks release of inhibitory neurotransmitters, especially **GABA and glycine**, from inhibitory interneurons.
- Produces **disinhibition → muscle rigidity and spasm**.
### Contrast
| Toxin | Main synaptic effect | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Botulinum toxin | ↓ ACh release at NMJ | **Flaccid paralysis** |
| Tetanus toxin | ↓ GABA/glycine inhibitory transmission | **Spastic paralysis / rigidity** |
# 5. CHOLINERGIC RECEPTORS
# 5.1 Nicotinic receptors
- **Ionotropic** ligand-gated cation channels.
- Pentameric structure.
- Excitatory.
- Important subtypes:
 - **Nn (neuronal):** autonomic ganglia and adrenal medulla.
 - **Nm (muscle):** neuromuscular junction.
### Nn
- Present on postganglionic neurons in autonomic ganglia.
- Present on adrenal medullary chromaffin cells.
**Antagonists**
- Ganglion blockers, e.g. **trimethaphan**.
### Nm
- Present at skeletal neuromuscular junctions.
- Stimulation → skeletal muscle contraction.
**Antagonists**
- Nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers:
 - Tubocurarine
 - Atracurium
 - Vecuronium
 - Others in the class
**Agonist:** nicotine.
# 5.2 Muscarinic receptors
- G-protein-coupled receptors.
- Seven-transmembrane structure.
| Receptor | G protein | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | Gq | Excitatory / secretory |
| M2 | Gi | Cardiac inhibition |
| M3 | Gq | Glandular secretion + smooth muscle contraction |
| M4 | Gi | Predominantly CNS |
| M5 | Gq | Predominantly CNS |
## High-yield peripheral associations
### M1
- Gastric/enteric and neural effects.
- Classically associated with ↑ gastric acid secretion.
### M2
- Heart, especially **SA and AV nodes**.
- Activation → ↓ cAMP and ↓ heart rate / conduction.
### M3
- Exocrine secretions.
- Smooth-muscle contraction in:
 - Bronchi
 - GI tract
 - Bladder detrusor
- Ocular:
 - Pupil constriction
 - Ciliary muscle contraction → accommodation
# 6. “DUMBBELSS” — CHOLINERGIC EXCESS
The mnemonic summarizes common muscarinic effects:
- **D**iarrhea
- **U**rination
- **M**iosis
- **B**ronchospasm
- **B**radycardia
- **E**mesis
- **L**acrimation
- **S**alivation
- **S**weating
### Mechanistic summary
```text
Muscarinic stimulation
├── Eye → miosis + accommodation
├── Heart → bradycardia
├── Lung → bronchoconstriction + secretions
├── GI tract → ↑ motility + secretion → diarrhea/cramps
├── Bladder → detrusor contraction + sphincter relaxation → urination
└── Glands → salivation + lacrimation + sweating
```
# 7. MUSCARINIC RECEPTOR–SELECTIVE DRUGS
- M1 antagonists: **pirenzepine, telenzepine**
- M2 agonist listed as: **methacholine**
- M3 agonist: **bethanechol**
- M3 antagonists: **solifenacin, darifenacin**
- **Methacholine is not M2-specific**; it is a relatively direct **muscarinic agonist** used primarily for bronchial challenge testing because of its bronchoconstrictor effect.
- **Bethanechol is not absolutely M3-specific**; clinically it is a direct muscarinic agonist with prominent bladder/GI effects.

# 9. TOBACCO CESSATION PHARMACOTHERAPY
## 9.1 Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT)
**Forms in **
- Patch
- Gum
- Lozenge
- Nasal spray
- Nicotine lozenges are designed for **buccal/oral dissolution**, not routine sublingual administration.
- NRT is used to reduce withdrawal and cravings while behavioural support addresses dependence.
## 9.2 Varenicline
- Partial agonist at **α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors**.
- Reduces withdrawal and decreases the rewarding effect of cigarette nicotine.
- Listed: depression/suicidal risk as a key adverse effect. The former prominent boxed neuropsychiatric warning for varenicline was removed by the FDA after later evidence. Neuropsychiatric symptoms can still occur and should be monitored, but the old blanket warning should not be presented as the current central teaching point.
## 9.3 Bupropion
- Antidepressant also used for smoking cessation.
- Mechanistically a **norepinephrine–dopamine reuptake inhibitor** with nicotinic receptor antagonism relevant to tobacco dependence.
Important contraindications include:
- Seizure disorder / significant seizure risk
- Current or recent MAOI use
- Eating disorders such as bulimia or anorexia nervosa
The statement that all three first-line drugs are contraindicated in “CNS and heart disease” is **too broad and incorrect**.
## 9.4 Current guideline context
WHO recommends pharmacologic tobacco-cessation options including:
- **Varenicline**
- **Nicotine replacement therapy**
- **Bupropion**
- **Cytisine**
Pharmacotherapy is recommended together with behavioural support when appropriate.
# 10. MUSHROOM POISONING
## 10.1 Muscarinic syndrome — early onset
** example:** *Inocybe*
**Toxin:** muscarine
**Features:** cholinergic / muscarinic toxidrome
- Salivation
- Lacrimation
- Sweating
- Miosis
- Bradycardia
- Bronchospasm/bronchorrhea
- Urination
- Diarrhea
- Vomiting
**Treatment:** atropine for significant muscarinic toxicity.
## 10.2 Amanita phalloides — amatoxin syndrome
**Toxin:** amatoxins
Typical syndrome:
- GI symptoms initially
- Followed by **severe hepatotoxicity / acute liver failure**
Listed: “thioctic acid and silybin” as treatment. Current management is centered on:
- Intensive supportive care
- Activated charcoal in selected cases
- Specialist toxicology/hepatology management
- **Silibinin** may be used where available
- **Urgent liver-transplant assessment** in severe hepatic failure
Do not memorize thioctic acid as a universal antidote.
## 10.3 Amanita muscaria — hallucinogenic syndrome
**Toxin:** muscimol (and ibotenic acid)
Features:
- Altered mental status
- Hallucinations
- Agitation or CNS depression
- Seizures may occur
**Treatment:** mainly supportive/toxicology-guided management.
Atropine is not a routine antidote for muscimol poisoning.

# 13. PHYSOSTIGMINE
- Tertiary amine anticholinesterase.
- Crosses the BBB.
- Increases ACh centrally and peripherally.
### Classic uses
- Antidote for severe **antimuscarinic toxicity**
- Miotic
- Historical glaucoma use
Physostigmine is primarily remembered clinically for **selected severe central anticholinergic toxicity** when appropriate and when ECG/other contraindications have been excluded. Its use requires monitoring and toxicology expertise. Reason: it can cause bradycardia and conduction abnormalities in like av block
# 14. ALZHEIMER DISEASE PHARMACOLOGY
## 14.1 Cholinesterase inhibitors
### Drugs listed in
- Tacrine
- Donepezil
- Galantamine
- Rivastigmine
### Mechanism
```text
AChE inhibition
 ↓
↑ synaptic acetylcholine
 ↓
Symptomatic cognitive benefit in Alzheimer disease
```
## 14.2 Drug-specific points
### Tacrine
- Historically used.
- **Hepatotoxicity** limits use.
Tacrine is no longer a routine treatment for Alzheimer disease in modern practice.
### Donepezil
- Centrally acting AChE inhibitor.
- Used across Alzheimer disease severity ranges depending on clinical context.
 donepezil the “most effective” and universal DOC. That is an oversimplification; current practice uses several cholinesterase inhibitors, with selection individualized to disease stage, tolerability, comorbidities, formulations, and access.
### Rivastigmine
- Inhibits AChE and butyrylcholinesterase.
- Oral and **transdermal patch** formulations.
### Galantamine
- AChE inhibitor with additional nicotinic receptor modulation.
## 14.3 MEMANTINE
- **NMDA receptor antagonist**.
- Reduces excessive glutamatergic excitotoxic signaling.
Do not memorize “glutamate causes memory loss.” The clinically useful concept is:
**Memantine → NMDA antagonism → reduces pathologic/excess glutamatergic signaling and excitotoxicity.**
Used mainly in **moderate-to-severe Alzheimer disease**, sometimes in combination with a cholinesterase inhibitor.
## 14.4 ANTI-AMYLOID ANTIBODIES
Listed::
- Aducanumab
- Lecanemab
- Donanemab
Mechanism:
- Target aggregated **amyloid-β** to reduce amyloid plaque burden.
### Current status
- **Aducanumab (Aduhelm):** FDA accelerated approval was later withdrawn in 2024.
- **Lecanemab (Leqembi):** FDA traditional approval for Alzheimer disease.
- **Donanemab (Kisunla):** FDA approval in 2024 for eligible patients with early Alzheimer disease.
These therapies are not generic replacements for symptomatic cholinesterase inhibitors and are used in selected patients with appropriate disease stage and amyloid pathology.
# 17. NEOSTIGMINE
## 17.1 Mechanism
- Reversible AChE inhibition.
- Increases ACh at:
 - Neuromuscular junctions
 - Parasympathetic synapses
 - Autonomic ganglia
## 17.2 High-yield uses from
### A. Reversal of nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockade
```text
Nondepolarizing blocker
 ↓
Nm receptor blockade
 ↓
↓ skeletal muscle contraction
Neostigmine
 ↓
↑ ACh
 ↓
Outcompetes blocker at Nm
 ↓
Restores neuromuscular transmission
```
Because neostigmine also increases muscarinic activity, an antimuscarinic (commonly glycopyrrolate or atropine) is co-administered.
### B. Myasthenia gravis — symptomatic therapy
Listed::
- Neostigmine
- Pyridostigmine
**Current clinical preference:** **pyridostigmine** is the usual first-line symptomatic AChE inhibitor for most patients with MG.
### C. Neurotoxic snake envenomation
 distinguishes postsynaptic versus presynaptic neurotoxicity.
- In selected **postsynaptic neurotoxic envenomations**, anticholinesterases may provide benefit as an adjunct to antivenom.
- Presynaptic neurotoxicity generally responds less well because the presynaptic nerve terminal itself is damaged.
- Do **not** treat antivenom as optional in significant systemic snake envenomation.
# 18. MYASTHENIA GRAVIS (MG) — DRUG-RELATED PHARMACOLOGY
## 18.1 Pathophysiology
- Autoimmune disorder of neuromuscular transmission.
- Most commonly antibodies target **ACh receptors (AChR)**; other targets such as **MuSK** also occur.
- Functional consequence → impaired neuromuscular transmission → fatigable weakness.
## 18.2 Symptomatic treatment
- **Pyridostigmine** is the usual first-line symptomatic anticholinesterase.
- Neostigmine is an alternative in appropriate settings.
### Why add atropine/glycopyrrolate?
AChE inhibition increases ACh at muscarinic as well as nicotinic synapses.
```text
Neostigmine
├── Nicotinic effect → desired ↑ NMJ transmission
└── Muscarinic effect → diarrhea, salivation, bradycardia, etc.
 ↓
 atropine/glycopyrrolate
```
## 18.3 Immunotherapy
Listed::
- Prednisolone
- Azathioprine
Current treatment may also include other steroid-sparing immunotherapies and targeted biologics depending on antibody status, severity, and specialist guidance.
## 18.4 Thymectomy
- Thymectomy if thymoma is present.
Thymectomy is indicated for **all patients with thymoma**, but in selected non-thymomatous generalized **AChR-antibody-positive** MG, thymectomy may also be recommended depending on age, disease severity, response to medical therapy, and specialist assessment.
## 18.5 Myasthenic crisis
Features:
- Respiratory muscle weakness
- Bulbar weakness may coexist
- Risk of ventilatory failure
Acute rescue:
- **IVIG**
- **Plasma exchange**
Ventilatory and ICU support are often essential.
## 18.6 Diagnosis — historical versus current
- Edrophonium (Tensilon) test
- Rapid improvement in ptosis was considered diagnostic.
Edrophonium testing is now **rarely used** because of safety/availability issues. Current evaluation generally relies on:
- AChR antibodies
- MuSK antibodies where indicated
- Repetitive nerve stimulation
- Single-fiber EMG
- Clinical examination and electrophysiology

# 20. SNAKE NEUROTOXICITY — EXAM COMPARISON
| Feature | Cobra (exam pattern) | Krait (exam pattern) |
|---|---|---|
| Major neurotoxicity | Predominantly postsynaptic NMJ dysfunction | Prominent presynaptic neurotoxicity |
| Anticholinesterase response | May help in selected cases | Often limited |

# 22. ORGANOPHOSPHATE POISONING
## 22.1 Mechanism
```text
Organophosphate
 ↓
Phosphorylation of AChE
 ↓
AChE inhibition
 ↓
Massive ACh accumulation
 ↓
Muscarinic + nicotinic + CNS toxicity
```
## 22.2 Clinical features
### Muscarinic
- Miosis
- Salivation
- Lacrimation
- Sweating
- Bronchorrhea
- Bronchospasm
- Bradycardia
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Urination
### Nicotinic
- Fasciculations
- Weakness
- Paralysis
- Respiratory-muscle weakness
### CNS
- Agitation/confusion
- Seizures
- Coma
## 22.3 Treatment
### 1. Atropine
- Competitive muscarinic antagonist.
- Controls:
 - Bronchorrhea
 - Bronchospasm
 - Muscarinic bradyarrhythmias
 - Other clinically significant muscarinic manifestations
**Important:** treatment is titrated to clinical resolution of pulmonary secretions/bronchospasm and adequate oxygenation/ventilation—not to a normal pupil size or normal heart rate.
### 2. Pralidoxime (2-PAM) / other oximes
Mechanism:
- Reactivates phosphorylated AChE **before aging**.
```text
OP–AChE complex
 ↓ pralidoxime
Regenerated AChE + reactivated enzyme
```
- Oximes are most clearly established for **organophosphate** toxicity.
- The blanket statement “ineffective for all carbamates” is too categorical. Some carbamate poisonings behave differently, and indiscriminate use should be guided by the suspected agent/toxicology advice.
### 3. Supportive care
- Airway protection
- Oxygenation and ventilation
- Decontamination when appropriate
- Seizure management, often with benzodiazepines
- ICU monitoring in severe poisoning

# 24. ANTIMUSCARINIC DRUGS — IMPORTANT EXAMPLES
## 24.1 Atropine
- Nonselective competitive muscarinic antagonist.
- Tertiary amine → CNS penetration.
Major uses:
- Symptomatic bradycardia
- Organophosphate poisoning
- Selected preoperative uses
- Ocular cycloplegia/mydriasis
## 24.2 Hyoscine / scopolamine
- Tertiary antimuscarinic.
- Strong CNS effects.
- Important in **motion sickness**.
## 24.3 Glycopyrrolate
- Quaternary antimuscarinic.
- Poor CNS penetration.
- Reduces salivary/airway secretions.
- Used perioperatively and with neostigmine in some settings.
## 24.4 Ipratropium, tiotropium, umeclidinium, aclidinium

# 25. ATROPINE / DATURA POISONING
Classic antimuscarinic toxidrome:
- **Hot as a hare** → hyperthermia
- **Red as a beet** → flushing
- **Dry as a bone** → dry mucosa/skin
- **Blind as a bat** → mydriasis + cycloplegia
- **Mad as a hatter** → delirium/hallucinations
- **Full as a flask** → urinary retention
May also produce:
- Tachycardia
- Ileus
- Seizures
- Coma
## Antidote
**Physostigmine**, in carefully selected patients with severe central anticholinergic toxicity.
**Why physostigmine rather than neostigmine?**
- Physostigmine crosses the BBB.
- Neostigmine does not meaningfully cross the BBB.
**Safety note:** physostigmine is not appropriate for every anticholinergic overdose; avoid/obtain expert guidance in important conduction abnormalities or mixed/unknown poisoning.
# 26. CLINICAL USES OF ANTIMUSCARINICS
## 26.1 CNS / vestibular system
### Motion sickness
- **Scopolamine** is a classic preventive agent.
- First-generation H1 antihistamines that cross the BBB:
 - Diphenhydramine
 - Promethazine
 - Cyclizine
 - Meclizine
 - Cinnarizine (drug class conventions vary by region)
**Administration**
- Oral agents
- Transdermal scopolamine patch
- Take before travel.
- Sedation is a common adverse effect.
**Current practical point:** prevention is the main role; established severe vomiting is harder to treat than preventing the syndrome.
## 26.2 Drug-induced parkinsonism
Antimuscarinics listed in :
- Trihexyphenidyl (benzhexol)
- Benztropine
- Biperiden
- Certain sedating antihistamines may also be used
Mechanism:
- Reduce excessive cholinergic influence in the striatum when dopamine blockade from antipsychotics produces parkinsonism.
## 26.3 Cardiovascular — atropine
- Blocks M2-mediated vagal effects at the SA/AV node.
- Increases heart rate and improves AV nodal conduction in appropriate vagally mediated bradyarrhythmias.
**[ resuscitation context]**
Atropine remains an ACLS option for symptomatic bradycardia in appropriate patients; it is not a universal treatment for every form of bradycardia.
**Transplanted heart**
- Denervation changes autonomic responses.
- Drug effects that depend on intact vagal reflexes may be reduced/unpredictable.
## 26.4 Respiratory tract
### Glycopyrrolate
- Decreases secretions.
- Used in selected perioperative settings.
### Inhaled antimuscarinics
- Ipratropium
- Tiotropium
- Umeclidinium
- Aclidinium
Used primarily as bronchodilators in obstructive airway disease, especially COPD.

### Common agents

Important situations from :

# 36. DOPAMINE
## Receptor effects are dose-dependent, but the old “renal-dose dopamine” model is misleading
- Low dose → D1/D2
- Medium dose → β1
- High dose → α1
###

The historical “renal dose dopamine” concept does not provide a recommended strategy for prevention/treatment of acute kidney injury.

- Dopamine can increase heart rate and arrhythmia risk.

# 37. DOBUTAMINE
- Predominantly **β1 agonist** with additional β2/α1 effects.

# 49. EPINEPHRINE vs NOREPINEPHRINE — BP AND HR
## 49.1 General receptor model
### Blood vessels
- **α1 → vasoconstriction → ↑ BP**
- **β2 → vasodilation → ↓ BP**
### Heart
- **β1 → ↑ heart rate and contractility**
### Reflex response
A rise in BP can trigger:
- ↑ baroreceptor firing
- ↑ vagal activity
- **reflex bradycardia**
A fall in BP can trigger:
- ↓ baroreceptor firing
- ↑ sympathetic outflow
- **reflex tachycardia**
# 50. EPINEPHRINE vs NOREPINEPHRINE
## Epinephrine
- Acts on **α1, α2, β1, β2** (and β3).
- Effects are **dose-dependent**.
- At sufficient α1 effect → vasoconstriction and BP rise.
- β2 effect can produce vasodilation in some vascular beds.
Direct cardiac β1 effect:
- ↑ heart rate
- ↑ contractility
## Norepinephrine
- Strong **α1 + α2 + β1** effects.
- Little clinically important β2 activity.
- Strong vasoconstriction → ↑ BP.
- Reflex bradycardia may override its direct β1 tachycardic effect.
### Net exam concept
```text
Epinephrine → direct β1 tachycardia often prominent
Norepinephrine → ↑ BP → strong reflex bradycardia
```
# 51. TRANSPLANTED HEART
- Cardiac transplantation produces denervation.
- Reflex autonomic input from the CNS is absent/reduced.
**Clinical implication**
- Drug effects depending on autonomic reflex arcs differ from those in an innervated heart.
- Direct β-adrenergic stimulation can still produce tachycardia.
# 52. VASOMOTOR REVERSAL OF DALE
## Definition
Epinephrine administered after **α-adrenergic blockade** can produce a paradoxical fall in BP.
### Mechanism
```text
Epinephrine
 ↓
α1 blocked
 ↓
α1 vasoconstriction removed
β2 effect remains
 ↓
Vasodilation
 ↓
Fall in BP
```
This phenomenon is known as **Dale's vasomotor reversal**.
Classic α blockers:
- Prazosin
- Phentolamine
- Phenoxybenzamine

- Useful in selected glaucoma settings.
- Particularly important in the management of angle closure after initial IOP reduction when the iris can respond.
# 72. MYDRIATICS AND GLAUCOMA
- Mydriasis can narrow/close the iridocorneal angle in susceptible patients.
### High-yield
**Mydriasis can precipitate acute angle closure in anatomically narrow angles.**
Therefore, routine mydriasis is avoided in patients at risk for angle closure until appropriate assessment.
# 73. OPEN-ANGLE vs ACUTE ANGLE-CLOSURE GLAUCOMA
## Chronic open-angle glaucoma
Main pharmacologic strategies:
- Prostaglandin analogues
- β-blockers
- α2 agonists
- Topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
- Other agents depending on patient and guideline context
## Acute angle closure
Medical emergency.
Typical features:
- Sudden severe ocular pain
- Blurred vision / halos
- Headache
- Nausea/vomiting
- Markedly elevated IOP
- Mid-dilated poorly reactive pupil
- Corneal edema
### Immediate treatment concept
```text
Acute angle closure
 ↓
Rapidly lower IOP
 ├── acetazolamide
 ├── topical aqueous suppressants
 ├── osmotic therapy (e.g., mannitol) when indicated
 └── pilocarpine once IOP has fallen sufficiently and the iris is responsive
 ↓
Definitive laser peripheral iridotomy
```
The statement that a single prostaglandin analogue is the “DOC for open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma” is too broad. **Acute angle closure requires urgent multi-drug pressure lowering and definitive treatment of the anatomic block, typically laser peripheral iridotomy.**
# 74. GLAUCOMA DRUG-SPECIFIC PEARLS
## Apraclonidine
- Topical α2 agonist.
- Allergic reactions/tachyphylaxis are relatively prominent.
- Often used short term rather than as chronic maintenance.
## Timolol / betaxolol
- Avoid/caution in asthma or significant obstructive airway disease.
- Timolol is nonselective; betaxolol is relatively β1-selective.
## Brimonidine
- CNS depression can occur in young children.
The “safest drug in pregnancy” statement should **not** be memorized. Pregnancy/lactation use requires individualized ophthalmologic/obstetric assessment.
## Dipivefrin
- Prodrug of epinephrine.
- Historical ophthalmic use.
The “dark/black eye due to black epinephrine” is an **exam mnemonic rather than a pharmacologically accurate adverse-effect description**.
## Pilocarpine
- Can produce:
 - Brow ache
 - Headache
 - Spasm of accommodation
 - Transient visual blur
- “Highly painful” is too absolute.
## Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and angle closure
Acetazolamide and topical CAIs are **treatments for glaucoma**. statement attributing secondary angle closure to acetazolamide/dorzolamide because of ciliary edema is incorrect.
A classic cause of **secondary angle closure from ciliochoroidal effusion** is **topiramate** and other sulfonamide-related agents in susceptible patients.
