# Anti-Cancer Drugs and Immunomodulators
# 1. Cytotoxic Drugs — Core Principles
## 1.1 Basic principle
Cytotoxic anticancer drugs preferentially damage **rapidly dividing cells**. The therapeutic problem is that many normal tissues also contain rapidly proliferating cells, so these drugs can be highly toxic.
### Common toxicities — mnemonic: **BHARAT MATA**
| Toxicity | Key point |
|---|---|
| **B**one-marrow suppression | Myelosuppression → anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia |
| **H** / mucosal injury | Gastrointestinal mucosal injury and ulceration |
| **A**lopecia | Hair loss |
| **R/T** / reproductive toxicity | Teratogenicity; infertility from gonadal toxicity |
| **A**zoospermia / anovulation | Male and female infertility may occur |
> is useful for remembering the broad toxicity pattern, but individual drugs differ substantially in which toxicity dominates.
## 1.2 Bone-marrow suppression
Consequences of myelosuppression:
- ↓ RBCs → **anemia** → erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy may be used in selected patients.
- ↓ WBCs / neutrophils → **neutropenia** → G-CSF such as **filgrastim** may be used when indicated.
- ↓ platelets → **thrombocytopenia**.
- **Oprelvekin** is an IL-11 analogue that historically stimulated platelet production.
- injectable administration for the listed growth-factor therapies.
### Growth-factor pearls
- **Filgrastim = G-CSF** (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor).
- **Oprelvekin = recombinant IL-11 analogue**.
Oprelvekin is a historical/legacy drug in many modern oncology settings rather than a routine contemporary solution for chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia. The FDA Purple Book lists the Neumega/oprelvekin product as **discontinued**. Do not generalize The oprelvekin statement into a recommendation for contemporary routine practice.

**Chlorambucil should not be included as a marrow-sparing exception.** Bone-marrow suppression is a common adverse effect of chlorambucil. Vincristine and bleomycin are comparatively less myelosuppressive than many cytotoxic agents, but chlorambucil is clearly myelosuppressive.
## 1.3 Mucosal toxicity
Severe mucosal injury can occur with cytotoxic therapy.
**palifermin** as treatment for severe gastrointestinal mucosal injury/diarrhea.
The high-yield, evidence-based use of **palifermin** is prevention/reduction of **severe oral mucositis** in selected patients with hematologic malignancies receiving myelotoxic therapy with **autologous hematopoietic stem-cell support**. It is not simply a general treatment for chemotherapy-induced severe diarrhea.
Palifermin is a recombinant keratinocyte growth factor that promotes epithelial repair.
## 1.4 Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS)
### Pathophysiology
Rapid destruction of tumor cells releases intracellular contents into the circulation:
- Nucleic acids → uric acid → **hyperuricemia**.
- K⁺ → **hyperkalemia**.
- Phosphate → **hyperphosphatemia**.
- Phosphate binds calcium → **secondary hypocalcemia**.
Major clinical consequences include:
- Acute kidney injury from urate and/or calcium-phosphate precipitation.
- Cardiac arrhythmias from hyperkalemia and other electrolyte abnormalities.
- Neuromuscular/neurologic complications from electrolyte disturbances.
### Management framework
| Problem | Management | Current clarification |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperuricemia | **Allopurinol** | Prevents formation of new uric acid; does not rapidly remove existing uric acid. |
| High-risk or established hyperuricemia | **Rasburicase** | Rapidly converts uric acid to allantoin; used in selected high-risk/established TLS. |
| Hyperphosphatemia + hyperkalemia / severe metabolic complications | Dialysis may be required | Renal replacement therapy is used for refractory/severe electrolyte abnormalities or kidney failure. |
| Hypocalcemia | Calcium listed in | **Do not routinely correct asymptomatic hypocalcemia** in TLS because calcium-phosphate precipitation may worsen tissue calcification. Treat symptomatic/severe hypocalcemia when clinically indicated. |
**Pegloticase is not a standard first-line TLS drug.** The modern TLS framework centers on hydration, risk assessment, allopurinol and/or rasburicase, frequent laboratory monitoring, and treatment of dangerous electrolyte abnormalities.
Contemporary expert guidance emphasizes hydration and risk-adapted uric-acid control; rasburicase is particularly useful when rapid reduction of pre-existing uric acid is needed. A 2025 British Society for Haematology update and a 2023 US expert-consensus statement provide contemporary guidance.
# 2. Classification of Cytotoxic Drugs by Cell Cycle
Two broad groups are emphasized:
1. **Cell-cycle non-specific (CCNS)** drugs — can act in multiple/all phases.
2. **Cell-cycle specific (CCS)** drugs — preferentially act during particular phases.
## 2.1 Cell-cycle non-specific drugs
### Major groups
- **Alkylating agents**
- **Platinum compounds**
### Core mechanism
Direct DNA damage. Because DNA is present throughout the cell cycle, these drugs are classified as cell-cycle non-specific.
### Platinum compounds
- Cisplatin
- Carboplatin
- Oxaliplatin
### Alkylating agents emphasized
- Mechlorethamine
- Dacarbazine
- Procarbazine
- Melphalan
- Cyclophosphamide
- Ifosfamide
- Chlorambucil
- Busulfan
- Carmustine
- Lomustine
> **Terminology note:** Dacarbazine and procarbazine are often grouped with alkylating-like/triazene agents in pharmacology teaching. They are grouped under alkylating agents for exam classification.
## 2.2 Cell-cycle specific drugs
The distributes the following across phases:
| Cell-cycle phase | Representative drugs |
|---|---|
| **G1** | CDK4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib — block G1→S transition |
| **S** | Hydroxyurea; antimetabolites; many topoisomerase I-directed agents are functionally S-phase dependent |
| **G2** | Bleomycin is taught as G2-specific ; topoisomerase II-directed agents are often associated with G2/S activity |
| **M** | Microtubule inhibitors — vinca alkaloids and taxanes |
Cell-cycle specificity is a useful exam framework, but several drug classes do **not** map cleanly onto a single phase in vivo. In particular, “topoisomerase inhibitors = G2/S” is more nuanced than a single-phase rule.
### Mnemonic
- **M phase:** microtubule inhibitors.
- **S phase:** hydroxyurea + antimetabolites.
- **G1:** CDK4/6 inhibitors block the G1→S transition.

# 13. Purine Analogues
## 13.1 Thiopurines
- **6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP)**
- **6-Thioguanine (6-TG)**
### Mechanism / activation
- Converted by **HGPRT** into active thiopurine nucleotides.
- Interfere with purine metabolism and DNA synthesis.
### Lesch-Nyhan syndrome
- **HGPRT deficiency**.
- that these drugs become ineffective because activation through HGPRT is impaired.
### Uses
- 6-MP is particularly important in **ALL maintenance therapy** in modern oncology, although that specific use is not emphasized .
## 13.2 Azathioprine
### General
- **Prodrug → 6-mercaptopurine**.
- Primarily an **immunosuppressive/antimetabolite** drug rather than a standard contemporary anticancer drug.
### Uses
- Autoimmune diseases.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease** (Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis) as a drug-of-choice association.
### Metabolism
Description::
- Liver inactivation involving **xanthine oxidase**.
- Cellular metabolism involving **TPMT**.
### Allopurinol interaction
Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase → reduced metabolism of azathioprine/6-MP → increased thiopurine toxicity.
Dose reduction with concomitant allopurinol is substantial. Contemporary references commonly recommend reducing azathioprine/6-MP to approximately **25–33% usual dose** (or a 60–75% reduction), with close blood-count monitoring.
### Pharmacogenomic update — TPMT + NUDT15
Severe thiopurine myelosuppression is not explained by TPMT alone. **NUDT15** is also a clinically important pharmacogenomic determinant, especially in populations in which NUDT15 loss-of-function variants are more prevalent.
- TPMT deficiency → increased active thiopurine metabolites → myelosuppression.
- NUDT15 deficiency → increased risk of severe thiopurine-associated leukopenia/neutropenia/myelosuppression.
- Genotype/phenotype information does **not** replace serial CBC monitoring.

# 31. Monoclonal Antibodies
## 31.1 Basic concept
### Production — classical hybridoma technique
The illustrates the classic workflow:
1. Mouse challenged with antigen.
2. Spleen cells harvested.
3. Myeloma cells prepared.
4. Fusion of spleen B cells and myeloma cells → **hybridomas**.
5. Culture in **HAT medium**.
6. Select antibody-producing clones.
7. Expand and harvest monoclonal antibody.
The hybridoma workflow is the classic examination model. Modern therapeutic antibodies can also be produced using recombinant expression systems rather than relying on a literal mouse-hybridoma manufacturing process.
## 31.2 Suffix nomenclature — exam convention
| Suffix | Classical origin convention |
|---|---|
| **-omab** | Murine / mouse |
| **-ximab** | Chimeric |
| **-zumab** | Humanized |
| **-umab** | Fully human |
> These suffixes are useful exam clues, but modern antibody naming is more nuanced than a single suffix dictionary.
