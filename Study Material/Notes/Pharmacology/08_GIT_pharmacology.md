## 1. Acid Peptic Disease and Gastric Acid Suppression

**PDF source:** printed pp. 120-123 (PDF pp. 1-4)

### 1.1 Acid Peptic Disease (APD)

#### PDF-derived concept

The handout defines acid peptic disease as excessive HCl/acid production in the stomach and links hyperacidity with:

- **Heartburn**
  - Acid exposure/reflux can produce a burning retrosternal sensation.
- **Peptic ulcer disease**
  - Gastric/duodenal mucosal injury can lead to ulcer formation.
- **GERD (gastro-esophageal reflux disease)**
  - Gastric contents reflux into the esophagus and cause symptoms.

### Current correction

The PDF definition is **simplified**. Acid-peptic disorders are not defined merely by excessive acid production; peptic ulcer disease and GERD are multifactorial disorders in which acid is an important therapeutic target, but not necessarily the sole or primary initiating abnormality.

For GERD, the clinically useful concept is **pathologic reflux of gastric contents causing troublesome symptoms and/or complications**, rather than simply "HCl going into the esophagus."

## 2. Physiology of Gastric Acid Secretion

### 2.1 Core pathway from the PDF

The handout depicts:

```text
Vagus nerve stimulation
        ↓
Acetylcholine
        ↓
Muscarinic pathway / histamine cycle
        ↓
Histamine release
        ↓
H2 receptor on parietal cell
        ↓
H+/K+-ATPase (proton pump)
        ↓
HCl secretion
```

It also shows the three principal drug targets:

```text
Muscarinic pathway → antimuscarinics
H2 receptor       → H2-receptor blockers
H+/K+-ATPase      → proton-pump inhibitors
```

### 2.2 More precise pharmacology

A simplified but physiologically more accurate framework is:

```text
                Acetylcholine
             ↙       ↓        ↘
        M3 receptor  M1       other vagal effects
        (parietal)   (ECL)
             ↓        ↓
          ↑ acid   ↑ histamine
                       ↓
                 H2 receptor
                 (parietal cell)
                       ↓
               ↑ cAMP / acid secretion
                       ↓
                 H+/K+-ATPase
                       ↓
                    H+ secretion

Gastrin → CCK-B receptor → parietal/ECL stimulation → ↑ acid
```

**High-yield principle:** the **H+/K+-ATPase is the final common pathway** of gastric acid secretion and is therefore the target producing the greatest acid suppression.

# 4. Proton-Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

**PDF source:** printed pp. 120-123 (PDF pp. 1-4)

## 4.1 Mechanism of action

### PDF-derived

- PPIs directly inhibit the **hydrogen-potassium ATPase (H+/K+-ATPase)** in gastric parietal cells.
- They are described as:
  - **Prodrugs** that become activated in the acidic secretory canaliculus.
  - **Irreversible inhibitors** / "suicide inhibitors."
  - The handout uses the mnemonic **DIL RO PA DA** and gives a potency ordering.
- The handout states that proton pumps are permanently inactivated and acid secretion remains suppressed beyond the short plasma half-life.

### Current pharmacology

PPIs are acid-labile prodrugs that become protonated/activated in the acidic canaliculus of the parietal cell and then **covalently and irreversibly inhibit active H+/K+-ATPase molecules**. New pump synthesis is required to restore secretion.

Therefore:

> **Short plasma half-life does not mean short pharmacodynamic effect.**

### Important correction to the PDF potency mnemonic

The PDF gives an exact "most potent to least potent" sequence:

> Dexrabeprazole → ilaprazole → lansoprazole → rabeprazole → omeprazole → pantoprazole

This is best regarded as a **course-specific exam mnemonic, not a universal modern potency ranking**. Apparent potency depends on dose, formulation, metabolism, CYP2C19 phenotype, and the acid-suppression endpoint being measured. Do not generalize this sequence as an evidence-based class-wide ranking.

---

## 4.2 Half-life and duration

### PDF-derived

- PDF gives a very short half-life of approximately **2-5 hours**.
- It states that acid suppression can continue for **2-5 days after stopping** because the proton pumps have been irreversibly inhibited.

### Current clarification

For most PPIs, the **plasma elimination half-life is usually around 0.5-2 hours**, although pharmacokinetics differ by agent and formulation. The antisecretory effect lasts much longer because of irreversible pump inhibition.

The exact duration after stopping varies; the important exam concept is:

> **Irreversible pump inhibition + need for new pump synthesis = prolonged pharmacodynamic effect despite short plasma half-life.**

---

## 4.3 When to administer PPIs

The PDF correctly emphasizes administration on an **empty stomach** and approximately **30 minutes to 1 hour before food**.

### Current recommendation

For classic GERD symptoms, PPIs are generally given **30-60 minutes before a meal**, usually before breakfast for once-daily therapy. For twice-daily therapy, dosing is generally before breakfast and before dinner. Bedtime administration is less effective for acid control. [1]

### Why?

Meals stimulate acid secretion and activate proton pumps. PPIs can inhibit pumps most effectively when the pumps are active.

---

## 4.4 Formulation

### PDF-derived

- PPIs are acid-labile when administered orally.
- Therefore they are generally supplied as **enteric-coated preparations** so that the drug survives gastric acid and reaches the intestine for absorption.

### High-yield sequence

```text
Oral dose
   ↓
Survives stomach because of enteric formulation
   ↓
Absorbed in intestine
   ↓
Reaches systemic circulation
   ↓
Concentrates in parietal cells
   ↓
Activated in acidic canaliculus
   ↓
Irreversible H+/K+-ATPase inhibition
```

---

## 4.5 CYP metabolism and drug interactions

### PDF-derived

The handout emphasizes:

- CYP-mediated hepatic metabolism.
- **Omeprazole** as a major CYP2C19 inhibitor.
- **Omeprazole + clopidogrel = avoid** because clopidogrel requires CYP2C19 activation.

### Current verification

The FDA-approved clopidogrel labeling states that **omeprazole and esomeprazole should be avoided with clopidogrel** because they reduce formation of the active metabolite and reduce antiplatelet activity. Pantoprazole and several other PPIs have less effect on clopidogrel pharmacology than omeprazole/esomeprazole. [2]

### Exam pearl

```text
Clopidogrel --CYP2C19--> active metabolite

Omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19
            ↓
Less active clopidogrel metabolite
            ↓
Reduced antiplatelet effect
```

### Nuance

Do not memorize "all PPIs are equally strong CYP2C19 inhibitors." Their interaction potential differs.

---

## 4.6 Clinical efficacy

The PDF gives several percentage ranges for acid suppression (40-60%, 60-80%, 80-99%) and describes PPIs as the most effective conventional acid-suppressant drugs.

### Current interpretation

The **direction of the comparison is useful** - PPIs provide substantially more acid suppression than H2 blockers or antacids - but the exact percentage of suppression is not a single fixed pharmacological property. It depends on drug, dose, timing, treatment duration, meal pattern, and measurement method.

---

## 4.7 PPIs in pregnancy

### PDF-derived statement

The PDF states:

- "All PPIs safe in pregnancy except omeprazole."
- "Omeprazole = most unsafe PPI."
- "Safest overall: lansoprazole."

### Current correction - important

This section of the PDF is **outdated/overstated and should not be memorized as current clinical guidance**.

UKTIS's July 2025 review reports that the available human data do **not provide compelling evidence that PPIs as a class increase major congenital malformations**, and the evidence does not establish increased risks of miscarriage, preterm delivery, low birth weight, or stillbirth. The data for individual PPIs differ in quantity, but omeprazole data are described as reassuring rather than demonstrating that it is uniquely unsafe. [3]

**Exam-safe interpretation:**

- Do not use "omeprazole is the unsafe PPI" as a current evidence-based pregnancy rule.
- Choice of PPI in pregnancy should depend on the indication, prior response, available safety data, and clinician assessment.

---

## 4.8 Indications / uses of PPIs

### Peptic ulcer disease

The PDF lists PPIs as **DOC** for peptic ulcer disease and specifically identifies:

- NSAID-associated peptic ulcer disease (e.g., aspirin/NSAIDs).
- *Helicobacter pylori*-associated peptic ulcer disease.

This is broadly consistent with modern clinical use, although management depends on cause and ulcer complications.

### GERD

The PDF states:

> Reduce acid with PPI + close the lower esophageal sphincter with a prokinetic.

It lists:

- Domperidone
- Metoclopramide

### Current correction

The PPI component is supported. However, **routine addition of a prokinetic solely for GERD is not recommended** unless there is objective evidence of gastroparesis. The ACG GERD guideline specifically recommends against prokinetic therapy for GERD in the absence of objective gastroparesis. [1]

Therefore:

> **PPI is standard pharmacologic therapy for GERD; prokinetics are not routine add-ons merely to "close the LES."**

### Prevention of aspiration during surgery

The PDF lists:

- PPI + prokinetic.

Perioperative aspiration-prevention regimens are context-specific and should not be reduced to a universal PPI-plus-prokinetic rule.

### Zollinger-Ellison syndrome / gastrinoma

PDF concept:

```text
Gastrinoma
   ↓
↑ Gastrin
   ↓
↑ Gastric acid secretion
   ↓
Peptic ulceration / acid-related disease
```

Treatment concept:

- **High-dose/adequate acid suppression with a PPI** is central.
- The PDF adds **octreotide** to reduce gastrin secretion.

### Current clarification

Octreotide has a role in selected neuroendocrine-tumor settings, but the statement in the PDF that it must "always" be combined with a PPI for gastrinoma is too absolute. Acid control with a PPI is the key pharmacologic objective; somatostatin analogues are individualized according to tumor burden, hormone secretion, metastatic disease, and other clinical factors.

# 5. Potassium-Competitive Acid Blockers (P-CABs / PCABs)

**PDF source:** printed p. 122 (PDF p. 3)

### PDF-derived

The handout mentions:

- Vonoprazan
- Revaprazan
- "Competitive blocker of potassium site"
- Reversible inhibition of the H+/K+-ATPase
- Treatment of *H. pylori* infection

### Current pharmacology

Vonoprazan is a **potassium-competitive acid blocker** that inhibits the gastric H+/K+-ATPase at the potassium-binding site. Unlike PPIs, it **does not require acid activation** and binds reversibly/noncovalently. Its current U.S. labeling includes GERD/erosive esophagitis indications and combination regimens for *H. pylori*. [4]

### P-CAB vs PPI

| Feature | PPI | P-CAB (e.g., vonoprazan) |
|---|---|---|
| Target | H+/K+-ATPase | H+/K+-ATPase |
| Binding | Irreversible/covalent after activation | Reversible, potassium-competitive |
| Acid activation required | Yes | No |
| Dependence on active pumps | Important | Less dependent on stimulated pump activation |
| Key example | Omeprazole | Vonoprazan |

# 6. Adverse Effects of Long-Term PPI Therapy

**PDF source:** printed pp. 122-123 (PDF pp. 3-4)

## 6.1 Mechanistic framework in the PDF

The PDF links chronic acid suppression with:

- Reduced absorption of selected micronutrients.
- Increased infection risk.
- Increased gastrin.

It specifically lists:

- Iron deficiency / iron-deficiency anemia.
- Calcium deficiency / osteoporosis.
- Magnesium deficiency.
- Vitamin B12 deficiency / megaloblastic anemia.
- Community-acquired pneumonia.
- *Clostridioides difficile* infection.
- Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.
- Hypergastrinemia.
- Gastric cell growth / gastric polyps.
- Kidney failure.
- Memory loss / Alzheimer-type dementia.

## 6.2 Current interpretation

The existence of **associations** in observational studies does not prove that PPIs causally produce all of these outcomes. AGA expert guidance emphasizes that evidence for many proposed long-term PPI adverse effects is low quality and advises against stopping a clearly indicated PPI solely because of fear of theoretical adverse effects. [5,6]

### Practical high-yield framework

```text
Long-term PPI
      ↓
Sustained gastric acid suppression
      ├── true/biologically plausible effects in some patients
      │      ├── hypergastrinemia
      │      ├── altered absorption of selected nutrients
      │      └── altered susceptibility to some enteric infections
      │
      └── numerous observational associations
             ↓
        not automatically causal
```

### AGA deprescribing principles

- Periodically review the **indication** for ongoing PPI therapy.
- If there is no definitive indication for chronic therapy, deprescribing can be considered.
- Patients with strong indications (e.g. complicated GERD or substantial upper-GI bleeding risk) generally should not have PPIs stopped solely because of concern about adverse-event associations.
- Rebound upper-GI symptoms can occur after discontinuation. [5]

### Routine monitoring is not automatically required

AGA best-practice guidance does **not** recommend routine screening of all long-term PPI users for bone mineral density, creatinine, magnesium, or vitamin B12 solely because they take a PPI. Testing should instead be individualized to the patient's clinical risks. [6]

---

## 6.3 Hypergastrinemia

### PDF-derived mechanism

```text
PPI
 ↓
↓ Gastric acid
 ↓
↓ Negative feedback on gastrin
 ↓
↑ Gastrin
```

The PDF associates elevated gastrin with:

- Gastric cell growth.
- Gastric polyp formation.

### Current interpretation

Mild-to-moderate hypergastrinemia can occur with chronic acid suppression. The clinical significance depends on the degree of elevation and the underlying condition. The PDF's simplified statement should not be interpreted as proof that routine PPI therapy inevitably causes clinically important neoplasia.

### Zollinger-Ellison / gastrinoma note

In gastrinoma, high gastrin is already part of the disease process. PPI therapy is used to control acid secretion; **somatostatin analogues such as octreotide are not mandatory in every patient simply because a PPI is being used**.

# 7. H2-Receptor Blockers

**PDF source:** printed p. 123 (PDF p. 4)

## 7.1 Drugs listed

- **Cimetidine**
  - First major H2 blocker historically.
  - Strong CYP inhibition.
  - Classically associated with endocrine adverse effects and drug interactions.
- **Ranitidine**
  - Historical H2 blocker.
- **Nizatidine**
  - Minimal CYP inhibition relative to cimetidine.
- **Famotidine**
  - Selective H2-receptor blocker with low CYP interaction potential.
- **Lafutidine**
  - Listed as a newer H2 blocker in the handout.

### Suffix

> **-tidine** → class clue for H2-receptor blockers (not an absolute rule for every chemical name in pharmacology).

---

## 7.2 Cimetidine - classic adverse effects

### PDF-derived

Cimetidine is associated with:

- **Gynecomastia**.
- **Sexual dysfunction / impotence**.
- Menstrual irregularities.
- Galactorrhea.
- Numerous drug interactions because of CYP inhibition.

### Mechanistic reasoning

Cimetidine can produce endocrine effects through antiandrogenic/prolactin-related mechanisms and is a strong inhibitor of multiple CYP enzymes.

### High-yield contrast

| H2 blocker | CYP interaction potential | Endocrine effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Cimetidine** | Highest among classic H2 blockers | Classic gynecomastia/sexual effects |
| **Famotidine** | Low | Much less characteristic |
| **Nizatidine** | Low/minimal | Less characteristic |

---

## 7.3 Ranitidine - current status

Ranitidine is present in the PDF as a standard H2 blocker, but its modern clinical status is different from the historical exam classification.

The U.S. FDA requested withdrawal of all ranitidine products in 2020 because of concerns about the contaminant **N-nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA)**. EMA also confirmed suspension of ranitidine medicines in the EU. [7,8]

Therefore:

> **For modern prescribing, do not treat ranitidine as an ordinary currently available default H2 blocker.**

Famotidine is the more relevant contemporary H2 blocker for routine study of the class.

# 8. Misoprostol and Gastric Mucosal Protection

**PDF source:** printed p. 124 (PDF p. 5)

## 8.1 Misoprostol

- **Prostaglandin E1 analogue**.
- PDF notes historical abortifacient use and current ulcer-prevention use.

### Physiologic basis for gastric protection

The PDF pathway is:

```text
COX-1 in stomach
      ↓
Prostaglandins
      ↓
├── ↓ gastric acid secretion
├── ↑ mucus
└── ↑ bicarbonate
      ↓
Gastric mucosal protection
```

### NSAID injury

```text
NSAID / aspirin
      ↓
COX inhibition
      ↓
↓ protective gastric prostaglandins
      ↓
↓ mucus + ↓ bicarbonate + impaired mucosal defense
      ↓
Ulcer risk
```

### Where misoprostol fits

Misoprostol can reduce NSAID-associated gastric ulcer risk by replacing some of the lost prostaglandin-mediated mucosal protection.

### Important PDF pearl

The handout says:

- Specific indication: **NSAID-induced ulcer disease / NSAID-associated ulcer risk**.
- Even in NSAID-associated disease, **PPI is generally preferred**, and misoprostol is not the routine drug of choice.

This distinction should be retained.

### Major adverse effect

- **Diarrhea** and abdominal cramping are common clinically important adverse effects.
- **Uterotonic effects** make misoprostol unsuitable for routine use during an ongoing wanted pregnancy; it is used in obstetric/gynaecologic medicine for specific indications.

# 9. Antacids

**PDF source:** printed pp. 124-125 (PDF pp. 5-6)

## 9.1 Definition

Antacids:

> **Directly neutralize acid already present in the stomach.**

They are **rapid-acting symptom-relief drugs**, but they do not provide the durable acid suppression obtained with PPIs.

---

## 9.2 Classification in the PDF

| Type | Examples in PDF | Systemic absorption |
|---|---|---|
| **Systemic antacids** | Sodium bicarbonate, sodium citrate | Significant systemic absorption |
| **Non-systemic antacids** | Magnesium hydroxide, aluminum hydroxide, calcium salts | Primarily act within GI tract; systemic effects can still occur depending on agent |

---

# 10. Sodium-Containing Antacids

## 10.1 Sodium bicarbonate

### PDF-derived

```text
NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2
```

The PDF emphasizes rapid neutralization and production of CO2.

### Clinical interpretation

CO2 generation can cause:

- Belching.
- Abdominal distension.
- Gastric fullness.

The PDF states that CO2 can stretch an ulcer and perforate it. **That is not a standard modern contraindication and should not be memorized as a routine rule.** More clinically relevant concerns with systemic sodium bicarbonate are sodium load and metabolic alkalosis in susceptible patients.

## 10.2 Sodium citrate

The PDF contrasts sodium citrate with bicarbonate because it does not produce the same amount of gastric CO2 on direct acid neutralization.

Systemically absorbed sodium-containing preparations can contribute to **sodium load and fluid/BP-related problems**, so they are not preferred in patients in whom sodium load is undesirable.

---

# 11. Calcium-Containing Antacids

### Examples

- Calcium carbonate.
- Calcium bicarbonate is listed in the PDF; clinically, **calcium carbonate** is the important common antacid.

### PDF-derived adverse effect

With frequent use, the handout describes **milk-alkali syndrome**.

### Milk-alkali syndrome - classic triad

```text
Excess calcium + absorbable alkali
          ↓
├── Hypercalcemia
├── Metabolic alkalosis
└── Renal impairment
```

### Rebound acid secretion

The PDF describes calcium-containing antacids as producing particularly prominent rebound acid secretion after discontinuation. The exact degree of rebound varies by preparation and dose; the exam concept is that **calcium carbonate can produce rebound acidity**.

### Renal disease correction

The PDF calls calcium antacid the "drug of choice in renal failure" because of osteoporosis/phosphate-related reasoning.

This is **too broad for modern clinical practice**. In CKD, antacid selection depends on renal function, phosphate balance, calcium burden, and the indication; calcium-containing agents are not universally the default choice and chronic calcium loading can be problematic.

---

# 12. Magnesium and Aluminum Antacids

**PDF source:** printed pp. 124-125 (PDF pp. 5-6)

## 12.1 Core comparison

| Feature | Magnesium salts (e.g. Mg(OH)2) | Aluminum salts (e.g. Al(OH)3) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Relatively fast | Slower, more sustained |
| GI motility | Increases | Decreases |
| Typical bowel effect | **Diarrhea** | **Constipation** |
| Main exam mnemonic | **MD = Magnesium Diarrhea** | Aluminum → constipation |

### Combination principle

Magnesium + aluminum preparations are commonly combined because:

- Magnesium tends to cause diarrhea.
- Aluminum tends to cause constipation.
- Their opposing GI effects can partially offset each other.

### Aluminum toxicity / adverse effects

The PDF lists:

- Hypophosphatemia → impaired bone mineralization/osteomalacia risk.
- Encephalopathy.

These are particularly important with **chronic exposure and impaired renal function**.

### Renal failure pearl

Magnesium-containing antacids can accumulate in significant renal impairment; aluminum-containing agents can also accumulate with chronic use. Therefore **renal failure is not a setting in which one should casually prescribe standard antacid mixtures without considering renal function**.

# 13. Ulcer-Protective Drugs

**PDF source:** printed p. 126 (PDF p. 7)

## 13.1 General mechanism

The PDF describes ulcer-protective agents as drugs that:

- Form a protective covering over the ulcer.
- Polymerize in an acidic environment.
- Bind to denuded/dead proteins and cells at the ulcer base.

The major examples are:

- **Sucralfate**
- **Colloidal bismuth salts**
  - Bismuth subacetate.
  - Bismuth subsalicylate.

---

# 14. Sucralfate

### Composition mnemonic from the PDF

> **SUCR = sucrose; AL = aluminum; FATE = sulfate**

### Mechanism

In an acidic environment, sucralfate forms a viscous polymer that adheres to damaged mucosa/ulcer bases and creates a protective barrier.

### Administration-spacing pearl from the PDF

The handout emphasizes separation from:

- Other medications (example given: phenytoin/ranitidine) by about **120 minutes**.
- Food by about **60 minutes**.
- Antacids by about **30 minutes**.

Exact spacing can vary by co-administered drug, so the practical principle is:

> **Sucralfate can interfere with absorption of other oral drugs; separate administration when appropriate.**

### Adverse effect

- **Constipation** - consistent with its aluminum component.

---

# 15. Colloidal Bismuth Salts

### Examples

- Bismuth subacetate.
- Bismuth subsalicylate.

### Important property

Bismuth compounds have activity against **H. pylori** and are therefore incorporated into eradication regimens.

### Adverse effects / exam pearl

- **Black tongue**.
- **Black stool**.

The PDF also lists osteoporosis as an adverse effect "like aluminum." This is **not a standard teaching point and should not be memorized as a routine adverse effect of bismuth**. Clinically important toxicity with prolonged/high-dose exposure can instead include neurologic toxicity, and bismuth subsalicylate also has salicylate-related considerations.

# 16. Helicobacter pylori Pharmacotherapy

**PDF source:** printed pp. 126-127 (PDF pp. 7-8)

## 16.1 PDF mnemonic for antibiotics

The handout gives **TALCM**:

- **T** = Tetracycline
- **A** = Amoxicillin
- **L** = Levofloxacin
- **C** = Clarithromycin
- **M** = Metronidazole

It also notes that nitroimidazoles other than metronidazole (e.g. tinidazole/ornidazole) may appear in some regimens.

### Important interpretation

This is a **drug pool**, not a statement that all five should routinely be combined.

---

## 16.2 Core treatment principles in the PDF

- Do not use a single antibiotic alone.
- Use at least two antibiotics in combination with acid suppression.
- PDF gives a typical duration of **10-14 days**, with **14 days** as the common duration.

These principles remain broadly useful, but the exact regimen has changed substantially because of antimicrobial resistance.

---

## 16.3 Current first-line approach - major update

The 2024 ACG guideline changed the emphasis of empiric *H. pylori* therapy.

For treatment-naive adults, the guideline recommends **optimized bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT) for 14 days** as a principal empiric regimen when susceptibility is unknown. [9,10]

### Optimized bismuth quadruple therapy

```text
PPI twice daily
+
Bismuth subcitrate/subsalicylate
+
Tetracycline 500 mg four times daily
+
Metronidazole 500 mg three or four times daily

→ 14 days
```

The 2024 ACG guidance specifically advises **against empiric PPI-clarithromycin triple therapy unless clarithromycin susceptibility has been demonstrated**. [9]

### Why?

- Clarithromycin resistance substantially reduces eradication success.
- Levofloxacin resistance is also important.

Therefore:

> **Do not treat the older PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin/metronidazole triple regimen as the universal empiric standard.**

---

## 16.4 PDF triple regimens

The PDF gives two three-drug regimens:

### PCM

- **P** = PPI
- **C** = Clarithromycin
- **M** = Metronidazole

### PCA

- **P** = PPI
- **C** = Clarithromycin
- **A** = Amoxicillin

- PDF duration: **14 days**.

### Current status of these regimens

These should be understood as **historical / susceptibility-dependent regimens**, not automatic empiric first-line therapy.

The modern ACG guideline states that PPI-clarithromycin triple therapy should be used only when **clarithromycin susceptibility is demonstrated**. [9,10]

---

## 16.5 PDF quadruple regimen

The PDF's mnemonic **PBMT**:

- **P** = PPI
- **B** = Bismuth
- **M** = Metronidazole
- **T** = Tetracycline

- Duration: **14 days**.
- PDF specifically associates it with clarithromycin-resistant cases.

### Modern interpretation

This has evolved into the modern **optimized bismuth quadruple therapy**, which is now a major empiric first-line option, not merely a rescue option for clarithromycin resistance. [9,10]

---

## 16.6 Other modern regimens

The 2024 ACG guideline also discusses:

- **Rifabutin-based triple therapy**.
- **Vonoprazan + amoxicillin dual therapy**.
- **Vonoprazan + amoxicillin + clarithromycin triple therapy** in appropriate susceptibility/clinical contexts.
- Levofloxacin-containing regimens when susceptibility supports their use.

The major exam-level principle is:

> **Choose therapy according to resistance/susceptibility and prior antibiotic exposure; avoid empiric clarithromycin- or levofloxacin-containing therapy when resistance is likely and susceptibility is unknown.** [9]

---

## 16.7 Test of cure

A major modern addition to the PDF is the need to **confirm eradication** after treatment.

The ACG 2024 guidance recommends testing with:

- Urea breath test, or
- Stool antigen test, or
- Biopsy-based testing when clinically appropriate.

Timing:

- Test **at least 4 weeks after completing antibiotics**.
- Hold **PPI/PCAB for at least 2 weeks** before testing to reduce false-negative results. [9]

---

## 16.8 Why eradicate H. pylori?

### PDF-derived / clinically important benefits

Eradication reduces risk of:

- Peptic ulcer disease/recurrence.
- *H. pylori*-associated gastric malignancy risk.
- **MALT lymphoma** associated with *H. pylori*.

### GERD correction

The PDF lists GERD as a risk reduced by eradication.

This is **too broad**. H. pylori eradication is not recommended simply as a treatment for typical GERD, and the effect of eradication on GERD symptoms is unpredictable. The historical ACG *H. pylori* guidance specifically notes that patients with typical GERD symptoms without peptic-ulcer disease do not need *H. pylori* testing solely for GERD. [11]

### Barrett's esophagus

The PDF says eradication does not reverse Barrett metaplasia because metaplastic change has already occurred.

The key concept is reasonable:

> **Eradication of H. pylori is not a treatment that reverses established Barrett's esophagus.**

# 17. Alginic Acid

**PDF source:** printed p. 127 (PDF p. 8)

### PDF-derived mechanism

The handout describes alginic acid as making a protective layer so that refluxed acid is less likely to contact the esophageal mucosa.

### Current correction

The clinically useful concept is a **physical reflux barrier / raft**, not an "antifoaming drug." Alginates are used for symptom relief in reflux by forming a floating barrier over gastric contents.

Therefore:

- **Alginate = reflux barrier/raft-forming therapy.**
- **Simethicone = antifoaming agent.**

Do not interchange these mechanisms.

# 21. Stimulant Laxatives

**PDF source:** printed p. 128 (PDF p. 9)

## 21.1 Drugs

- **Bisacodyl**
- **Senna**
- Cascara
- **Prucalopride** is listed here in the PDF, but this classification needs correction (see below).

### Mechanism of classical stimulant laxatives

- Direct stimulation of colonic secretory/motor activity.
- Increased propulsion and bowel movement.

### Clinical use

The AGA-ACG chronic idiopathic constipation guideline supports:

- **Bisacodyl/sodium picosulfate** for short-term or rescue treatment.
- **Senna** as an evidence-based option, although the certainty of evidence is lower than for PEG. [12]

### Major correction: prucalopride is not a stimulant laxative

Prucalopride is a **selective 5-HT4 receptor agonist** that enhances intestinal motility through enteric serotonergic pathways.

Therefore:

> **Prucalopride belongs with serotonin-4 agonists / prokinetic agents, not with classical direct stimulant laxatives such as senna and bisacodyl.** [12]

## 21.2 "-pride" clue

The PDF correctly points toward:

- **Prucalopride**
- **Mosapride**

These are associated with **5-HT4 agonism**, although their clinical indications and pharmacologic details differ.

## 21.3 Pregnancy correction

The PDF calls stimulant laxatives absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy because of presumed uterine-contraction/abortion risk.

That is **too absolute and outdated**.

Standard stimulant laxatives such as senna/bisacodyl are not universally contraindicated during pregnancy. Selection depends on the clinical situation, prior therapies, dose, and obstetric context.

For exam purposes, the safer general memory aid is:

> **Bulk-forming agents are commonly favored first for constipation in pregnancy; stimulant agents are not an automatic abortion-producing contraindication.**

---

## 21.4 "Spastic colon" correction

The PDF lists spastic colon as an important contraindication because stimulant laxatives can produce abdominal cramping.

A more precise current statement is:

- Stimulant laxatives can cause **abdominal cramping, urgency, and diarrhea**.
- "Spastic colon" is not best treated as a universal absolute contraindication in modern pharmacology.

# 26. Newer Drugs for Constipation

**PDF source:** printed pp. 129-130 (PDF pp. 10-11)

## 26.1 Lubiprostone

### PDF-derived

- Increases chloride secretion.
- Listed as similar to misoprostol.
- Described as a prostaglandin E1 analogue.

### Current correction

Lubiprostone is a **chloride channel activator**. It is structurally related to prostaglandin E1, but its clinically important mechanism is activation of intestinal chloride secretion rather than simply acting as a generic prostaglandin analogue.

Current labeling includes treatment of:

- Chronic idiopathic constipation in adults.
- Opioid-induced constipation in specified adults.
- IBS-C in adult women. [13]

### Common adverse effects

- Nausea.
- Diarrhea.
- Abdominal discomfort/distension.

---

## 26.2 Linaclotide

- **Guanylate cyclase-C (GC-C) agonist.**
- Activates intestinal GC-C.
- ↑ intracellular cGMP.
- Promotes chloride and water secretion and reduces fluid absorption.

Used for both **chronic idiopathic constipation** and **IBS-C** in appropriate patients.

---

## 26.3 Plecanatide

- **GC-C agonist.**
- Similar overall secretory concept to linaclotide.
- Used for **CIC** and **IBS-C**.

---

## 26.4 Tenapanor

### PDF-derived

- "Sodium secretion increaser."
- Inhibits a sodium-hydrogen antiporter.
- Increases sodium/water delivery into feces.

### Current pharmacology

Tenapanor inhibits the intestinal **Na+/H+ exchanger 3 (NHE3)**, reducing sodium absorption and thereby increasing stool water. It is specifically used for **IBS-C** in adults in jurisdictions where approved. [14]

# 35. Exam-Oriented One-Liners

## Acid suppression

- **Most powerful conventional acid suppression:** PPIs.
- **Final common pathway of gastric acid secretion:** H+/K+-ATPase.
- **PPI activation:** acidic parietal-cell canaliculus.
- **PPI binding:** irreversible inhibition of activated proton pumps.
- **PPI administration:** usually 30-60 min before a meal for GERD. [1]
- **Classic major CYP inhibitor H2 blocker:** cimetidine.
- **Classic cimetidine adverse effect:** gynecomastia + drug interactions.
- **Historical H2 blocker now withdrawn/suspended in major jurisdictions:** ranitidine. [7,8]
- **Classic PPI-clopidogrel interaction:** avoid omeprazole/esomeprazole because of CYP2C19 inhibition. [2]
- **P-CAB prototype:** vonoprazan.
- **P-CAB mechanism:** potassium-competitive blockade of H+/K+-ATPase.

## Mucosal protection

- **PGE1 analogue:** misoprostol.
- **Classic NSAID-ulcer prevention drug:** misoprostol (but PPIs are often preferred clinically).
- **Ulcer-coating drug:** sucralfate.
- **Bismuth pearl:** black tongue + black stool.

## H. pylori

- **Never use single-agent antibiotic therapy** for eradication.
- **Modern empiric first-line anchor:** optimized bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days when susceptibility is unknown. [9,10]
- **Avoid empiric clarithromycin triple therapy unless clarithromycin susceptibility is demonstrated.** [9,10]
- **Confirm eradication:** urea breath test / stool antigen / biopsy-based test as appropriate. [9]
- **Test-of-cure timing:** at least 4 weeks after antibiotics and off PPI/PCAB for at least 2 weeks. [9]

## Laxatives

- **Bulk-forming:** psyllium/ispaghula.
- **Fast, evidence-supported osmotic option for CIC:** PEG. [12]
- **Stimulant laxatives:** bisacodyl, senna.
- **5-HT4 agonist:** prucalopride.
- **Chloride secretagogue:** lubiprostone.
- **GC-C agonists:** linaclotide, plecanatide.
- **NHE3 inhibitor:** tenapanor.
- **Hepatic encephalopathy:** lactulose.
- **Melanosis coli:** think chronic anthraquinone laxative exposure, especially senna.

## Hepatobiliary

- **PBC mainstay:** UDCA 13-15 mg/kg/day. [15,16]
- **Selected cholesterol gallstones:** UDCA may dissolve stones, but it is not a universal gallstone therapy.
- **Short bowel syndrome - GLP-2 analogue:** teduglutide.
- **Somatostatin analogue reducing intestinal secretion:** octreotide.
