# 1. Cytotoxic Drugs — Core Principles

## 1.1 Basic principle

[PDF]

Cytotoxic anticancer drugs preferentially damage **rapidly dividing cells**. The therapeutic problem is that many normal tissues also contain rapidly proliferating cells, so these drugs can be highly toxic.

### Common toxicities — mnemonic: **BHARAT MATA**

| Toxicity | Key point |
|---|---|
| **B**one-marrow suppression | Myelosuppression → anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia |
| **H** / mucosal injury | Gastrointestinal mucosal injury and ulceration |
| **A**lopecia | Hair loss |
| **R/T** / reproductive toxicity | Teratogenicity; infertility from gonadal toxicity |
| **A**zoospermia / anovulation | Male and female infertility may occur |

> **Exam frame:** The mnemonic is useful for remembering the broad toxicity pattern, but individual drugs differ substantially in which toxicity dominates.

## 1.2 Bone-marrow suppression

[PDF]

Consequences of myelosuppression:

- ↓ RBCs → **anemia** → erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy may be used in selected patients.
- ↓ WBCs / neutrophils → **neutropenia** → G-CSF such as **filgrastim** may be used when indicated.
- ↓ platelets → **thrombocytopenia**.
- **Oprelvekin** is an IL-11 analogue that historically stimulated platelet production.
- The PDF notes injectable administration for the listed growth-factor therapies.

### Growth-factor pearls

- **Filgrastim = G-CSF** (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor).
- **Oprelvekin = recombinant IL-11 analogue**.

[CURRENT] Oprelvekin is a historical/legacy drug in many modern oncology settings rather than a routine contemporary solution for chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia. The FDA Purple Book lists the Neumega/oprelvekin product as **discontinued**. Do not generalize the PDF's oprelvekin statement into a recommendation for contemporary routine practice.

**External verification:** [FDA Purple Book — Neumega (oprelvekin)](https://purplebooksearch.fda.gov/index.cfm?blaNo=103694&event=productdetails)

### Correction to the PDF's “VBC” mnemonic

[PDF] The source states that bone-marrow suppression occurs with essentially all cytotoxic drugs except **VBC = vincristine, bleomycin, chlorambucil**.

[CORRECTION] **Chlorambucil should not be included as a marrow-sparing exception.** Bone-marrow suppression is a common adverse effect of chlorambucil. Vincristine and bleomycin are comparatively less myelosuppressive than many cytotoxic agents, but chlorambucil is clearly myelosuppressive.

**External verification:** [DailyMed — chlorambucil](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=58a3c995-5ad6-465d-8437-5970c9088213); [NCI — chlorambucil](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/treatment/drugs/chlorambucil)

## 1.3 Mucosal toxicity

[PDF]

Severe mucosal injury can occur with cytotoxic therapy.

The source lists **palifermin** as treatment for severe gastrointestinal mucosal injury/diarrhea.

[CORRECTION] The high-yield, evidence-based use of **palifermin** is prevention/reduction of **severe oral mucositis** in selected patients with hematologic malignancies receiving myelotoxic therapy with **autologous hematopoietic stem-cell support**. It is not simply a general treatment for chemotherapy-induced severe diarrhea.

Palifermin is a recombinant keratinocyte growth factor that promotes epithelial repair.

**External verification:** [DailyMed — Kepivance (palifermin), revised 2025](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=426f6e64-2c20-4a61-6d65-7320426f6e64)

## 1.4 Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS)

[PDF]

### Pathophysiology

Rapid destruction of tumor cells releases intracellular contents into the circulation:

- Nucleic acids → uric acid → **hyperuricemia**.
- K⁺ → **hyperkalemia**.
- Phosphate → **hyperphosphatemia**.
- Phosphate binds calcium → **secondary hypocalcemia**.

Major clinical consequences include:

- Acute kidney injury from urate and/or calcium-phosphate precipitation.
- Cardiac arrhythmias from hyperkalemia and other electrolyte abnormalities.
- Neuromuscular/neurologic complications from electrolyte disturbances.

### Management framework

| Problem | Source-oriented management | Current clarification |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperuricemia | **Allopurinol** | Prevents formation of new uric acid; does not rapidly remove existing uric acid. |
| High-risk or established hyperuricemia | **Rasburicase** | Rapidly converts uric acid to allantoin; used in selected high-risk/established TLS. |
| Hyperphosphatemia + hyperkalemia / severe metabolic complications | Dialysis may be required | Renal replacement therapy is used for refractory/severe electrolyte abnormalities or kidney failure. |
| Hypocalcemia | Calcium listed in PDF | **Do not routinely correct asymptomatic hypocalcemia** in TLS because calcium-phosphate precipitation may worsen tissue calcification. Treat symptomatic/severe hypocalcemia when clinically indicated. |

[CORRECTION] **Pegloticase is not a standard first-line TLS drug.** The modern TLS framework centers on hydration, risk assessment, allopurinol and/or rasburicase, frequent laboratory monitoring, and treatment of dangerous electrolyte abnormalities.

[VERIFY] Contemporary expert guidance emphasizes hydration and risk-adapted uric-acid control; rasburicase is particularly useful when rapid reduction of pre-existing uric acid is needed. A 2025 British Society for Haematology update and a 2023 US expert-consensus statement provide contemporary guidance.

**External verification:**

- [British Society for Haematology — updated TLS guideline, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40903841/)
- [US expert consensus on TLS prophylaxis and management, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37579533/)
- [eviQ — prevention of tumour lysis syndrome, reviewed 2025](https://www.eviq.org.au/clinical-resources/side-effect-and-toxicity-management/prophylaxis-and-treatment/108-prevention-of-tumour-lysis-syndrome)

# 2. Classification of Cytotoxic Drugs by Cell Cycle

[PDF]

Two broad groups are emphasized:

1. **Cell-cycle non-specific (CCNS)** drugs — can act in multiple/all phases.
2. **Cell-cycle specific (CCS)** drugs — preferentially act during particular phases.

## 2.1 Cell-cycle non-specific drugs

### Major groups

- **Alkylating agents**
- **Platinum compounds**

### Core mechanism

Direct DNA damage. Because DNA is present throughout the cell cycle, these drugs are classified as cell-cycle non-specific.

### Platinum compounds

- Cisplatin
- Carboplatin
- Oxaliplatin

### Alkylating agents emphasized in the source

- Mechlorethamine
- Dacarbazine
- Procarbazine
- Melphalan
- Cyclophosphamide
- Ifosfamide
- Chlorambucil
- Busulfan
- Carmustine
- Lomustine

> **Terminology note:** Dacarbazine and procarbazine are often grouped with alkylating-like/triazene agents in pharmacology teaching. The exam-focused source places them under alkylating agents; that organizational choice is retained.

## 2.2 Cell-cycle specific drugs

The PDF distributes the following across phases:

| Cell-cycle phase | Representative drugs in the source |
|---|---|
| **G1** | CDK4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib — block G1→S transition |
| **S** | Hydroxyurea; antimetabolites; many topoisomerase I-directed agents are functionally S-phase dependent |
| **G2** | Bleomycin is taught as G2-specific in the source; topoisomerase II-directed agents are often associated with G2/S activity |
| **M** | Microtubule inhibitors — vinca alkaloids and taxanes |

[CORRECTION] Cell-cycle specificity is a useful exam framework, but several drug classes do **not** map cleanly onto a single phase in vivo. In particular, “topoisomerase inhibitors = G2/S” is more nuanced than a single-phase rule.

### Mnemonic shown in the source

- **M phase:** microtubule inhibitors.
- **S phase:** hydroxyurea + antimetabolites.
- **G1:** CDK4/6 inhibitors block the G1→S transition.

# 3. Alkylating Agents

## 3.1 Mechlorethamine

[PDF]

- Cell-cycle non-specific.
- Alkylates DNA.
- The source describes transfer of an alkyl group and binding at **N7 of guanine**, causing DNA damage.
- Used historically in **Hodgkin lymphoma** regimens.

### Hodgkin lymphoma: MOPP

**MOPP** =

- **M**echlorethamine
- **O**ncovin (vincristine)
- **P**rocarbazine
- **P**rednisone/prednisolone

[PDF] The source notes **ABVD** as the drug-of-choice regimen for Hodgkin lymphoma in its exam framework.

> **Current-context note:** Contemporary Hodgkin lymphoma management is risk- and stage-specific, and treatment has evolved beyond a simple “ABVD = drug of choice” rule. The regimen is retained here because it is explicitly present in the source.

## 3.2 Procarbazine

[PDF]

Important adverse effects:

- **Azoospermia / infertility**
- **Depression**
- **Disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol**

These remain useful exam associations.

## 3.3 Melphalan

[PDF]

- Major use highlighted: **multiple myeloma**.

## 3.4 Cyclophosphamide

### Uses listed in the source

[PDF]

- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma — **R-CHOP** regimen.
- CLL — historical **FCR** regimen.
- Burkitt lymphoma.
- Breast cancer.
- Autoimmune disease — e.g. **granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener disease)**.

### R-CHOP

- **R**ituximab
- **C**yclophosphamide
- **H**ydroxydaunorubicin = doxorubicin
- **O**ncovin = vincristine
- **P**rednisolone/prednisone

### Metabolism

Cyclophosphamide is converted in the liver to active intermediates, with formation of:

- **4-hydroxycyclophosphamide / aldophosphamide** → active antineoplastic pathway.
- **Acrolein** → urotoxic metabolite responsible for hemorrhagic cystitis.

### Toxicity

- **Hemorrhagic cystitis** → painful hematuria.

### Drugs causing hemorrhagic cystitis highlighted by the source

- Cyclophosphamide
- Ifosfamide

### Prevention/treatment: Mesna

- **Mesna** binds/detoxifies acrolein in the urinary tract.
- The source shows oral prevention and IV treatment concepts.

> **High-yield mechanism:** Cyclophosphamide → acrolein → hemorrhagic cystitis → **mesna**.

## 3.5 Chlorambucil vs busulfan

| Feature | Chlorambucil | Busulfan |
|---|---|---|
| Main cellular target emphasized in source | Peripheral lymphocytes | Bone marrow / myeloid lineage |
| Marrow sparing | Source describes relative sparing | Major marrow toxicity |
| Uses | CLL; ALL listed in source | CML; AML listed in source |
| Other high-yield association | — | Hepatic veno-occlusive disease / sinusoidal obstruction syndrome |

[CORRECTION] Chlorambucil is **not** genuinely “does not cause bone-marrow suppression.” Myelosuppression is a prominent adverse effect; the source's comparison is best understood as a teaching contrast rather than a literal safety statement.

### Busulfan — hepatic veno-occlusive disease

[PDF]

- Can produce a **Budd–Chiari-like picture** due to hepatic venous injury.
- Also termed hepatic veno-occlusive disease / sinusoidal obstruction syndrome.
- The source lists **defibrotide** as treatment/antidotal therapy.

> **Exam pearl:** Busulfan → **myelosuppression + pulmonary toxicity + hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome**.

# 5. Platinum Compounds

## 5.1 Drugs

- **Cisplatin**
- **Carboplatin**
- **Oxaliplatin**

[PDF] Classified as **cell-cycle non-specific** DNA-damaging drugs.

## 5.2 Core pharmacology

Platinum agents form DNA adducts/cross-links and interfere with DNA replication and transcription.

## 5.3 Uses shown in the source

- Lung cancer
- Osteosarcoma
- Gastrointestinal cancers: esophagus, stomach, colorectal, anal
- Head and neck cancers
- Urogenital cancers: testicular, cervical
- Retinoblastoma — **carboplatin** is highlighted

### Combination therapy

- **Platinum + 5-FU** → gastrointestinal and head-and-neck cancers in the source.
- The source calls platinum agents **radiosensitizers**.

[CORRECTION] The statement that lung cancer is simply “the most common cancer” is not a reliable universal current teaching point. Keep the lung-cancer association, but do not treat that wording as a current global incidence ranking.

## 5.4 Platinum toxicity comparison

| Drug | High-yield toxicity association |
|---|---|
| **Cisplatin** | **Nephrotoxicity**, **ototoxicity**, highly emetogenic; also neuropathy |
| **Carboplatin** | Less nephrotoxic/ototoxic/emetic than cisplatin; **myelosuppression** is prominent |
| **Oxaliplatin** | **Peripheral sensory neuropathy**, especially cold-triggered acute symptoms and cumulative sensory neuropathy |

### Exam-style ranking from the source

- **Most emetogenic:** cisplatin
- **Most ototoxic:** cisplatin
- **Most nephrotoxic:** cisplatin
- **Most neurotoxic:** oxaliplatin
- **Least nephro/oto/emetic toxicity among the classical three:** carboplatin

[VERIFY] Cisplatin's ototoxicity involves injury to inner-ear structures/hair cells; oxaliplatin is characteristically associated with sensory neuropathy.

**External verification:** [NIDCD/NIH — cisplatin and inner-ear hair-cell toxicity](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/research/labs/section-sensory-cell-biology)

## 5.5 Cisplatin nephrotoxicity — supportive protection

[PDF]

- **Amifostine** is listed as a nephroprotective agent.
- Mannitol + chloride diuresis is listed as an older strategy to enhance excretion.

[CORRECTION] **Amifostine is a nephroprotective agent, not a literal antidote to cisplatin.** Forced diuresis is not a universal routine treatment protocol and should not be memorized as a stand-alone modern antidote.

# 7. S-Phase Drugs and Antimetabolites

## 7.1 Hydroxyurea

### Mechanism

- Inhibits **ribonucleotide reductase**.
- ↓ deoxyribonucleotide availability → inhibits DNA synthesis.

### Uses listed in the source

- **Polycythemia vera** — highlighted as DOC in the source.
- Essential thrombocytosis.
- Myelofibrosis.
- **Sickle-cell disease** — major use.

### Sickle-cell disease mechanism

Hydroxyurea increases **fetal hemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂)**.

→ Compensates functionally for the β-globin defect.

### Other sickle-cell drugs in the source

#### Voxelotor

[PDF]

- Prevents HbS polymerization.

[CURRENT/CORRECTION] **Voxelotor should not be learned as a current available therapy.** The FDA announced voluntary withdrawal of Oxbryta (voxelotor) from the market in September 2024 because subsequent data showed an unfavorable benefit-risk profile; FDA advised clinicians to stop prescribing it.

**External verification:** [FDA — voluntary withdrawal of Oxbryta/voxelotor](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-alerting-patients-and-health-care-professionals-about-voluntary-withdrawal-oxbryta-market-due)

#### Crizanlizumab

- Monoclonal antibody against **P-selectin**.
- Reduces adhesion of blood cells and helps reduce vaso-occlusive events.

# 9. Folate Analogues

## 9.1 Drugs

- **Methotrexate**
- **Pemetrexed**
- **Pralatrexate**
- **Trimetrexate**

### Mechanism

- Inhibit **dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)** and/or folate-dependent pathways.
- ↓ tetrahydrofolate → impaired synthesis of nucleotides → ↓ DNA synthesis.

## 9.2 Antidote / rescue

### Folinic acid = leucovorin = citrovorum factor

- Active folate derivative.
- Used as **leucovorin rescue** in appropriate methotrexate toxicity settings.
- Can reduce methotrexate toxicity.
- Leucovorin is also combined with **5-FU** in several chemotherapy regimens to enhance 5-FU effect.

[PDF] The source also mentions folic acid + vitamin B12 as an alternative concept.

[CORRECTION] Ordinary folic acid is not a substitute for leucovorin in clinically significant methotrexate toxicity. Routine folate/B12 supplementation has a different role in selected methotrexate regimens (for example, with pemetrexed) and should not be conflated with **leucovorin rescue**.

## 9.3 Pemetrexed

- **Mesothelioma** is highlighted in the source.
- Also an important modern drug in selected non-small-cell lung cancer regimens.

## 9.4 Pralatrexate

- Highlighted use: **cutaneous T-cell lymphoma**.

## 9.5 Trimetrexate

- The source associates it with **Pneumocystis jirovecii** infection.

## 9.6 Methotrexate — key exam uses

### Drug-of-choice association in the source

- **Choriocarcinoma / gestational trophoblastic disease** — β-hCG emphasized.

### Autoimmune uses

- **Rheumatoid arthritis** — source highlights as DOC.
- Psoriatic arthritis.

### Methotrexate toxicity

- **Bone-marrow suppression**
- **Hepatotoxicity**
- **Pneumonitis**
- Mucositis and other folate-related toxicities may occur.

### Monitoring in the source

- CBC
- Liver-function tests
- Chest X-ray / respiratory assessment

> **Clinical caveat:** Monitoring schedules are indication- and regimen-specific. A chest X-ray is not a substitute for clinical assessment of pulmonary symptoms.

### Management of severe methotrexate toxicity

- **Leucovorin rescue**
- **Glucarpidase** — enzymatically breaks down methotrexate; particularly relevant in severe high-dose methotrexate toxicity with delayed clearance.
- The source also mentions **forced alkaline diuresis**.

[CORRECTION] Routine urine alkalinization/forced alkaline diuresis is not the core modern “antidote.” Modern management emphasizes hydration, urine alkalinization as appropriate to facilitate high-dose methotrexate elimination, leucovorin rescue, drug-level monitoring, and **glucarpidase** for selected severe delayed-clearance cases.

### Mechanism

### Toxicity — hand-foot syndrome

- **Palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia**.
- More prominent with **capecitabine**.

# 13. Purine Analogues

## 13.1 Thiopurines

- **6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP)**
- **6-Thioguanine (6-TG)**

### Mechanism / activation

- Converted by **HGPRT** into active thiopurine nucleotides.
- Interfere with purine metabolism and DNA synthesis.

### Lesch-Nyhan syndrome

- **HGPRT deficiency**.
- The source notes that these drugs become ineffective because activation through HGPRT is impaired.

### Uses

- The source mentions resistant lymphomas; this is retained as a source-derived historical association.
- 6-MP is particularly important in **ALL maintenance therapy** in modern oncology, although that specific use is not emphasized in the PDF.

## 13.2 Azathioprine

### General

- **Prodrug → 6-mercaptopurine**.
- Primarily an **immunosuppressive/antimetabolite** drug rather than a standard contemporary anticancer drug.

### Uses in the source

- Autoimmune diseases.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease** (Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis) as a drug-of-choice association.

### Metabolism

The source describes:

- Liver inactivation involving **xanthine oxidase**.
- Cellular metabolism involving **TPMT**.

### Allopurinol interaction

Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase → reduced metabolism of azathioprine/6-MP → increased thiopurine toxicity.

[CURRENT] Dose reduction with concomitant allopurinol is substantial. Contemporary references commonly recommend reducing azathioprine/6-MP to approximately **25–33% of the usual dose** (or a 60–75% reduction), with close blood-count monitoring.

**External verification:** [FDA pharmacogenomic/drug-label information — azathioprine, TPMT/NUDT15 and allopurinol](https://www.fda.gov/media/124784/download?attachment=); [eviQ — tumour lysis prophylaxis also summarizes the 60–75% thiopurine reduction interaction](https://www.eviq.org.au/clinical-resources/side-effect-and-toxicity-management/prophylaxis-and-treatment/108-prevention-of-tumour-lysis-syndrome)

### Pharmacogenomic update — TPMT + NUDT15

[CURRENT]

Severe thiopurine myelosuppression is not explained by TPMT alone. **NUDT15** is also a clinically important pharmacogenomic determinant, especially in populations in which NUDT15 loss-of-function variants are more prevalent.

- TPMT deficiency → increased active thiopurine metabolites → myelosuppression.
- NUDT15 deficiency → increased risk of severe thiopurine-associated leukopenia/neutropenia/myelosuppression.
- Genotype/phenotype information does **not** replace serial CBC monitoring.

**External verification:** [FDA — pharmacogenomic biomarkers in drug labeling](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/science-and-research-drugs/table-pharmacogenomic-biomarkers-drug-labeling); [CPIC — thiopurines, TPMT/NUDT15 2025 update](https://files.cpicpgx.org/data/guideline/publication/thiopurines/2026/41618934.pdf)

# 15. Microtubule Inhibitors

## 15.1 General mechanism

[PDF]

- Predominantly act during **M phase**.
- Interfere with microtubule dynamics and arrest mitosis.

### Two major groups

1. **Vinca alkaloids**
2. **Taxanes**

## 15.2 Vinca alkaloids

### Drugs

- Vincristine
- Vinblastine
- Vinorelbine

### Mechanism

- Inhibit **microtubule polymerization**.

## 15.3 Taxanes

### Drugs

- Paclitaxel
- Docetaxel
- Cabazitaxel

### Mechanism

- Promote microtubule stabilization and inhibit **depolymerization**.

## 15.4 Toxicity comparison

### Vincristine

- **Most neurotoxic** among the classic vinca alkaloids.
- Peripheral neuropathy.
- **Foot drop** from peroneal nerve involvement is highlighted.

### Vinblastine

- More prominent **bone-marrow suppression** than vincristine.

### Taxanes

- **Glove-and-stocking peripheral neuropathy**.
- Hypersensitivity reactions and myelosuppression are also clinically important, although not emphasized in the PDF.

## 15.5 Other microtubule-active drugs in the source

### Eribulin and ixabepilone

- Breast cancer.

### Estramustine

- Prostate cancer.

### Colchicine

[PDF]

- Microtubule inhibitor.
- Used for **acute gout**.
- Inhibits leukocyte migration.

> **Important:** Colchicine is included here for its microtubule pharmacology but is **not** an anticancer cytotoxic drug in ordinary clinical use.

# 17. Antitumor Antibiotics

[PDF]

## 44.1 Mechanism

- Binds **cereblon**.
- Alters immune signaling.

  - IL-4
  - IL-6

The source also directly identifies **multiple myeloma**.

- **Pomalidomide**

## 44.4 Adverse effects

- **Peripheral neuropathy**, which may be persistent/permanent.
- **Constipation**.

## 44.5 Teratogenicity

- Historical Category X terminology is used in the source.
- Causes severe embryopathy including **phocomelia** (short/underdeveloped limbs).

### Enantiomer concept

The source emphasizes:

- One enantiomer associated with sedative effects.
- The other associated with teratogenic effects.
- **Interconversion between enantiomers (R ↔ S)** means administration of either form cannot be assumed to eliminate teratogenic risk.

[VERIFY] Thalidomide is a powerful human teratogen; pregnancy is contraindicated and even single-dose exposure can cause severe fetal harm.

# 46. Immunomodulators in Pregnancy

## Source statement

[PDF]

The final page gives a broad statement that immunostimulants are contraindicated in pregnancy because increased immunity can harm the fetus. For condyloma in pregnancy, the source recommends **cryotherapy** and says **do not give imiquimod**.

## Corrected current interpretation

[CORRECTION]

The blanket statement **“all immunostimulants are contraindicated in pregnancy” is too broad**. Pregnancy restrictions are **drug-specific**.

### Thalidomide

- **Contraindicated in pregnancy**.
- Powerful human teratogen.

### Imiquimod

- Pregnancy data are limited.
- CDC guidance states that imiquimod appears to pose low risk but **should be avoided until more data are available**.
- For anogenital warts during pregnancy, provider-administered approaches such as **cryotherapy** can be considered.

**External verification:** [CDC STI Treatment Guidelines — pregnancy and anogenital warts](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/rr/rr7004a1.htm); [DailyMed — thalidomide](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=2eda833b-1357-4ed4-a093-194524fcb061)

# 50. Important Corrections / OCR and Layout Issues Found in the PDF

This section is intentionally explicit so that an exam fact from the source is not accidentally converted into a false clinical rule.

| Source issue | Corrected interpretation |
|---|---|
| “All cytotoxics cause marrow suppression except VBC” | Vincristine and bleomycin are relatively marrow-sparing; **chlorambucil is myelosuppressive**. |
| Palifermin presented as treatment for severe diarrhea/GI mucosa | Palifermin's established high-yield role is **severe oral mucositis prevention/reduction** in selected autologous HSCT settings. |
| Pegloticase presented as routine resistant TLS therapy | Modern TLS management uses **allopurinol and/or rasburicase** with hydration and aggressive electrolyte management; pegloticase is not a standard TLS agent. |
| “All cytotoxic drugs are given by injection” appears in source context | False as a general rule; many are oral, including chlorambucil, procarbazine and temozolomide. |
| “Irinotecan” appears among topoisomerase II inhibitors | **Irinotecan = topoisomerase I inhibitor.** |
| Gilbert/Crigler-Najjar listed as absolute irinotecan contraindications | Reduced UGT1A1 activity increases toxicity risk; the oversimplified absolute-contraindication wording should not be used literally. |
| Cytarabine listed under hair-cell damage | Misaligned/incorrect; **cisplatin** damages cochlear hair cells, while cytarabine is associated with **cerebellar toxicity**. |
| Lapatinib included as anti-tumor monoclonal antibody | **Lapatinib is a small-molecule TKI** targeting HER2/EGFR. |
| Eculizumab/ravulizumab included among anti-tumor mAbs | They are **C5-targeting complement antibodies**, especially important in PNH and other complement disorders. |
| “Checkpoint inhibitors → cytokine storm” | Classic checkpoint-inhibitor toxicity is **immune-related adverse events**; CRS is particularly associated with CAR-T/T-cell engaging therapies. |
| “Aflibercept = aptamer” | **Aflibercept = recombinant VEGF-trap fusion protein**; pegaptanib is the classic VEGF-targeting RNA aptamer. |
| Tazemetostat described as a generic DNA-methylation inhibitor | Tazemetostat specifically inhibits **EZH2 histone methyltransferase**, reducing H3K27 methylation. |
| Glasdegib placed under BCC | **Glasdegib is a Smoothened inhibitor associated with AML + low-dose cytarabine**, not a BCC drug. |
| Iniparib included as a PARP inhibitor | **Iniparib is not a bona fide PARP inhibitor**; remove it from modern PARP lists. |
| “Obinutoclax” appears under BCL-2 inhibitors | Extraction is unreliable; **venetoclax** is the clearly established BCL-2 inhibitor represented here; “obinutuzumab” is a CD20 antibody. |
| Blanket statement: all immunostimulants contraindicated in pregnancy | Pregnancy restrictions are **agent-specific**. Thalidomide is clearly contraindicated; imiquimod should generally be avoided during pregnancy until more data are available. |
| ATRA section visually mixed with bleomycin skin-toxicity images | **Flagellate pigmentation belongs to bleomycin**, not ATRA. |
