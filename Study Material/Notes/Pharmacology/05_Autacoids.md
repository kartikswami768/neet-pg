# 1. HISTAMINE

**PDF coverage:** pages 1–3 (printed pp. 69–71)

The source begins the autacoid chapter with histamine, including histamine synthesis, H1 receptor actions in the CNS and periphery, and classification of H1 antihistamines. The first page visually presents these concepts in a two-column comparison of central versus peripheral H1 effects. **[PDF]**

## 1.1 Production of histamine

- **Precursor:** L-histidine.
- **Enzyme:** **Histidine decarboxylase** converts histidine to histamine.
- Histamine receptors in the source: **H1, H2, H3, H4**.
- All four are **G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)**. **[PDF + verified]**

### Receptor coupling — high-yield

| Receptor | Major G-protein coupling | High-yield effect |
|---|---|---|
| **H1** | Gq/11 | Allergy, vasodilation via endothelial signaling, bronchoconstriction, pruritus; CNS arousal |
| **H2** | Gs | Gastric acid secretion; cardiac stimulation |
| **H3** | Gi/o | Presynaptic autoreceptor/heteroreceptor in CNS; reduces neurotransmitter release |
| **H4** | Gi/o | Immune-cell chemotaxis and inflammatory signaling |

**[Verified update]** The uploaded PDF focuses almost entirely on **H1** and does not develop the H2–H4 pharmacology. Modern receptor coupling is summarized here so that the basic receptor map is not lost.

## 1.2 H1 receptor — CNS actions

**[PDF]**

Histaminergic neurons in the brain contribute to:

- **Wakefulness/arousal**
- Attention and cognitive functions, including memory-related processes
- Vestibular signaling associated with **nausea, vomiting, and vertigo**
- Motion sickness / seasickness concepts in the source are linked to H1-blocking therapy.

### Why first-generation H1 antihistamines cause sedation

First-generation H1 antihistamines cross the blood–brain barrier and block central H1 receptors. The source frames this as causing:

- Sedation
- Reduced alertness
- Memory impairment
- Anti-emetic action
- Anti-vertigo action

**[PDF + verified]** CNS penetration and sedation are characteristic of first-generation H1 antihistamines. First-generation agents also commonly have antimuscarinic and other off-target effects.

## 1.3 H1 receptor — peripheral actions

**[PDF]**

- Histamine is stored predominantly in **mast cells** in peripheral tissues.
- Mast-cell histamine contributes to **type I hypersensitivity/allergic reactions**.
- H1 activation contributes to vasodilation and the classic **triple response** framework used in teaching:
  - **Redness**
  - **Wheal**
  - **Flare**

**Exam frame:** H1 = major receptor for many immediate allergic manifestations.

## 1.4 H1 antihistamines — two generations

| Feature | 1st generation | 2nd generation |
|---|---|---|
| BBB penetration | **Yes** | Much less; generally limited |
| Sedation | Common | Much less / minimal with most agents |
| Anticholinergic effects | Common | Less |
| Central anti-emetic/anti-vertigo action | **Yes** | Limited |
| Typical use | Allergy + selected nausea/vertigo/motion sickness | Allergic rhinitis, urticaria and other allergic disorders |

**Important nuance:** The source says second-generation drugs “do NOT cross the BBB” and identifies **cetirizine as an exception**. A more accurate statement is that most second-generation agents have **limited CNS penetration**; cetirizine and levocetirizine can still cause somnolence in some patients. **[Verified update]**

---

## 1.5 First-generation antihistamines

### General features

**[PDF]**

- Cross the BBB.
- Can cause sedation and impairment of psychomotor performance.
- Also block muscarinic receptors to varying degrees.
- Useful for motion sickness and, for selected agents, vertigo/nausea.

### Occupational safety

The PDF states that first-generation antihistamines are absolutely contraindicated in pilots, drivers and machinery workers because of sedation.

**[PDF]** This is an exam-oriented safety rule.

**[Verified update]** In practice, the key issue is **marked caution/avoidance when alertness is required**, rather than a universal legal prohibition. Sedation varies markedly by drug and person, and alcohol or other CNS depressants can worsen impairment.

### Individual drugs

| Drug | Source emphasis | Current pharmacology note |
|---|---|---|
| **Promethazine** | Very sedating; strong anticholinergic effect | Potent first-generation H1 antagonist with substantial CNS and antimuscarinic effects |
| **Diphenhydramine** | Motion-sickness use; transdermal patch mentioned | First-generation H1 blocker; sedating and anticholinergic |
| **Hydroxyzine** | “Longest acting”; source notes active form → cetirizine | Hydroxyzine is metabolized to cetirizine; duration depends on indication/formulation |
| **Doxylamine** | With pyridoxine for morning sickness | Established antiemetic/anti-H1 use in pregnancy in appropriate formulations |
| **Cyclizine** | Motion sickness/seasickness | First-generation H1 antihistamine with anti-emetic action |
| **Meclizine** | Motion sickness/seasickness | First-generation H1 antihistamine used for vestibular nausea |
| **Cinnarizine** | Motion sickness/seasickness | First-generation H1-related vestibular drug; availability varies by country |
| **Cyproheptadine** | Antiserotonergic property; migraine-prophylaxis mention | H1 + 5-HT2 antagonist; appetite stimulation is a notable effect |
| **Chlorpheniramine** | Least sedating 1st generation in source; daytime use | It is less sedating than many older first-generation agents, but impairment is still possible |

### Anticholinergic clue

The source highlights first-generation antihistamines as anticholinergic drugs. This explains adverse effects such as:

- Dry mouth
- Blurred vision
- Urinary retention
- Constipation
- Tachycardia in susceptible patients

## 1.6 Second-generation antihistamines

**[PDF]**

Examples in the source:

- **Fexofenadine**
- **Cetirizine**
- **Levocetirizine**
- **Azelastine**
- **Ketotifen**
- **Olopatadine**

### Fexofenadine

**[PDF]**

- Active metabolite of terfenadine.
- Non-sedating / minimally sedating.
- No clinically important QT-prolonging effect characteristic of terfenadine.
- Used for allergic disorders.
- Brand mentioned in PDF: **Allegra**.

### Terfenadine and astemizole

**[PDF]**

- Withdrawn/banned because of **QT prolongation and torsades de pointes**, particularly with elevated concentrations and drug interactions.
- Terfenadine's active metabolite is **fexofenadine**.

### Cetirizine and levocetirizine

- Cetirizine is a second-generation H1 antihistamine but can cause **dose-related somnolence** in some patients.
- **Levocetirizine** is the levo-enantiomer of cetirizine.
- The PDF says levocetirizine requires a “half dose” and is less sedating. **[PDF]**
- **[Verified update]** Do not treat “half-dose” as a universal clinical conversion rule; dosing is product- and patient-specific.

### Topical H1 antihistamines

**Azelastine**

- Nasal/ophthalmic formulations are used in **allergic rhinitis** and **allergic conjunctivitis**.
- Source emphasizes strong topical activity. **[PDF]**

**Ketotifen / Olopatadine**

- H1-antagonist activity plus **mast-cell stabilizing** properties.
- Used in allergic conjunctivitis and related allergic disease depending on formulation.

## 1.7 Special antihistamine: Betahistine

**[PDF]**

- Brand mentioned: **Vertin**.
- Used for vertigo; the source includes benign positional vertigo and Ménière disease.
- PDF describes it as a **partial H1 agonist** rather than a conventional H1 antagonist.

**[Exam framing]** Betahistine is often memorized as a “histamine analogue” used for vertigo.

## 1.8 Pitolisant

**[PDF]**

- **H3 receptor inverse agonist/antagonist**.
- Increases wakefulness.
- Used for **narcolepsy**.
- The source notes that modafinil was previously regarded as the drug of choice.

**[Verified update]** Pitolisant is an H3-receptor inverse agonist used for excessive daytime sleepiness/cataplexy in adult narcolepsy in jurisdictions where approved. It is not an H1 antihistamine despite appearing in the broader histamine section.

## 1.9 Motion sickness and seasickness

**[PDF]**

### Commonly listed drugs

- **Scopolamine** — antimuscarinic; transdermal patch available.
- **Diphenhydramine** — first-generation H1 blocker; transdermal formulation mentioned in source.
- **Cyclizine**
- **Meclizine**
- **Cinnarizine**

### Exam distinction

- **Motion sickness:** nausea/vomiting while traveling in a car/bus etc.
- **Seasickness:** same vestibular phenomenon in a marine environment.

**Key pharmacologic principle:** Drugs acting centrally on H1 and/or muscarinic pathways are useful; peripheral second-generation H1 blockers are not appropriate substitutes for this vestibular indication.

## 1.10 Morning sickness

**[PDF]**

- Definition in source: vomiting associated with pregnancy.
- First non-drug advice: **small, frequent meals**.
- Source drug: **doxylamine + pyridoxine (vitamin B6)**.

**[PDF + verified]** Doxylamine/pyridoxine is a well-established treatment approach for nausea/vomiting of pregnancy in appropriate patients and formulations.

## 1.11 High-altitude illness / “Mountain sickness”

**[PDF]** The source has a boxed section titled **Mountain Sickness** immediately before the serotonin section. It notes:

- Respiratory compromise can occur at very high altitude.
- **Acetazolamide** is listed as the drug of choice.
- First assess whether the patient is developing **hypoxia** and check oxygen saturation.
- If saturation is falling or the patient is deteriorating: **rapid descent to a lower altitude**.

**[Verified update — CDC Yellow Book 2026]** Acetazolamide is used for prevention and treatment of **acute mountain sickness (AMS)**, while worsening altitude illness may require oxygen and/or **descent**, depending on severity. “Mountain sickness” is a broad term; current altitude medicine distinguishes AMS from potentially life-threatening HACE and HAPE. [Ref 16]

> **Exam pearl:** High altitude + prevention/treatment of AMS → **acetazolamide**; deterioration → **descent is definitive rescue therapy**.

# 2. SEROTONIN (5-HYDROXYTRYPTAMINE; 5-HT)

**PDF coverage:** pages 4–8 (printed pp. 72–76), with the serotonin concept continuing into migraine and antiemetic sections.

The PDF page 4 contains a synthesis diagram and a receptor classification table. **[PDF visual reconstruction]**

## 2.1 Synthesis of serotonin

```text
L-Tryptophan
     │
     │ tryptophan hydroxylase
     ↓
5-Hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP)
     │
     │ aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase
     ↓
Serotonin (5-HT)
```

**[PDF]** The source explicitly presents tryptophan → 5-HTP → serotonin.

## 2.2 Serotonin receptors

- **7 receptor families:** 5-HT1 through 5-HT7.
- **Most are GPCRs.**
- **Exception: 5-HT3 is ionotropic** (ligand-gated cation channel).

| Receptor family | Broad high-yield association |
|---|---|
| **5-HT1** | Gi/o; migraine therapeutics, anxiety-related signaling |
| **5-HT2** | Gq; cortical/vascular and smooth-muscle effects; several antagonists have psychiatric/GI uses |
| **5-HT3** | **Ionotropic**; nausea/vomiting pathways |
| **5-HT4** | Gs; promotes GI motility |
| **5-HT5** | Gi/o; less clinically central for the PDF topics |
| **5-HT6** | Gs; CNS |
| **5-HT7** | Gs; CNS/vascular/GI roles |

# 3. SEROTONIN AND MIGRAINE

## 3.1 5-HT1A — Buspirone

**[PDF]**

- **Buspirone** = partial agonist at **5-HT1A** receptors.
- Use: **generalized anxiety disorder**.

**Exam point:** Buspirone is an anxiolytic without the pharmacologic profile of a classical benzodiazepine.

## 3.2 5-HT1B / 5-HT1D — Triptans and ergotamine

The source links migraine to trigeminal neurons and release of inflammatory/neuropeptide mediators, especially **CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide)**. **[PDF]**

### Migraine concept map

```text
Trigeminovascular activation
        ↓
Release of CGRP + other mediators
        ↓
Neurogenic inflammation / vasodilatory signaling
        ↓
Migraine pain

5-HT1B/1D agonism
        ↓
Reduced trigeminal neuropeptide release
+ cranial vasoconstrictive effects
        ↓
Migraine relief
```

## 3.3 Triptans

Examples in the PDF:

- **Sumatriptan**
- **Eletriptan**
- **Rizatriptan**
- **Frovatriptan**
- **Zolmitriptan**

### Core mechanism

- **Selective 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonists**.
- Reduce release of **CGRP and other neuropeptides** from trigeminal pathways.
- Produce cranial vasoconstrictive effects.

### Routes and source-specific pearls

| Drug | PDF pearl |
|---|---|
| **Sumatriptan** | Oral, injection, nasal spray; nasal route useful when vomiting limits oral treatment |
| **Eletriptan** | PDF calls it “most effective” |
| **Rizatriptan** | PDF associates “Riza” with rapid action / fastest acting triptan |
| **Frovatriptan** | Longest-acting/longest half-life among triptans in the exam framework |
| **Zolmitriptan** | Nasal spray mentioned |

**[Exam framing]** “Most effective,” “fastest,” and similar labels depend on formulation, endpoint and comparator; they are retained here because they are explicitly taught in the source, not as universal treatment rankings.

### Triptan contraindication — important correction

The PDF lists hypertension, heart disease, angina and MI as contraindications because of vasoconstriction.

**[Verified update]** The clinically important distinction is especially **uncontrolled hypertension and established ischemic/cerebrovascular/peripheral vascular disease or other labeling-specific vascular contraindications**. Controlled hypertension is not equivalent to uncontrolled hypertension. Always apply the individual triptan's current prescribing information.

## 3.4 Lasmiditan — 5-HT1F agonist

**[PDF + verified]**

- **5-HT1F agonist**.
- Developed for **acute treatment of migraine**.
- Does **not** produce the cranial vasoconstriction associated with triptans.

**Clinical implication:** It may be considered when vasoconstrictor triptans are unsuitable, but “safe in heart disease” should not be interpreted as permission to ignore the drug's own adverse effects, interactions or driving restrictions.

---

## 3.5 Ergotamine and dihydroergotamine

**[PDF]**

- **Ergotamine** is a non-selective serotonergic/adrenergic/other receptor agonist with vasoconstrictive properties.
- **Dihydroergotamine (DHE)** is obtained by hydrogenation of ergotamine and has modified receptor selectivity/pharmacology.
- The source emphasizes 5-HT1B/1D effects and additional adrenergic activity.

### Ergotism

**Definition in source:** ischemic **dry gangrene**, classically affecting distal digits after excessive ergot exposure.

```text
Ergot / strong vasoconstriction
          ↓
Digital ischemia
          ↓
Necrosis
          ↓
Dry gangrene
```

The source lists **nitroprusside** as a vasodilator treatment. **[PDF]**

**[Verified update]** Ergotism is a medical emergency requiring removal of the offending drug and restoration of perfusion; vasodilator selection is individualized and depends on severity/local protocol. Do not treat “nitroprusside” as the sole universal antidote.

# 4. MIGRAINE PREVENTION

**[PDF]**

The source gives:

- **Propranolol** as its traditional drug of choice.
- If asthma limits β-blocker use, mnemonic **A-C-T**:
  - **A — antiepileptic drugs:** valproate, topiramate, gabapentin.
  - **C — calcium-channel blockers:** flunarizine, verapamil.
  - **T — tricyclic antidepressants:** amitriptyline, nortriptyline.

## 4.1 CGRP-targeted therapy

### Acute migraine — gepants

- **Ubrogepant**
- **Rimegepant**

The source also lists **atogepant** under acute therapy.

**[Verified correction]** **Atogepant is a preventive gepant, not an acute-treatment gepant.** Ubrogepant and rimegepant are established acute options; rimegepant and other agents may also have preventive indications depending on country/label.

### Prevention

- **Erenumab** — CGRP receptor monoclonal antibody.

Other CGRP-targeted monoclonal antibodies exist, including ligand-directed agents, but they are not all named in the source.

**[Verified update]** A September 2026 AHS/AAN guideline update incorporates many newer migraine-prevention therapies, including CGRP-targeted options, and emphasizes discussing evidence, adverse effects, access and patient priorities rather than relying on a single universal “drug of choice.” [Ref 1]

## 4.2 Migraine in pregnancy

**[PDF]**

The source says:

- “Never give any drug for prophylaxis.”
- Treat only the acute attack.
- **Paracetamol** is listed as the drug of choice.

**[Verified correction]** This is too absolute for current practice. ACOG's Clinical Practice Guideline on headaches in pregnancy and postpartum includes both **preventive and acute-treatment recommendations**, with treatment individualized to the patient and pregnancy context. The guideline was reaffirmed in 2024, with a later correction. [Ref 2]

For analgesic/antipyretic use, ACOG reaffirmed in September 2025 that **acetaminophen remains the preferred analgesic/antipyretic in pregnancy when used judiciously and appropriately**. [Ref 3]

**Exam-safe phrasing:**

> **Paracetamol/acetaminophen is a key acute option in pregnancy; preventive therapy is not universally prohibited and should be individualized.**

# 6. 5-HT3 ANTAGONISTS (“SETRONS”)

**PDF coverage:** pages 7–9, with continuation into the CINV treatment section.

## 6.1 Receptor and mechanism

- **5-HT3** is the only major serotonin receptor family in this chapter that is **ionotropic**.
- Antagonism reduces nausea/vomiting signaling.
- Source places the action at the **chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ)**.

### Drugs in the source

- **Ondansetron**
- **Granisetron**
- **Tropisetron**
- **Ramosetron**
- **Palonosetron**

### Key adverse effects

- **Constipation** / reduced GI motility.
- **QT prolongation** can occur, particularly with certain 5-HT3 antagonists and susceptible patients.

### Motion sickness

**[PDF]** Setron drugs are not effective for classic motion sickness; first-generation antihistamines and/or antimuscarinic drugs are used instead.

**[Verified update]** The distinction is pharmacologically sensible: motion sickness is strongly vestibular and involves pathways not adequately controlled by isolated 5-HT3 antagonism.

## 6.2 Alosetron

**[PDF]**

- Selective **5-HT3 receptor antagonist**.
- Reduces GI motility.
- Source use: treatment of diarrhea-predominant **irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D)**.
- The PDF emphasizes that it is used in **females** and warns about **ischemic colitis**.

**[Verified update]** Current U.S. prescribing information restricts alosetron to **women with severe chronic IBS-D who have not responded adequately to conventional therapy**. It carries a boxed warning for serious GI adverse reactions, including **ischemic colitis** and serious constipation complications. [Ref 17]

### Exam association

> **5-HT3 blockade → ↓ GI motility → selected severe IBS-D therapy**

The important adverse-effect pair is:

**Constipation + ischemic colitis.**

# 7. CHEMOTHERAPY-INDUCED NAUSEA AND VOMITING (CINV)

## 7.1 Source mechanism

**[PDF]**

```text
Cytotoxic chemotherapy
        ↓
Damage to rapidly dividing GI cells
        ↓
Release of inflammatory mediators / serotonin signaling
        ↓
Vagal + central emetic pathways
        ↓
CTZ / vomiting center activation
        ↓
Nausea + vomiting
```

The source identifies **cisplatin** as having a very high emetogenic risk.

## 7.2 Source's early/late framework

The uploaded note divides treatment into:

1. **Early phase** — 5-HT3 antagonists such as ondansetron, granisetron, palonosetron.
2. **Delayed phase** — NK1 antagonists such as aprepitant/fosaprepitant.
3. Resistant cases — add **dexamethasone**; source then adds cannabinoids.

### Current clinical correction

**[Verified update]** Modern CINV prophylaxis is **risk-based and combination-based**, not simply “setron for 24 h, then aprepitant.” ASCO guidance supports combinations incorporating:

- **5-HT3 antagonist**
- **NK1 receptor antagonist**
- **Dexamethasone**
- **Olanzapine** in high-emetic-risk regimens

For cisplatin/high-emetic-risk chemotherapy, current guideline-based prophylaxis uses a multi-drug regimen rather than single-agent ondansetron followed by delayed aprepitant. [Ref 5]

# 8. PROKINETIC DRUGS

**PDF coverage:** pages 9–10 (printed pp. 77–78)

## 8.1 Definition

**Prokinetic drugs** increase GI motility and facilitate forward propulsion of gastric/intestinal contents.

### Uses in the PDF

- **Constipation**
- **Gastroparesis**
- **GERD**, particularly by increasing LES tone in appropriate agents

### Core physiologic idea

```text
Serotonin / acetylcholine signalling  → ↑ GI motility
Dopamine signalling                  → ↓ GI motility
```

**[Exam framing]** This is a useful conceptual simplification.

- **Mosapride**
- **Renzapride**
- **Itopride**
- **Prucalopride**

### Prucalopride

- **5-HT4 agonist**.
- Used for **chronic constipation**.

### Cisapride / tegaserod

**[PDF]**

Cisapride was withdrawn from broad routine use because of serious ventricular arrhythmias; tegaserod has had restricted/reintroduced use for selected populations in some jurisdictions. ## 8.3 Dopamine D2 antagonists

### Domperidone

**[PDF]**

- D2 antagonist.
- Increases GI motility.
- Does **not significantly cross the BBB**, so extrapyramidal effects are less frequent than with metoclopramide.
- Source links its peripheral prokinetic effect with increased acetylcholine signaling.

**[Verified update]** Domperidone has clinically important cardiac safety concerns, including **QT prolongation, torsades de pointes and other serious arrhythmias**. It is not FDA-approved for human use in the United States; availability and restrictions differ by country. [Ref 6]

### Metoclopramide

**[PDF]**

- **D2 antagonist**.
- Also has **5-HT4 agonist** activity.
- Enhances cholinergic signaling and GI motility.
- Crosses the BBB.
- Central D2 blockade explains:
  - Akathisia
  - Dystonia
  - Drug-induced parkinsonism
  - Other extrapyramidal reactions

**Important clinical safety update:** Prolonged use of metoclopramide is limited by the risk of **tardive dyskinesia**; current prescribing guidance emphasizes the shortest effective duration.

### Metoclopramide vs domperidone

| Feature | Metoclopramide | Domperidone |
|---|---|---|
| D2 antagonism | Yes | Yes |
| 5-HT4 activity | Yes | Mainly peripheral D2 effect; pharmacology differs by formulation/source |
| BBB penetration | **Yes** | **Limited** |
| CNS/EPS risk | **Higher** | Lower, but not zero overall adverse-effect risk |
| QT/cardiac concern | Less characteristic than domperidone | **Important** |
| Major exam clue | “Crosses BBB → EPS” | “Does not significantly cross BBB” |

**Visual note:** The PDF page 10 contains a graphic contrasting **domperidone behind a BBB** with **metoclopramide entering the brain**, reinforcing this distinction.

### Drug-induced parkinsonism

The PDF lists **benzhexol (trihexyphenidyl)** as an anticholinergic treatment.

**[PDF + verified]** Anticholinergic agents can be used for acute drug-induced dystonia/parkinsonism, with choice depending on the specific syndrome. Current practice distinguishes acute dystonia, akathisia and tardive syndromes rather than treating every EPS manifestation identically.

## 8.4 Erythromycin

# 15. NSAIDs (NON-STEROIDAL ANTI-INFLAMMATORY DRUGS)

## 15.1 Mechanism

- Inhibit **cyclooxygenase (COX)** enzymes.
- Reduce prostaglandin and thromboxane synthesis.
- Produce combinations of:
  - analgesic effect
  - antipyretic effect
  - anti-inflammatory effect
  - antiplatelet effect for aspirin specifically

---

## 15.2 Aspirin

### Unique property

**[PDF + verified]** Aspirin is the classic **irreversible COX inhibitor** because it acetylates COX.

The source uses:

- “Suicide inhibitor”
- “Hit-and-run drug”

These are useful memory aids.

### Dose framework in the PDF

| Dose range taught in PDF | Intended effect in source |
|---|---|
| 40–325 mg | Antiplatelet |
| Up to 2 g | Analgesic + antipyretic |
| >2–5 g | Anti-inflammatory |

**[Exam framing / caution]** Historical dose-response teaching is retained, but exact dosing in current practice is indication-specific and should not be inferred from this table for prescribing.

### Antiplatelet mechanism

- Platelets have no nucleus and cannot efficiently resynthesize COX-1.
- Aspirin therefore causes persistent suppression of platelet **TXA2** synthesis for the life of affected platelets.

### Acute coronary/cerebrovascular disease

The source calls aspirin the drug of choice for MI and stroke.

**[Verified update]** Aspirin is a key antiplatelet drug in acute coronary syndromes and selected ischemic cerebrovascular settings, but timing, dose, contraindications and combination with other antithrombotic therapy depend on the specific diagnosis. It should not be generalized to all “stroke” or all chest pain.

## 15.3 NSAID adverse effects

### Gastrointestinal toxicity

- Dyspepsia
- Gastritis
- Peptic ulcer disease
- GI bleeding/perforation

The source recommends **PPI or misoprostol** for NSAID-related ulcer contexts.

**[Verified update]** Current NICE guidance supports a **PPI** (or, depending on context, H2-receptor antagonist) when gastroprotection is indicated; choice depends on GI and cardiovascular risk. [Ref 10]

### Renal effects

- Reduced renal prostaglandin synthesis can reduce afferent arteriolar vasodilation.
- Can reduce GFR and precipitate kidney injury in susceptible patients.
- Long-term analgesic exposure can contribute to chronic kidney disease patterns.

### Hypersensitivity / cutaneous reactions

The PDF highlights:

- Rashes
- Stevens–Johnson syndrome (rare but severe)

These are serious but not the typical most-common NSAID adverse effects.

# 16. ASPIRIN POISONING

The source presents aspirin as acidic and sodium bicarbonate as the antidote.

**[PDF + verified]** More precise terminology:

- **Salicylate poisoning** is treated with **serum/urine alkalinization**, commonly using IV sodium bicarbonate, plus supportive care.
- Severe poisoning may require **hemodialysis**.

Do not reduce salicylate poisoning to a simple “acid + base neutralization” concept; bicarbonate works largely through **alkalinization and enhanced renal elimination**, while serious acid–base and CNS/respiratory abnormalities must be managed.

# 19. COX-1 VS COX-2

The source's teaching shortcut is:

- **COX-1 inhibition** → GI adverse effects.
- **COX-2 inhibition** → anti-inflammatory effect.

**[Exam framing]** Useful, but overly binary.

### Better conceptual model

- **COX-1** contributes to gastric mucosal defense, platelet function and renal homeostasis.
- **COX-2** is induced in many inflammatory settings but also has constitutive roles, including in the kidney and vascular system.

Therefore selective COX-2 inhibition:

- tends to reduce GI ulcer risk relative to nonselective NSAIDs;
- **does not eliminate renal toxicity**;
- can be associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in susceptible patients.

# 21. PARACETAMOL (ACETAMINOPHEN)

## 21.1 What the PDF teaches

The PDF calls paracetamol:

- Safe in renal failure relative to NSAIDs
- Safe in children with respect to Reye syndrome
- A key option in pregnancy
- Low GI ulcer risk

**[PDF]**

### Current correction to mechanism

The source states that paracetamol is a **COX-3 inhibitor** in the brain.

**[Verified correction]** The clinically accepted mechanism is **not adequately explained by a human COX-3 enzyme model**. Evidence does not support treating “COX-3 inhibition” as the definitive human mechanism. Paracetamol has complex central actions involving prostaglandin pathways and metabolites including **AM404**, with downstream TRPV1/CB1-related signaling described in the literature. [Ref 11]

### Pharmacologic profile

- **Very weak anti-inflammatory** activity at usual doses compared with classical NSAIDs.
- It should not be described as completely inactive on COX-1/COX-2 pathways; the biology is more complex.

---

## 21.2 Paracetamol toxicity

### Metabolism

A small fraction of paracetamol is metabolized by CYP enzymes to the reactive metabolite:

**NAPQI = N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine**

Normally:

```text
Paracetamol
   ↓
small CYP-mediated fraction
   ↓
NAPQI
   ↓
Glutathione conjugation
   ↓
Detoxification
```

In overdose:

```text
Excess paracetamol
      ↓
↑ NAPQI production
      ↓
Glutathione depletion
      ↓
NAPQI accumulation
      ↓
Hepatocellular injury
      ↓
Centrilobular (zone 3) necrosis
```

### PDF visual diagram

The page 18 image shows:

```text
PCM → CYP2E1 → NAPQI → Hepatotoxicity
                     ↘
                 Centrilobular necrosis
                 with periportal sparing
```

The accompanying histology image demonstrates central-zone necrosis with relative periportal sparing. **[PDF visual reconstruction]**

### Risk factors

The source emphasizes:

- Alcohol
- Smoking

**[Verified update]** Chronic alcohol use can alter CYP2E1 activity, but the relationship between alcohol timing, nutritional status and toxicity is more nuanced than a simple “alcohol + paracetamol = toxicity” rule. Fasting/malnutrition can also increase vulnerability.

### Dose warning

The PDF says >4 g/day increases hepatotoxicity risk.

**[Verified update]** **4 g/day is not a universal overdose threshold.** Acute hepatotoxicity is assessed primarily after a potentially toxic **single acute ingestion** using the Rumack–Matthew nomogram when applicable, while chronic supratherapeutic dosing has different risk dynamics. Lower maximum daily doses may be recommended in some adults and products.

## 24.3 Colchicine

### Mechanism

**[PDF + verified]**

- Binds tubulin.

### Why toxicity affects rapidly dividing cells

- Bone-marrow suppression at toxic exposure
- Neuromuscular toxicity, especially with renal dysfunction or interacting drugs

**Important correction:** Colchicine is **not “renal safe.”** It requires careful dosing and toxicity monitoring in renal impairment.

# 33. SOURCE-CRITICAL CORRECTIONS TO MEMORIZE

This section is deliberately compact: it identifies statements in the PDF that should **not** be carried into clinical reasoning without qualification.

| PDF statement / teaching point | Corrected study formulation |
|---|---|
| Second-generation H1 blockers “do not cross BBB” | Most have limited CNS penetration; some, especially cetirizine, can still cause somnolence. |
| Chlorpheniramine can always be given daytime | Less sedating than many 1st-gen agents, but impairment is still possible. |
| Hypertension is a universal triptan contraindication | Think **uncontrolled hypertension and relevant vascular disease/labeling-specific contraindications**. |
| Lasmiditan is simply “safe in heart disease” | No vasoconstriction, but still has its own CNS/driving and adverse-effect restrictions. |
| Atogepant is an acute migraine drug | **Atogepant is preventive**; ubrogepant/rimegepant are acute gepants. |
| Never give migraine prophylaxis in pregnancy | Prevention can be considered; ACOG provides both acute and preventive guidance. |
| Lorcaserin was banned specifically for GI cancer | FDA withdrawal followed an **increased overall cancer signal**; wording is broader. |
| 5-HT3 blockers are sufficient for CINV early phase, then aprepitant delayed | Modern CINV prophylaxis is risk-based and commonly uses **5-HT3 + NK1 + dexamethasone ± olanzapine**. |
| Domperidone has no important cardiac issue | QT prolongation/torsades and serious arrhythmias are important safety concerns. |
| Carbetocin is drug of choice for labour induction | Carbetocin is primarily a **long-acting oxytocin analogue for PPH prevention** in selected settings. |
| Ergometrine/methylergometrine are not used for PPH | They may be used in selected contexts if hypertensive disease is excluded. |
| ABC tocolytic mnemonic is the current algorithm | Current guidance commonly prioritizes **nifedipine**, with atosiban when needed; betamimetics are not recommended by NICE. |
| Corticosteroids simply “block PLA2” | Upstream inflammatory suppression involves annexin A1 and broad genomic effects. |
| COX-1 = ulcer, COX-2 = inflammation, full stop | Useful mnemonic, but both enzymes have physiologic roles; COX-2 selectivity does not remove renal/CV risk. |
| All aspirin <18 use is absolutely prohibited | Avoid with viral illness unless a specific indication warrants it; Kawasaki is classic. |
| Paracetamol works by COX-3 inhibition | **COX-3 is not an adequate established human mechanism**; mechanism is central and complex. |
| >4 g/day is the sole definition of paracetamol toxicity | Acute poisoning assessment is based on dose, timing, serum level and clinical context; chronic supratherapeutic exposure is different. |
| Colchicine is renal-safe | It requires dose adjustment/caution in renal impairment and has serious toxicity potential. |
| Febuxostat is preferred over allopurinol now | ACR guidance prefers **allopurinol first-line**, including in CKD, with febuxostat as an alternative. |
| All listed uricosurics are current routine drugs | Availability/approval varies; probenecid is the most established classic example. |
| TNF inhibitors are all monoclonal antibodies | Certolizumab is a PEGylated Fab fragment; etanercept is a receptor-Fc fusion protein. |
| JAK inhibitors are biologics | They are **targeted synthetic DMARDs**. |
| Tofacitinib is approved for all IBD | Tofacitinib has an indication for **ulcerative colitis**, not blanket all-IBD use. |
