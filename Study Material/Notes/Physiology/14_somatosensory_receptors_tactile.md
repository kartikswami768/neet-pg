# 14. Somatosensory Receptors: Tactile

## Somatosensory receptors

- Receptors are present on the surface of the body, in skin, muscles, joints and viscera.
- They are classified into five major categories: mechanoreceptors, thermoreceptors, photoreceptors, chemoreceptors and polymodal receptors.

![[images/ch14_p097_somatosensory_receptor_classification.png]]

### Mechanoreceptors

- Activated by mechanical deformation: mechanical stretch, torsion or tension.
- Example: baroreceptors in the cardiovascular system. An increase in blood pressure stretches blood vessels and activates baroreceptors.

| Receptor group | Function or location listed in the source |
|---|---|
| **Tactile receptors** | Receptors for touch and pressure. |
| **Proprioceptors** | Positional sense of joints in three-dimensional space; examples include muscle spindle, Golgi tendon apparatus and joint-capsule receptors. |
| **Nociceptors** | Receptors for pain; pain receptors may be stimulated by mechanical deformation/stretch. |
| **Hair cells** | Present in the organ of Corti in the inner ear; bending of their hairs (mechanical deformation) activates the cells. |

### Thermoreceptors

Activated by temperature; classified as cold receptors and warm/warmth receptors.

![[images/ch14_p097_thermoreceptor_classification.png]]

### Photoreceptors

- Activated by photons/light; also called electromagnetic receptors.
- Examples: rods and cones in the retina.

### Chemoreceptors

- Receptors for smell and taste.
- Activated by chemical stimuli.

### Polymodal receptors

- Activated by multiple modalities of stimulus.
- Example: nociceptors, which can be activated by mechanical, chemical or thermal stimuli.

## Tactile receptors

- Receptors for touch and pressure (sustained touch).
- **Tactile acuity** is the discriminative power of touch. The source notes: fingertip (maximum tactile receptors) > lips.
- Tactile receptors are divided into rapidly adapting (RA) and slowly adapting (SA) receptors.

| Rapidly adapting (RA) | Slowly adapting (SA) |
|---|---|
| Adapted/inactivated within fractions of a second. | Not inactivated soon; signal for a longer duration. |
| Pacinian corpuscle | Merkel disc |
| Meissner corpuscle | Ruffini ending |
| Hair-end organ/receptor | Free nerve ending |

### Rapidly adapting receptors

#### Pacinian corpuscle

- Most sensitive touch receptor and largest touch receptor.
- Best stimulus: vibration, especially high-frequency vibration (200 Hz or more).
- Located in the deep dermis of the skin.
- Sustained touch/deep pressure is also detected by the Pacinian corpuscle.

#### Meissner corpuscle

- Present only in non-hairy skin (glabrous skin).
- Listed as the most numerous touch receptors in the body.
- Best stimulus: moving touch.
- Also activated by slow-frequency vibration (50 Hz).

#### Hair-end organ / hair-follicle receptor

- Best stimulus: moving touch on hairy skin.
- Present at the base of hair follicles.
- Bending of the hair follicle activates the receptor; the source states that the first touch on skin is detected by the hair-follicle receptor.

### Slowly adapting receptors

#### Merkel discs

- The only touch receptor listed at the epidermal layer of skin.
- Multiple Merkel discs form a group.
- Also called the **Iggo dome receptor**: a dome-shaped elevation of the epidermis formed by Merkel discs. Iggo is named as the scientist who first discovered it.
- Best stimulus: point stimulus, corners, edges and margins.
- Detects very slow vibration (~5 Hz).

#### Ruffini endings

- Present in the dermis.
- Best stimulus: stretch on the skin.
- Shape and size of a large object held in the hand are best detected by Ruffini endings.

#### Free nerve endings

- The end of a neuron without myelination.
- The tip generally contains nociceptors. Some free nerve endings at the skin surface contain nociceptors and touch receptors.
- Detect itching and tickling sensations.

### Distribution in hairy and non-hairy skin

- Merkel discs, Ruffini corpuscles, Pacinian corpuscles and free nerve endings (C fibres) are present in both hairy and non-hairy skin.
- Meissner corpuscles → glabrous skin.
- Hair-follicle receptors → hairy skin.
- Encapsulated receptors listed: Pacinian corpuscles, Ruffini corpuscles and Meissner corpuscles.

![[images/ch14_p099_hairy_vs_glabrous_skin.png]]

## Two-point discrimination

- Best receptor for two-point discrimination: **Merkel disc**.
- Tactile discrimination/tactile acuity is the detection and resolving power of two points on the skin surface.
- Each sensory receptor has a particular receptive field.
  - The brain perceives two points as separate if they fall in two separate receptive fields.
  - If both points fall in the same receptive field, the brain perceives one point.
  - To perceive two points separately, the receptive field should be small; the source lists Merkel disc as having the smallest receptive field.

## Braille reading

- Braille contains elevated dots arranged horizontally or vertically.
- The reader moves their fingers across the dots to feel them and identify the letter.
- Feeling the points → Merkel discs are activated.
- Moving the fingers (moving touch) → Meissner receptors are activated.
- Braille reading requires both Merkel and Meissner receptors, with Merkel's contribution greater than Meissner's. Merkel discs are essential for perceiving the number of dots and give the best dot perception.
- Distance between Braille dots is generally **2–3 mm**.

![[images/ch14_p100_braille_receptor_activity.png]]

## Receptor potential

- Receptor potential: change in voltage within a receptor.
- A sensory neuron (pseudounipolar type) enters through the dorsal region of the spinal cord. The end of the sensory neuron at the skin surface contains a receptor; the Pacinian corpuscle is given as an example.
- Pacinian corpuscles contain multiple layers, with a myelinated Aβ nerve fibre at the centre. The first node of Ranvier is present within the receptor.
- The source compares the consistency of the Pacinian corpuscle to a viscoelastic structure (a balloon filled with water).

Sequence of receptor-potential generation:

1. Touch on the skin → indentation of the receptor → increased tension inside the receptor.
2. Mechanosensitive (stretch/tension-activated), non-specific ion channels open at the tip of the nerve fibre.
3. Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ enter the neuron (the source shows Ca²⁺ greater than Na⁺).
4. Gradual depolarisation of the nerve ending from −70 mV to −55 mV (the neuronal threshold) produces a receptor/generator potential.
5. Receptor potential is a local potential: it cannot travel to the end of the neuron, is non-propagated and decremental, does not follow the all-or-none law and is graded. More touch → greater depolarisation.
6. When threshold (−55 mV) is reached at the node of Ranvier, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; a large amount of Na⁺ enters and voltage rises to +35 mV.
7. An action potential is generated and moves from one node of Ranvier to another by saltatory conduction, reaching the spinal cord.

![[images/ch14_p101_receptor_potential_diagram.png]]

- In a sensory neuron, the action potential is first generated at the node of Ranvier.

## Perceiving different intensities of touch

- Receptor potential continues to increase from light touch to crude touch as indentation increases.
- Action-potential amplitude does not increase with stronger touch; the frequency of action potentials changes.
  - Crude touch → higher action-potential frequency.
  - Light touch → lower action-potential frequency.
- **Stimulus strength is directly proportional to action-potential frequency.**

![[images/ch14_p102_touch_intensity_ap_frequency.png]]

## Adaptation of receptors

A long-sustained stimulus leads to a decrease in action-potential frequency; the action potential may disappear and sensation decreases. This is the **adaptation phenomenon**.

### Mechanisms of adaptation listed in the source

1. **Adaptation of the receptor:** prolonged stimulus changes receptor shape/tension → tension inside the receptor decreases → stretch-sensitive channel closes → action-potential frequency decreases or disappears.
2. **Accommodation of ion channels:** with the same stimulus for a long duration, channels gradually become inactivated, so the stimulus no longer produces an action potential.

### Rapidly adapting receptors (phasic)

- Increasing stimulus strength causes action potentials to appear.
- With the same stimulus for a long duration, action-potential amplitude does not change, but frequency decreases and may gradually disappear.
- When the stimulus is withdrawn, action-potential frequency increases.
- Also called **phasic receptors**: a change in stimulus phase increases action-potential frequency; no change in phase reduces it.

### Slowly adapting receptors (tonic)

- Action potentials continue at the same frequency during prolonged stimulation of the same strength; there is no adaptation in the source's description.
- Also called **tonic receptors**.

## Thermoreceptors

| Cold receptors | Warm receptors |
|---|---|
| Activated at 10–40°C. | Activated at 30–49°C. |
| Maximum activation around 24°C. | Maximum activation around 45°C. |

- The source's graph labels freezing cold, cold, cool, indifferent, warm, hot and burning hot, alongside cold-pain and heat-pain regions.
- Maximum thermoreceptor density is listed as lips > fingertips.
- More cold receptors are present than warm receptors.
- Temperatures below 10°C activate nociceptors (pain), according to the source.

## MCQs

**1.** Which sensation is detected by the Pacinian corpuscle?

- a. Pressure
- b. Touch
- c. Temperature
- d. Pain

**Answer:** (a)

**2.** Discriminative touch sensation is identified by:

- a. Merkel disc
- b. Meissner corpuscle
- c. Pacinian corpuscle
- d. Ruffini ending

**Answer:** (a)
