# 54. Pituitary Hormones

## Anterior and posterior pituitary hormones

### Pituitary gland
- The human pituitary gland is composed of anterior and posterior lobes.
- It is controlled by the hypothalamus.
- The connecting stalk connects the hypothalamus to the pituitary.

**Pituitary gland anatomy**

![[images/ch54_p373_pituitary_anatomy.png]]

### Anterior lobe
- Develops from Rathke's pouch.
- Also called the **adenohypophysis**.
- Contains:
  - **Trope cells:** secrete hormones from the anterior pituitary and give it a glandular appearance.
  - **Folliculostellate cells:** secrete paracrine factors that help growth and development of trope cells.

### Posterior lobe
- Develops as an extension of the hypothalamus.
- Also called the **neurohypophysis**.
- Contains neuronal endings directly from the hypothalamus and pituicyte cells (with an astrocyte-like function).


## Hypothalamic control of the pituitary

### Magnocellular neurons
- Long neurons extend from the hypothalamus to the posterior pituitary.
- ADH and oxytocin (neuropeptides) are released from neuronal endings by neurosecretion.

### Parvocellular neurons
- Small neurons arise from the hypothalamus.
- They secrete substances into a capillary network formed by the superior hypophyseal artery at the median eminence of the hypothalamus.
- These substances may be releasing or inhibitory hormones.
- The superior hypophyseal artery forms another capillary network, the **long portal vessels**, at the anterior pituitary.
- Products of parvocellular neurons travel through the long portal vessels (the hypothalamo–hypophyseal portal system) and reach anterior-pituitary trope cells.

**Hypothalamo–hypophyseal portal system**

![[images/ch54_p373_hypothalamo_hypophyseal_portal_system.png]]

## Hypothalamic nuclei and hormones

| Hypothalamic nucleus | Hormones or associations listed |
|---|---|
| **Supraoptic** | ADH (antidiuretic hormone); secreted from posterior pituitary; magnocellular neuron |
| **Paraventricular** | Oxytocin; secreted from posterior pituitary; magnocellular neuron. TRH and CRH are also listed as secreted through the long portal vessels by parvocellular neurons. |
| **Preoptic** | GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone); described as major secretion |
| **Arcuate** | GnRH, GHRH (growth hormone-releasing hormone), dopamine (PIF—prolactin inhibitory factor) |

## Anterior-pituitary trope cells

| Cell staining/type | Trope cell | Approximate proportion shown | Hormone produced |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Acidophilic | Somatotropes | 50% | Growth hormone (GH) |
| Acidophilic | Lactotropes | 10–30% | Prolactin (PRL) |
| Basophilic | Gonadotropes | 20% | FSH, LH |
| Basophilic | Corticotropes | 10% | ACTH |
| Basophilic | Thyrotropes | 5% | TSH |

### Fetal pituitary development
- Corticotropes appear at the 6th week of gestation (earliest).
- Somatotropes appear at the 8th week.
- All other trope cells appear at the 12th week.

## Control of pituitary hormones
- **Dopamine:** prolactin inhibitory factor (PIF).
- **TRH:** thyrotropin-releasing hormone.
  - Major stimulus for TSH secretion.
  - Very high TRH levels also stimulate prolactin secretion.
- **GHRH:** stimulates GH secretion.
- **Somatostatin:** inhibits GH secretion.
- **CRH:** stimulates secretion of ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone).
- High levels of ADH can also stimulate ACTH through the V3 receptor (vasopressin receptor type 3).

The source's combined pituitary-axis diagram links:
- GnRH → LH/FSH → gonads → estrogen/progesterone → reproduction.
- TRH → TSH → thyroid → T3/T4 → metabolism.
- Dopamine inhibition and TRH stimulation → prolactin → breast → lactation.
- GHRH/somatostatin → GH → liver/IGF-1 → growth.
- CRH (and high ADH through V3) → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol/androgens → stress-related functions.
- ADH → distal nephron → water balance.
- Oxytocin → uterus and breast → parturition and lactation.

## Growth hormone (GH)

### Synthesis and genes
- GH synthesis is controlled by the long arm of chromosome 17q.
- The source lists five types of GH-related genes on chromosome 17:

| Number of genes | Name | Features given in the source |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | **hGH-N** | Normal GH secreted by the pituitary; adult GH; also called GH1 |
| 1 | **hGH-V** | Variant GH secreted by the placenta; major GH secreted by the placenta during pregnancy; also called GH2 or placental GH |
| 2 | Human chorionic somatomammotropin (hCS) | Synthesized by placenta; also called human placental lactogen (hPL) |
| 1 | Pseudogenes | No GH action |

**Species difference:** Bovine and porcine GH do not affect human growth, according to the source.

### Feedback control of GH
- GHRH stimulates the anterior pituitary → increased GH.
- Somatostatin inhibits the anterior pituitary → decreased GH.
- Negative feedback:
  - IGF and GH stimulate somatostatin secretion, which inhibits the anterior pituitary and reduces GH.
  - IGF has a direct inhibitory effect on the anterior pituitary.
  - GHRH has an inhibitory effect on the hypothalamus (as stated in the source).

**Growth-hormone feedback**

![[images/ch54_p376_gh_feedback.png]]

### Insulin-like growth factors (IGFs; somatomedins)
- Secreted from the liver.
- Also called somatomedins; mediate GH action.
- The source lists somatomedin A, B and C.

| Comparison in the source | Somatomedin C / IGF-I | Somatomedin A / IGF-II |
|---|---|---|
| Other name | IGF-1 | IGF-II |
| Major IGF in | Adult | Fetal life |
| Role | Fetal and postnatal growth | Embryonic and fetal development |
| GH dependence | Dependent on GH | Independent of GH |

### Control of GH secretion
- Secretion: adolescents > children > adults.
- Maximum secretion occurs during NREM sleep.
- Release is pulsatile.

**Factors controlling GH**
- Stressful stimuli: surgery, trauma.
- Hypoglycemia: fasting, exercise.
- Increased plasma amino acids: protein meal.
- Sleep: NREM stimulates and REM inhibits GH secretion.

**Additional sleep information in the source:** REM sleep stimulates secretion of PRL, LH and FSH.

**Pulsatile GH secretion across the day**

![[images/ch54_p376_gh_secretion_graph.png]]

### GH functions
- **Direct effects**
  - Decreases glucose uptake and increases insulin resistance.
  - Increases lipolysis and free fatty acids (FFA).
  - Stimulates bone and cartilage growth (chondrogenesis).
  - Increases protein synthesis in tissues and lean body mass.
  - Increases sodium, potassium and water retention; hypertension is listed.
- **Indirect effects through IGF-1**
  - Anti-lipolytic effect.
  - Increased protein synthesis in chondrocytes.
  - Linear growth.
  - Increased protein synthesis in muscle and lean body mass.

### GH hypersecretion: gigantism and acromegaly

| Gigantism | Acromegaly |
|---|---|
| Before epiphyseal closure | After epiphyseal closure |
| Increased deposition of chondrocytes at the growth plate increases long-bone length. | Deposition of chondrocytes on the surface of long bones increases bone thickness; particularly affects the long bones of the extremities. |

**Features labelled in the source's acromegaly illustration**
- Headache.
- Skull growth with prominent supraorbital ridges.
- Enlargement of lips, nose and tongue.
- Prognathism (growth of the lower jaw).
- Cardiomyopathy.
- Hypertension.
- Thickened skin.
- Enlargement of liver.
- Impaired glucose tolerance (25%) / type 2 diabetes (10%).
- Colonic cancer.
- Enlargement of hands; arthropathy and carpal tunnel syndrome.
- Myopathy.
- Enlargement of feet and increased heel-pad thickness.

**Clinical features of acromegaly**

![[images/ch54_p377_acromegaly_features.png]]

**Treatment listed**
- **Octreotide:** somatostatin analogue; prevents GH secretion.
- **Pegvisomant:** GH-receptor blocker.

### GH deficiency (hypopituitarism)
- Inherited or acquired.
- Acquired causes include tumour mass effect (61% as shown in the source).
- The usual sequential loss of hormones is given as: GH → FSH/LH → TSH → ACTH.

**Clinical features / labels**
- **Hypopituitary dwarf:** short stature, increased fat and propensity to hypoglycemia.
- **Laron dwarf:** mutation of the GH receptor (at the liver); low IGF-1.
- **African pygmies:** described in the source as similar to Laron dwarf (GH-receptor mutation), and also attributed to nutritional deficiency. This source wording is potentially contradictory and has not been reconciled here.


## Prolactin (PRL)

- Homologous to GH and human placental lactogen (hPL); the source associates these with chromosome 17q.
- PRL secretion is pulsatile.
- Highest secretory peaks occur during REM sleep.
- Marked lactotroph cell hyperplasia develops during pregnancy and in the first few months of lactation.

### Regulation of prolactin
- Main control is inhibitory control from the hypothalamus.
- Dopamine (PIF) acts on D2 receptors in the anterior pituitary.
- If the stalk between the hypothalamus and pituitary is cut, the source lists: PRL ↑, ACTH ↑, TSH ↓, GH ↓ and LSH ↓. The text labels the last hormone as “LSH”; that printed label is retained.
- In severe hypothyroidism, very high TRH stimulates PRL secretion from the anterior pituitary.
- Negative feedback: increased PRL stimulates dopamine secretion, which inhibits PRL secretion.

**Hypothalamic regulation of prolactin**

![[images/ch54_p378_prolactin_regulation.png]]

### Factors controlling prolactin
- Pregnancy (estrogen): described as the most potent stimulus for prolactin secretion.
- Lactation.
- Sleep (REM).
- Sexual intercourse / nipple stimulation.
- Hypoglycemia (fasting, exercise).
- Stressful stimuli (surgery, trauma).

### Prolactin functions
- Stimulates milk production (lactogenesis).
- Stimulates breast development (mammogenesis).
- Induces and maintains lactation (galactopoiesis).
- Inhibits ovulation by decreasing GnRH.
- Inhibits spermatogenesis by decreasing GnRH.
- Inhibits folliculogenesis.
- Suppresses sexual drive.

**Important information listed**
- Prolactinoma: prolactin-producing tumour of the anterior pituitary → increased PRL.
- Infertility.
- Amenorrhoea.
- Galactorrhoea: spontaneous secretion of milk from a non-pregnant breast.

## Posterior pituitary

- ADH and oxytocin are neurohormones released by neurosecretion.
- They are synthesized in hypothalamic cell bodies and reach neuronal endings at the posterior pituitary by axonal transport.
- They are peptides; protein synthesis occurs in rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER). In neurons, RER remains in aggregates mainly in cell bodies, called Nissl bodies.

| Diabetes insipidus (DI) | SIADH |
|---|---|
| Defined in the source as a decrease of 75% or more in ADH secretion or action. | Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion. |
| **Central/neurogenic DI:** attributed to hypothalamic destruction. | Hypersecretion of ADH can have many causes, including ectopic ADH-producing tumours such as lung carcinoma, various drugs, head injury and a large dose of oxytocin. |
| **Nephrogenic DI:** associated with V2-receptor mutation. | Clinical features are due to reduced water excretion and cellular overhydration. |

## Oxytocin
- Acts primarily on the breasts and uterus.
- Contracts myoepithelial cells lining the breast ducts → milk ejection.
- Contracts uterine smooth muscle.
- Facilitates sperm transport.
- Causes contraction of vas deferens smooth muscle during ejaculation in males.
