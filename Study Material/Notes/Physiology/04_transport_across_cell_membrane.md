# 04 — Transport Across Cell Membrane

> **Source:** Prep XI Physiology notes, printed pages 23–31 (PDF pages 23–31). Full source-page images are embedded to preserve molecular diagrams, flowcharts, and graph labels.


### Source page 23

#### General principles of transport

- Small nonpolar, hydrophobic molecules (O₂ and CO₂) diffuse rapidly across the cell membrane.
- Small, uncharged, polar molecules (water or urea) diffuse across the cell membrane much more slowly.
- Charged molecules (ions) are essentially impermeable to the lipid bilayer, regardless of size; they require transport proteins to cross the cell membrane.

#### Membrane transport proteins

| Carrier protein | Channel protein |
|---|---|
| Solute binds to the protein → conformational change → solute is released toward the inner surface of the cell membrane. | Solute binds weakly; there is no conformational change. A central pore facilitates solute transport. |
| Helps in active and passive transport. | The source diagram shows a pore and Na⁺ transport through a channel. |

**Properties of carrier proteins**

- **Saturation:** occurs when the number of solute molecules greatly exceeds the number of carrier proteins; all carriers become saturated.
- **Stereospecificity:** a carrier dedicated to D-glucose cannot transport the other isomer, L-glucose.
- **Competition:** one carrier may transport two solutes, for example glucose and galactose; the solutes compete for binding to the carrier.

**Types of channel proteins**

- **Ligand-gated channels:** a gate opens in the presence of particular ligands, such as hormones or neurotransmitters.
- **Voltage-gated channels:** a gate opens in response to a voltage change/depolarization.

#### Types of transport

The source classification diagram includes:

- **Active transport:** primary active transport and secondary active transport; secondary transport may be symport/co-transport or antiport/counter-transport.
- **Passive transport:** osmosis and diffusion; diffusion is divided into simple, facilitated, and non-ionic diffusion.
- **Endocytosis/exocytosis.**

| Active transport | Passive transport |
|---|---|
| Energy-dependent; ATP-dependent. Moves solute from low to high concentration, against the concentration gradient (“uphill” transport). | Does not require ATP. Moves solute from high to low concentration, down the concentration gradient (“downhill” transport). |

#### Active transport

| Primary active transport | Secondary active transport |
|---|---|
| Direct energy use: ATPase activity breaks down ATP and supplies energy. Examples listed: Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, and H⁺-ATPase. | Indirect energy use. Types: symport and antiport. |

#### Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase subunits and cycle

- The pump is described as a heterodimer with one α subunit and one β subunit.
- **β subunit:** the source calls it the binding subunit and states that it has three glycosylation sites through which it remains attached to extracellular-matrix protein.
- **α subunit:** the source calls it the regulatory subunit.

**Cycle shown in the source**

1. Three Na⁺ bind to the α subunit on the ICF side.
2. ATP is hydrolysed; the α subunit is phosphorylated.
3. A conformational change occurs.
4. Na⁺ is released into the ECF.
5. Two K⁺ bind to the α subunit on the ECF side.
6. Dephosphorylation occurs.
7. The pump returns to its intracellular conformation.
8. K⁺ is released into the ICF.

> **Source wording retained:** the α/β subunit roles above follow the source page; see QA report for an item flagged for review rather than silently reclassified.

### Source page 26

#### SGLT, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, and NCX — comparison notes

**SGLT and primary active transport**

- In SGLT, Na⁺ moves from high to low concentration, down its concentration gradient.
- Glucose moves from lower to higher concentration, against its concentration gradient.
- Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is a primary active transporter: K⁺ moves into the cell against its gradient and Na⁺ moves out against its gradient; one ATP is broken down to ADP.
- SGLT is a secondary active transporter and depends on the primary active transporter. If the primary transporter is blocked, secondary active transport stops functioning.

**NCX (sodium-calcium exchanger)**

- Na⁺ moves along its concentration gradient.
- Ca²⁺ binds to the exchanger on the intracellular side while Na⁺ binds on the extracellular side.
- A conformational change expels one Ca²⁺ and transports three Na⁺ into the cell; coupling ratio Na⁺:Ca²⁺ = **3:1**.
- NCX does not directly use ATP and depends on Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.
- The source states that blocking Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase with digitalis stops NCX; Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ accumulate inside the cell, increasing myocardial contractility.

#### Passive transport

**Simple diffusion**

- Movement of nonpolar or very small molecules from high to low solute concentration without ATP.
- Does not require carrier proteins; it may or may not involve channel proteins.
- Examples: O₂ and CO₂ cross without a carrier/channel because they are lipophilic gases; Na⁺ moves down its concentration gradient through a channel protein.

**Facilitated diffusion**

- Requires a carrier protein.
- Example: glucose moves from high to low concentration through a GLUT carrier.

#### Fick's law and diffusion-rate graphs

The source gives the rate of simple diffusion as:

\[
J = rac{D \cdot A \cdot \Delta C}{X}
\]

- **D:** diffusion constant.
- **A:** surface area of the membrane for diffusion.
- **ΔC:** concentration gradient between two compartments; the source identifies this as the main factor determining the rate of simple diffusion.
- **X:** thickness of the membrane.

**Concentration and rate of transport**

- **Simple diffusion:** increasing substance concentration increases the rate of diffusion steadily.
- **Facilitated diffusion:** initially, transport rate increases with substrate concentration; later, it no longer increases because carriers become saturated. The maximum transport rate is shown as **Tmax**.

#### Non-ionic diffusion

- Occurs in the kidney and GIT for transport of weak acids or bases.
- The source illustrates ammonium handling in the collecting duct and nephron lumen.

| In the kidney's collecting-duct cells | In the lumen of the nephron |
|---|---|
| Glutamine generates ammonium ions and bicarbonate ions. Ammonium ions are broken into H⁺ ions and ammonia gas (NH₃). NH₃ crosses the cell membrane by simple diffusion into the lumen. H⁺ is transported into the lumen by a hydrogen-ATPase pump. | H⁺ and NH₃ combine again in the lumen to form ammonium ions. The formed ammonium ions attempt to return to collecting-duct cells, but cannot. The source states that diffusion of ammonium ions occurs only in non-ionic form. |

#### Exocytosis

- Vesicles inside cells contain packed substances/protein molecules.
- The Golgi body facilitates packing of vesicles.
- Fusion of the vesicle membrane with the cell membrane produces **exocytosis**.
- The process is **Ca²⁺-dependent**.

| Constitutive pathway | Non-constitutive/regulatory pathway |
|---|---|
| Vesicle is directly targeted to the membrane → fuses with membrane → secretion occurs. | Vesicles move toward other parts of the cytoplasm → are stored for some time → undergo preprocessing → secretion occurs. |

#### Endocytosis

The source lists four types:

- **Phagocytosis (“cell eating”):** cells engulf bacteria, dead cells, and other microscopic material. Example: neutrophil.
- **Pinocytosis (“cell drinking”):** much smaller vesicles; ingested substances are in solution.
- **Clathrin-mediated endocytosis:** mediated by clathrin proteins, described as having a triskelion shape.
- **Caveolae-dependent uptake:** mediated by caveolin proteins, which form a hairpin-shaped loop in the membrane.

### Source page 29

#### Caveolae-dependent uptake

- Occurs in cholesterol-rich and sphingolipid-rich regions of the cell membrane (raft regions).
- A depression forms, called a caveola.

#### Clathrin-mediated/receptor-mediated endocytosis

- Clathrin requires ligand attachment to a receptor to facilitate endocytosis.
- Example sequence shown for LDL:
  1. LDL binds to the LDL receptor on the cell membrane (receptor–ligand interaction), recruiting clathrin.
  2. A depression forms containing the receptor–ligand complex, with clathrin outside.
  3. The depression becomes a fully formed vesicle containing the receptor–ligand complex, with a clathrin coat outside.
  4. Dynamin splits the neck of the vesicle, separating it from the cell membrane.
  5. The detached clathrin-coated vesicle contains the receptor–ligand complex.
  6. The diagram shows early endosome, late endosome, and fusion with a lysosome.

**Examples of clathrin-mediated endocytosis**

- LDL receptor-mediated uptake by cells.
- Growth-factor uptake, including nerve growth factor.
- Transferrin uptake.

#### Caveolin-mediated endocytosis

1. A depression forms at a raft.
2. Caveolin is introduced at the depression in the cell membrane.
3. A flask-shaped vesicle forms.
4. Caveolin forms a hairpin-shaped loop on the vesicle.
5. Dynamin breaks the vesicle away from the cell membrane.

- Caveolin-mediated endocytosis is also called **receptor-independent endocytosis** or **raft-dependent endocytosis**.
- Example stated: endothelial cells obtain nutrition from blood through caveolin-mediated endocytosis.

#### SGLT and GLUT

- **SGLT:** secondary active transporter.
- **GLUT:** facilitated diffusion.

| Transporter | Expression site listed in the source |
|---|---|
| SGLT1 | Kidney (PST), intestinal luminal membrane |
| SGLT2 | Kidney (PCT) |
| SGLT3 | Skeletal muscle, small intestine (glucose sensor) |
| GLUT1 | RBC, BBB, placenta; described as universal GLUT |
| GLUT2 | β-cells of pancreas, liver, intestinal epithelium |
| GLUT3 | WBC, neuronal membrane |
| GLUT4 | Insulin-responsive GLUT; muscle and adipose tissue |
| GLUT5 | Fructose transporter; intestinal luminal membrane, sperm |

#### SGLT1 versus SGLT2

| SGLT1 | SGLT2 |
|---|---|
| GIT: transports glucose/galactose and Na⁺ from the lumen; present on the luminal surface. | Present in the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT); absorbs about 90% of glucose and Na⁺ from the kidney; present on the luminal surface. |
| On the basolateral side, glucose enters blood through GLUT2. In the kidney (proximal straight tubule), SGLT1 accounts for 10% of glucose absorption; basolateral glucose exits through GLUT1. | On the basolateral side, glucose enters blood through GLUT2. Coupling ratio: **1:1** (one Na⁺ for one glucose). |
| Coupling ratio: **2:1** (two Na⁺ for one glucose). |  |

#### Important GLUTs

- Insulin-responsive GLUT: **GLUT4 and GLUT12**.
- Insulin-responsive GLUT in blastocyst: **GLUT8**.
- Fructose transporters: **GLUT5 and GLUT11**.
- GLUT in CNS: GLUT1 in endothelial cells of the BBB; GLUT3 in neurons; GLUT5 in astrocytes.
- Major GLUT in placenta and fetus: **GLUT1**.
