# 19. Basal Ganglia and Cerebellum

## Basal ganglia

- The term **ganglia** is a misnomer here:
  - Ganglia are collections of neuronal cell bodies in the peripheral nervous system.
  - The basal ganglia are part of the central nervous system.
- The basal ganglia are a collection of nuclei in the subcortical region of the brain, bilaterally.

![[images/ch19_p136_basal_ganglia_anatomy.png]]

### Parts of the basal ganglia

The source lists the caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra, and nucleus accumbens.

![[images/ch19_p136_parts_of_basal_ganglia_tree.png]]

- **Striatum/neostriatum:** nucleus accumbens + caudate nucleus + putamen.
- **Globus pallidus:** external and internal segments.
- **Substantia nigra:** pars reticularis and pars compacta.
- **Lentiform nucleus:** putamen + globus pallidus.
- **Corpus striatum:** lentiform nucleus + nucleus accumbens + caudate nucleus.

### Neurotransmitters in the basal ganglia

| Site | Neurotransmitter(s) listed in the source |
|---|---|
| Subthalamic nucleus | Glutamate |
| Substantia nigra pars compacta | Dopamine |
| Substantia nigra pars reticularis | GABA |
| Striatum: 95% medium spiny neurons | GABA |
| Striatum: 5% aspiny neurons | Acetylcholine, somatostatin, GABA |
| Globus pallidus externus and internus | GABA |

## General plan of movement

- Basal ganglia and lateral cerebellum: planning and programming of movements.
- Spinocerebellum: smoothing and coordination of movement.

![[images/ch19_p137_general_plan_of_movement.png]]

## Connections and pathways of the basal ganglia

- **Striatum:** input nucleus.
- **Globus pallidus internus:** output nucleus.

![[images/ch19_p137_basal_ganglia_connections.png]]

### Direct and indirect pathways

- **Direct pathway:** Striatum (GABA) → globus pallidus internus (GABA) → thalamus.
- **Indirect pathway:**
  - Striatum (GABA) → globus pallidus externus (GABA) → globus pallidus internus (GABA) → thalamus.
  - Striatum (GABA) → globus pallidus externus (GABA) → subthalamic nucleus (glutamate) → globus pallidus internus (GABA) → thalamus.
- Information reaching the thalamus is projected to the cortex; multiple glutamatergic neurons then project to the striatum, forming a loop.
- Stimulation of the direct pathway activates the thalamus and promotes movement.
- Stimulation of the indirect pathway inhibits the thalamus and suppresses movement.

![[images/ch19_p137_direct_indirect_pathways.png]]

### Centre–surround model

- Both pathways are activated when the cortex signals the basal ganglia.
- The centre zone (direct pathway) facilitates the intended movement.
- The surrounding zone (indirect pathway) inhibits unwanted or competing movements.

## Role of the substantia nigra

- Damage to the substantia nigra is associated with Parkinson’s disease in the source.
- Dopaminergic neurons from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) project to the striatum via the **nigrostriatal pathway**.
- Neurotransmitter of SNpc: dopamine.
- Dopamine acts on both direct and indirect pathways:
  - **D1 receptors:** stimulate the direct pathway → stimulate the thalamus.
  - **D2 receptors:** inhibit the indirect pathway → stimulate the thalamus.
- Damage to dopaminergic neurons of SNpc (Parkinsonism) is associated with reduced thalamic activity, akinesia, bradykinesia, and rigidity (source wording).

![[images/ch19_p138_dopamine_direct_indirect_pathway.png]]

### Tremors in Parkinsonism

- The source states that thalamic pacemaker neurons produce high-frequency tremors (10–14 Hz), described as physiological tremors.
- When thalamic pacemaker neurons are inhibited, high-frequency tremor is converted into slow-frequency coarse tremor (4–8 Hz), described as Parkinsonian tremor.

## Functions and diseases of the basal ganglia

### Functions

| Function | Description in the source |
|---|---|
| Motor | Basal ganglia and motor cortex form a processing loop; the basal ganglia enable the proper motor programme (planning and programming). |
| Cognitive | Basal ganglia form loops with the prefrontal association cortex and limbic cortex. Through these loops, the basal ganglia are thought to play a role in cognitive function; the source specifies the caudate nucleus. The striatum is also involved in skill and procedural memory. |

### Diseases

- **Hypokinetic disease — Parkinsonism:** dopamine deficiency in the substantia nigra; some hyperkinetic features, such as tremor, are also listed.
- **Hyperkinetic disease — Hemiballismus:** subthalamic nucleus is damaged; the source describes ipsilateral, sudden, ballistic, high-amplitude movements.
- **Huntington disease:** striatum (mainly the indirect pathway) is damaged; involuntary movements similar to hemiballismus are listed.
- **Dystonia:** damage to the direct pathway, including basal ganglia, produces a thalamocortical-network problem; slower twisting movements are listed.

### Features of Parkinsonism

- Hypokinetic: akinesia and bradykinesia.
- Hyperkinetic: cogwheel rigidity and tremor at rest (8 Hz, as listed in the source).
- Other features: shuffling gait, flexed posture, reduced facial expression, decreased blinking, small handwriting, depression, anxiety, and cognitive impairment.

### Huntington disease

- Medium spiny neurons in the striatum are damaged (indirect pathway).
- Defect on chromosome 4. The source states that the normal number is 11–34 CAG repeats and that in patients with Huntington disease the number increases to 42–86 or more.
- The greater the number of repeats, the earlier the age of onset and the more rapid the progression.
- Expansion of CAG repeats results in abnormal huntingtin protein.
- An early sign is a jerky trajectory of the hand when reaching to touch a spot.
- Later, hyperkinetic choreiform movements appear.
- Slurred speech, progressive dementia, and death are also listed in later stages.

## Cerebellum

### Anatomical parts

- **Lobes:** anterior, posterior (middle), and flocculonodular lobe.
- The lobes are segmented into multiple lobules:
  - Lobules 1–5: anterior lobe.
  - Lobules 6–9: middle/posterior lobe.
  - Lobule 10: flocculonodular lobe.
- Structures from surface to deep:
  - Cerebellar cortex.
  - Inner white matter.
  - Deep cerebellar nuclei: dentate, fastigial, globose, and emboliform. Globose and emboliform are grouped as the interpositus nucleus in the source.

![[images/ch19_p139_cerebellum_anatomical_parts.png]]

### Layers of the cerebellar cortex

| Layer | Cells and neurotransmitters listed in the source |
|---|---|
| Molecular layer | Basket and stellate cells; both contain inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA. |
| Purkinje layer | Purkinje cells: inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA. |
| Granular layer | Granule cells: excitatory, glutamate. Golgi cells: inhibitory, GABA. |

Proprioceptive input (excitatory input) from various parts of the body reaches the cerebellum. The input is modified at the level of cerebellar interconnections, delivered to the deep cerebellar nuclei, and excitatory output goes to the spinal cord, supporting smoothing and coordination, planning, and programming of movement (sequence as described in the source).

### Internal connections

- Granule-cell axons extend to the cerebellar surface and form **parallel fibres** in the molecular layer.
- Granule cells stimulate basket cells, stellate cells, and Golgi cells:
  - Golgi cells inhibit granule cells, creating negative feedback.
  - Basket and stellate cells inhibit Purkinje cells; Purkinje cells inhibit deep cerebellar nuclei (feed-forward as labelled in the source).
- Input is carried by two ascending fibres:
  - **Mossy fibres:** stimulate deep cerebellar nuclei, granular cells, and Golgi cells; one mossy fibre gives input to multiple neurons.
  - **Climbing fibres:** give input to deep cerebellar nuclei and Purkinje cells (maximum input); one climbing fibre gives input to one Purkinje cell.
- All ascending stimuli reaching the cerebellum ultimately act on Purkinje neurons (called output neurons in the source).
- Purkinje neurons provide inhibitory input to the deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN), but the final DCN output to the spinal cord is excitatory.
- The source states that this modifies output for smoothing and coordination, planning, and programming of movement.
- The synapse between mossy fibres, Golgi-cell axons, and granule-cell dendrites is called the **glomerulus**.

![[images/ch19_p140_cerebellar_cortex_internal_connections.png]]

### Afferent tracts of the cerebellum

- **To climbing fibres (from all body parts):** receives input from olivocerebellar tracts; the source states that olivocerebellar tracts receive proprioceptive input from all body parts.
- **To mossy fibres:**
  - Vestibulocerebellar (labyrinth).
  - Spinocerebellar (muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ).
  - Cuneocerebellar (muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ).
  - Tectocerebellar (visual and auditory).
  - Pontocerebellar (motor and other parts of the cerebral cortex).

### Functional divisions and functions of the cerebellum

| Division | Location/input/output and function listed in the source |
|---|---|
| **Spinocerebellum** | Vermis and inner lateral cerebellum. Smooths and coordinates movement. The vermis receives input from the head, neck, and axial body and sends output to the medial descending spinal tracts. The lateral part receives input from distal body parts/limbs and sends output to the lateral descending spinal pathways. |
| **Cerebrocerebellum** | Outer lateral cerebellar hemispheres on both sides. Plans and programmes movement; receives input from the cortical association area and sends modified output to premotor and motor cortex. The source also lists planning/timing of movement, motor learning by trial and error (e.g. hitting a football), and cognitive functions such as language processing. |
| **Vestibulocerebellum** | Flocculonodular lobe. Receives input from and sends output back to the vestibular nucleus; maintains balance and equilibrium, vestibular reflexes, and posture. |

![[images/ch19_p141_functional_divisions_of_cerebellum.png]]

### Cerebellar diseases

The source mnemonic is **DANISH**:

- **D —** Dysdiadochokinesia, dysmetria (past pointing), decomposition of movement, and delay in initiation of movement.
- **A —** Ataxia (drunken gait).
- **N —** Nystagmus.
- **I —** Intention tremor.
- **S —** Slurred/scanning speech (affecting skilled movements).
- **H —** Hypotonia (on the same side of the body).
- No sensory deficit.
- No abnormalities at rest, except for changes in stretch reflexes.

## MCQs

**1.** A 65-year-old patient has resting tremor, rigidity, and loss of facial expression. Which part of the brain is commonly involved? (NEET 2023)

- a. Hippocampus
- b. Amygdala
- c. Cerebellum
- d. Basal ganglia

**Answer:** (d)

**2.** Hemiballismus is due to a lesion of which structure? (NEET 2022)

- a. Caudate nucleus
- b. Putamen
- c. Substantia nigra
- d. Subthalamic nucleus

**Answer:** (d)

**3.** A cerebellar lesion produces all except (AIIMS 2016):

- a. Ataxic gait
- b. Past pointing
- c. Resting tremor
- d. Nystagmus

**Answer:** (c)
