# Regulation of Respiration

## Classification of mechanisms

Respiration is regulated by neural mechanisms, chemical regulation, and reflexes.

- **Neural regulation**
  1. Voluntary control by the cerebral cortex.
  2. Automatic control:
     - Pons: pneumotaxic centre (controls rate of respiration) and apneustic centre (controls depth of respiration).
     - Medulla: dorsal respiratory group (DRG) and ventral respiratory group (VRG, labelled as pacemaker in the source).
- **Chemical regulation:** by chemoreceptors.
  - Peripheral: carotid and aortic bodies.
  - Central: ventral surface of the medulla.
- **Reflexes:** Hering–Breuer reflex and J-receptor reflex.

## Ondine’s curse

Also called **congenital central hypoventilation syndrome**.

- Pathogenesis:
  - Loss of automatic control of respiration.
  - Voluntary control of respiration remains intact.
- Acquired causes listed in the source: bulbar poliomyelitis, trauma, encephalitis, brainstem infarction, and haemorrhage.

## Neural regulation of respiration

![[images/ch46_p325_neural_respiratory_centres.png]]

### Voluntary control

- Controlled by the cerebral cortex.
- Allows voluntary breath-holding or alteration of breathing patterns.

### Automatic control

#### Pons

- **Pneumotaxic centre**
  - Location: nucleus parabrachial complex, bilaterally.
  - Controls the rate of breathing.
- **Apneustic centre**
  - Location: lower part of the pons, between the pneumotaxic centre and DRG, bilaterally.
  - Controls the depth of breathing.

#### Medulla

- **Dorsal respiratory group (DRG):** receives afferent input from the periphery and acts as a sensory integration centre.
- **Ventral respiratory group (VRG):** contains four subgroups:
  1. **Bötzinger complex:** dominant activity during expiration; contains expiratory neurons.
  2. **Pre-Bötzinger complex:** generates respiratory rhythm and is described as the main pacemaker of respiration; contains inspiratory and expiratory neurons.
  3. **Rostral VRG:** controls airway dilation of the larynx and pharynx; contains inspiratory neurons.
  4. **Caudal VRG:** works during forceful expiration to control accessory respiratory muscles; contains expiratory neurons.

### Neuronal connections: pre-Bötzinger complex

- Contains inspiratory (I) and expiratory (E) neurons with inherent pacemaker activity.
- The neurons mutually inhibit each other:
  - I-neurons discharge for 2–3 seconds and inhibit E-neurons.
  - E-neurons discharge and inhibit I-neurons.
- Pacemaker activity depends on specialised, not fully specified ion channels.
- I-neurons project to the spinal cord, stimulating the phrenic (cervical) and intercostal (thoracic) nerves.
- **Inspiration:** phrenic and intercostal nerve stimulation contracts the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles, expanding the thoracic cavity.
- **Expiration:** after 2–3 seconds of I-neuron discharge, E-neurons activate and inhibit I-neurons; the diaphragm and intercostal muscles relax, resulting in passive expiration.

## Pons, vagal feedback, and cerebral cortex

- The pons contains two centres: the pneumotaxic centre in the upper part and the apneustic centre in the lower part.
- The apneustic centre stimulates I-neurons; apneustic-centre neurons (A-neurons) are described as tonically active.
- The pneumotaxic centre limits inspiration by inhibiting the apneustic centre:
  - Inhibitory control on A-neurons.
  - Stimulatory control on E-neurons.
- **Vagus nerve (stretch receptors):** prolonged inspiration increases tidal volume (the source gives TV > 500 mL as the threshold), activating stretch receptors. Signals travel to the DRG via the vagus nerve and then to the apneustic centre via the vagus nerve, inhibiting the apneustic centre.
- The cerebral cortex influences multiple respiratory centres and is responsible for voluntary control of breathing.

### Effects of brain lesions on respiration

| Lesion | Vagus intact | Vagus cut |
|---|---|---|
| Cut above pons (cerebral cortex separated) | Loss of voluntary control | Loss of voluntary control; increased depth of respiration (as shown in the source). |
| Cut at mid-pons (removes pneumotaxic control) | Slow and deep respiration | Apneusis: deep inspiration followed by spasm of respiration. |
| Cut above medulla (removes pneumotaxic and apneustic centres) | Irregular respiration | Irregular respiration. |
| Cut below medulla | Death | Death. |

## Chemical regulation

### Chemoreceptors

- **Peripheral chemoreceptors:** located in carotid and aortic bodies. These bodies contain chemoreceptor cells called glomus cells.
- **Central chemoreceptors:** located on the ventral surface of the medulla; the source describes chemosensitive neurons there as central chemoreceptors.

| Feature | Carotid body | Aortic body |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Junction of internal and external carotid arteries | Arch of the aorta |
| Shape | Tennis-racket shaped | Tennis-racket shaped |
| Number | Not specified separately | 2–3 |
| Weight | 13 mg | 13 mg |
| Diameter | 2 mm | 2 mm |

### Glomus cells

- **Type I:** main chemoreceptor cells; activated by hypoxia, hypercapnia, and acidosis in arterial blood.
- **Type II:** supporting cells for Type I cells.

![[images/ch46_p328_glomus_cell_types_diagram.png]]

### Mechanism of activation of Type I glomus cells

1. Low PO₂ (hypoxia) closes O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels in glomus cells.
2. K⁺ accumulation inside glomus cells leads to depolarisation.
3. Depolarisation opens calcium channels, allowing Ca²⁺ influx.
4. Ca²⁺ influx causes release of the neurotransmitter dopamine, stimulating afferent nerve endings.
5. The afferent neuron activates respiratory centres (including the VRG), increasing ventilation.
6. Glomus-cell signals travel via:
   - Carotid body → cranial nerve IX (glossopharyngeal nerve).
   - Aortic body → cranial nerve X (vagus nerve).
- The source also lists the neurotransmitter order as **ATP > ACh > dopamine**.
- Hypercapnia and acidosis cause indirect closure of O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels.

### Central chemoreceptors

- Located on the ventral surface of the medulla and mainly stimulated by H⁺ ions.
- Hypoxia cannot stimulate central chemoreceptors according to the source and may depress them.
- H⁺ ions in blood cannot stimulate central chemoreceptors because of the blood–brain barrier.
- Increased PCO₂: CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier, enters CSF, and reacts with H₂O to form H₂CO₃, which dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻. Increased H⁺ in CSF/interstitial fluid (ISF) stimulates central chemoreceptors.

| Feature | Peripheral chemoreceptors | Central chemoreceptors |
|---|---|---|
| Stimuli listed | ↓PO₂, ↑H⁺, ↑PCO₂ | ↑H⁺ ions in CSF/ISF of brain |
| Primary/direct stimulus | ↓PO₂ | ↑H⁺ ions in CSF/ISF of brain |
| Most sensitive stimulus (source wording) | ↑PCO₂ | ↑PCO₂ |

## Response to hypercapnia

![[images/ch46_p329_hypercapnia_response_graph.png]]

- X-axis: PCO₂; Y-axis: ventilation.
- Increasing arterial PCO₂ produces a linear rise in ventilation.
- **Apnoea point:** below PCO₂ of 37 mmHg, ventilation stops temporarily, causing apnoea.
- Ventilation rises linearly as PCO₂ increases to 80 mmHg.
- Beyond 80 mmHg, ventilation ceases to rise and may be depressed.
- The source describes a linear relationship between 40 and 80 mmHg and states that doubling CO₂ doubles ventilation.

## Response to hypoxia

![[images/ch46_p329_hypoxia_response_graph.png]]

- X-axis: PO₂; Y-axis: ventilation.
- Unlike the linear CO₂ curve, the hypoxia response curve is nonlinear.

| PO₂ range | Ventilatory response | Mechanism as given in the source |
|---|---|---|
| PO₂ falls from 100 to 60 mmHg | Hypoxia begins, but ventilation rises modestly | 1. CO₂ washout causes alkalosis, which suppresses chemoreceptors. 2. Increased deoxyhaemoglobin is described as behaving like an alkali and inhibiting chemoreceptors. |
| PO₂ below 60 mmHg | Ventilation increases sharply | Strong hypoxic stimulation overcomes the inhibitory effects attributed to deoxyhaemoglobin and CO₂-washout alkalosis. |

## Combined response to hypoxia and hypercapnia

- X-axis: PCO₂; Y-axis: ventilation; pH is normal for the initial comparison.
- Without hypoxia, hypercapnia stimulates ventilation in a linear fashion.
- Hypoxia plus hypercapnia causes a faster rise in ventilation (leftward curve shift), while the apnoea point remains the same.
  - PO₂ = 60 mmHg: steep rise in ventilation.
  - PO₂ = 40 mmHg: steeper rise in ventilation.

![[images/ch46_p330_combined_response_graph.png]]

### Acidosis

- Normal curves are plotted at pH 7.4.
- In acidosis, the curve is described as shifting left, parallel to the original.
- At pH 7.3 with PO₂ = 100 mmHg, the apnoea point shifts left.
- Adding hypoxia (PO₂ = 60 mmHg) produces another parallel curve; a third curve is shown at PO₂ = 40 mmHg.
- When hypercapnia, hypoxia, and acidosis combine, the source states that the apnoea point is below 37 mmHg.

## Reflex regulation

### Hering–Breuer reflex

| Reflex | Description | Receptor details in the source |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation reflex | Prevents further lung inflation during deep breaths | Stretch receptors in lungs; slow-adapting type; located in smooth muscle of bronchi and bronchioles; activation when TV > 1 litre. |
| Deflation reflex | Strong deflation prevents lung collapse | Stretch receptor and proprioceptors. |

### Head’s paradox reflex

- First described by Henry Head in 1884.
- Lung inflation stimulates further inflation, potentially leading to lung rupture.
- The source states that it is not present in adult lungs and is observed during the first cry of a newborn baby.
- It allows continuous lung inflation until water is expelled from the lungs.

### J-receptor reflex / C-fibre reflex

- Discovered by Dr A. S. Paintal.
- J-receptors are located in lung interstitial tissue close to capillaries.
- They send afferent signals via unmyelinated C-fibres of the vagus nerve.
- Activation is described as occurring only in pathological conditions. Conditions listed:
  - Pulmonary oedema.
  - Pulmonary emboli.
  - Pneumonia.
  - Congestive heart failure (CHF).
  - Lung hyperinflation.
  - Intravenous capsaicin.
- Response to activation: apnoea followed by rapid, shallow breathing, bradycardia, and hypotension.
