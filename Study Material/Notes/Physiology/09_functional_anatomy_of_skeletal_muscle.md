# 09. Functional Anatomy of Skeletal Muscle

## Muscle types and skeletal-muscle hierarchy

Muscle is classified as **skeletal muscle**, **cardiac muscle**, or **smooth muscle**.

Skeletal muscle is composed of multiple bundles of muscle fibres. The structural hierarchy shown is:

1. **Skeletal muscle** — composed of multiple bundles of muscle fibres.
2. **Fascicles** — bundles containing muscle fibres.
3. **Muscle fibres/cells** — composed of multiple myofibrils.
4. **Myofibrils** — contain contractile protein molecules, actin and myosin.
5. **Sarcomere** — structural repeating unit containing thick and thin filaments (myofilaments).

![[images/ch09_p059_muscle_hierarchy.png]]

Each myosin is surrounded by six actin molecules, forming a hexagonal pattern. Under a compound microscope, alternating dark and light bands are seen, producing the striated appearance due to the arrangement of actin and myosin.

## Muscle fibre composition

The muscle fibre is longitudinal in structure.

- **Sarcolemma:** plasma membrane of the muscle fibre. It invaginates into the cytoplasm at certain points, creating **T-tubules** (transverse tubules).
- **Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR):** calcium store associated with the tubular system. The terminal portions of the SR store the maximum calcium and are called **cisternae**. The SR surrounds T-tubules along the length of the muscle cell and are also called L-tubules (longitudinal tubules).
- **Sarcotubular system:** combination of T-tubules and L-tubules.
- **Myofibrils:** multiple fibrillar structures present in the cytoplasm along with multiple nuclei. Their alternating dark and light bands are due to the particular arrangement of actin and myosin and give skeletal muscle its striated appearance.

## Sarcolemma and the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex

Sarcolemmal components are grouped into integral/transmembrane and peripheral proteins.

| Component                       | Description                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Integral/transmembrane proteins | Extend through the cell membrane; examples listed are <strong>dystroglycan</strong> (alpha and beta) and **sarcoglycan**. They remain attached to **laminin**, described as part of the extracellular-matrix protein. |
| Peripheral proteins             | Attach to integral proteins; examples listed are **dystrophin** (long) and <strong>syntrophin</strong> (small). ==They remain attached to the skeletal-muscle cytoskeleton (actin).==                                 |

These proteins provide tensile strength to the muscle membrane. Mutation of these proteins can cause muscle-fibre weakness.

![[images/ch09_p060_dystrophin_glycoprotein_complex.png]]

### Muscular dystrophies

| Disorder | Mutation/protein description | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Duchenne muscular dystrophy** | Complete mutation/absence of dystrophin protein from muscle | Usually fatal by age 30 (with cardiac-muscle involvement). Pseudohypertrophy occurs due to degeneration of muscle fibres and infiltration by fatty tissue, producing bulky muscles. |
| **Becker muscular dystrophy** | Partial mutation/partial absence of dystrophin; dystrophin may be present but altered or reduced | Described as the milder form. Pseudohypertrophy of muscles is also described, from degeneration of muscle fibres and fatty-tissue infiltration. |
| **Limb-girdle muscular dystrophy** | Mutation of the sarcoglycan complex or other components of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex | Arm, shoulder, hip and leg muscles are listed as affected; the dominant symptom is muscle weakness. |

## Sarcotubular system and calcium channels

The sarcotubular system combines T-tubules and L-tubules.

- The **L-tubule** contains a calcium-release channel: **RyR** (ryanodine receptor channel).
- The **T-tubule** contains an ==L-type voltage-gated calcium channel (**CaV**)==, identified as the **DHPR** (dihydropyridine receptor).
- Interaction between CaV and RyR produces **excitation–contraction coupling**; its described function is to pull cisternal calcium into the cytoplasm and cause contraction.

![[images/ch09_p061_sarcotubular_system.png]]

### RyR receptors and calcium-channel types

| Channel/receptor | Location or type |
|---|---|
| RyR1 | Skeletal muscle |
| RyR2 | Myocardial cells |
| RyR3 | Smooth muscle and epithelial cells |
| L-type/DHP receptor | CaV1.1 is present in skeletal muscle; CaV1.2 is present in ==myocardium and smooth muscle== |
| P/Q-type | CaV2.1; neuronal |
| N-type | CaV2.2; neuronal |
| T-type | CaV3.1, CaV3.2 and CaV3.3; ==pacemaker tissue== and osteocyte |

## DHPR–RyR coupling: skeletal versus cardiac muscle

![[images/ch09_p063_dhpr_ryr_coupling.png]]

| Feature | Skeletal muscle | Cardiac muscle |
|---|---|---|
| Junction | **Triadic junction** between the T-tubule and L-tubule | **Dyadic junction** between the T-tubule and L-tubule |
| Cisternae | Present on both sides of the T-tubule | Present on only one side of the T-tubule |
| Channels | CaV1.1 (DHPR) and RyR1 | CaV1.2 and RyR2 |
| Sequence described | Action-potential generation → membrane depolarisation → T-tubule depolarisation → DHPR (CaV1.1) conformational change → mechanical DHPR–RyR1 interaction → RyR opening → calcium enters the cytoplasm from the SR → contraction | Action-potential generation → membrane depolarisation → T-tubule depolarisation → DHPR (CaV1.2) activation/opening → extracellular-fluid Ca²⁺ enters the cell → RyR2 stimulation/opening → further calcium enters the cytoplasm from the SR → contraction |
| Coupling/calcium source | Electromechanical coupling between DHPR and RyR; 100% of the calcium for contraction is cisternal calcium from the SR | Electrochemical coupling between DHPR and RyR; calcium sources are ECF 20% (described as essential) and ICF/SR 80%. Calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) occurs. |

Additional details:

- Size of the sarcoplasmic reticulum: skeletal muscle greater than myocardium.
- Diameter of the T-tubule: myocardium greater than skeletal muscle.

## Type I RyR1 mutation

| Mutation | Disease | Mechanism and features |
|---|---|---|
| Gain-of-function mutation of RyR1 | **Malignant hyperthermia** | Inability of the RyR1 channel to close (“negative feedback”) → increased Ca²⁺ entry. Clinical features: muscle rigidity, high fever and rapid heart rate. Rapid skeletal-muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) causes a high plasma K⁺ level. Treatment listed: **dantrolene**, which antagonises/blocks ryanodine receptors. |
| Loss-of-function mutation of RyR1 | **Central core disease** | Defective RyR1 channel “to close” → decreased Ca²⁺ entry. Features listed: hypotonia at birth, mild delay in child development and facial-muscle weakness. No specific treatment is listed. |

## Mechanism of relaxation

| Skeletal muscle | Cardiac muscle |
|---|---|
| **SERCA pump** (sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic-reticulum Ca²⁺ ATPase) carries out primary active transport and returns cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ to the SR. SERCA is encoded by the **ATP2A1** gene. | SERCA is a primary active transporter that returns cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ to the SR. The **NCX pump** (sodium–calcium exchanger) is an antiporter and secondary active transporter that transports Ca²⁺ back to the ECF. |

## Brody myopathy

- Autosomal-recessive mutations in **ATP2A1**.
- The gene encodes SERCA.
- Stiffness and muscle cramping occur after exercise and are prominent when exposed to cold.
- Muscles of the arms and legs and eyelid muscles are listed as commonly affected.
