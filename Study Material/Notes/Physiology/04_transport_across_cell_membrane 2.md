# 04 — Transport Across Cell Membrane

## General principles of transport (*00:00:42*)

- Small nonpolar, hydrophobic molecules (**O₂ and CO₂**) diffuse rapidly across the cell membrane.
- Small, uncharged, polar molecules (**water or urea**) diffuse across the cell membrane much more slowly.
- Charged molecules (ions) are essentially impermeable to the lipid bilayer.
  - This does not depend on size.
  - Transport across the cell membrane requires a transport protein.

## Membrane transport proteins (*00:02:22*)

![[images/ch04_p023_transport_proteins.png]]

*Carrier-protein and channel-protein schematic.*

| Carrier protein | Channel protein |
|---|---|
| Solute binds to the transport protein → a conformational change occurs → solute is released toward the inner surface of the cell membrane. | Solute binds weakly to the transport protein. |
| Helps in active and passive transport. | No conformational change is described. A pore at the centre facilitates solute transport. |

### Properties of carrier proteins

- **Saturation:** occurs when the number of solute molecules exceeds the number of carrier proteins; all carrier proteins can become saturated with the substances/solutes.
- **Stereospecificity:** a carrier dedicated to one isomer (example: D-glucose) cannot transport another isomer (L-glucose).
- **Competition:** a carrier may transport two solutes (example: glucose and galactose); the solutes compete for binding to the carrier protein.

### Types of channel protein

- **Ligand-gated channels:** contain a gate that opens in the presence of certain ligands, such as hormones or neurotransmitters.
- **Voltage-gated channels:** contain a gate that opens in response to a voltage change/depolarization.

## Types of transport (*00:07:48*)

![[images/ch04_p024_transport_classification.png]]

*Classification of active, passive, and vesicular transport.*

- **Active transport:** primary active transport and secondary active transport; secondary active transport includes symport/co-transport and antiport/counter-transport.
- **Passive transport:** osmosis and diffusion; diffusion is divided into simple, facilitated, and non-ionic diffusion.
- **Endocytosis/exocytosis** are listed separately as vesicular transport processes.

| Active transport | Passive transport |
|---|---|
| Energy-dependent and ATP-dependent. | Does not require ATP for transportation. |
| Moves from low solute concentration to high solute concentration. | Moves from high solute concentration to low solute concentration. |
| Against the concentration gradient: uphill transport. | Downhill transport. |

## Active transport

| Primary active transport | Secondary active transport |
|---|---|
| Direct use of energy: ATPase activity breaks down ATP and the energy is utilized. | Indirect use of energy. |
| Examples: Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, H⁺–K⁺-ATPase, H⁺-ATPase. | Types: symport and antiport. |

## Structure of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (*00:12:29*)

![[images/ch04_p024_nak_atpase_structure.png]]

*Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase with labelled binding sites.*

- The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is described as a **heterodimer** with one α subunit and one β subunit.
- **β subunit** (as labelled in the source):
  - Binding subunit.
  - Three glycosylation sites by which the β subunit remains attached to extracellular-matrix protein.
- **α subunit** (as labelled in the source): regulatory subunit.

> **Source wording retained:** the page labels the β subunit as “binding” and the α subunit as “regulatory”; these descriptions have been transcribed as printed.

### Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase cycle

![[images/ch04_p025_nak_pump_cycle.png]]

*Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase transport cycle.*

1. Three Na⁺ bind to the α subunit on the intracellular side (ICF).
2. ATP hydrolysis occurs.
3. The α subunit is phosphorylated.
4. A conformational change occurs.
5. Na⁺ is released to the extracellular fluid (ECF).
6. Two K⁺ bind to the α subunit on the ECF side.
7. Dephosphorylation occurs.
8. Intracellular conformation is restored.
9. K⁺ is released into the ICF.

The pump moves Na⁺ against its gradient to the outside of the cell and K⁺ against its gradient into the cell; one ATP is broken down to ADP.

## Antiporter and symporter (*00:18:25*)

![[images/ch04_p025_symport_antiport.png]]

*Symport (SGLT) and antiport (NCX) diagrams.*

| Symporter/co-transport | Antiporter/counter-transport |
|---|---|
| Example: **SGLT** (sodium-glucose-linked cotransporter). Present in the GIT; SGLT1 is on the luminal membrane. | Example: **NCX** (sodium-calcium exchanger). Present in myocardium. |
| Glucose and sodium bind to SGLT simultaneously; a conformational change in the carrier protein releases glucose and sodium together inside the cell. | Calcium in myocardium triggers muscle contraction. After contraction, calcium is pushed outside the cells. |
| Sodium moves from high to low concentration (along its concentration gradient). Glucose moves from lower to higher concentration (against its concentration gradient). | Sodium moves along its concentration gradient while calcium is transported from inside to outside, against its gradient. One Ca²⁺ is expelled and three Na⁺ are transported inside; coupling ratio Na⁺:Ca²⁺ = **3:1**. |

### Relationship to Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase

- In Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, K⁺ moves against its gradient into the cell and Na⁺ moves against its gradient out of the cell.
- One ATP is broken down to one ADP, and energy is used by the primary active transporter.
- SGLT is a secondary active transporter and depends on the primary active transporter.
- If the primary transporter is blocked, the secondary active transporter stops functioning.
- NCX does not utilize ATP directly; it depends on Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase to function.
- The source states that digitalis blocks Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, causing NCX to stop functioning; Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ accumulate inside the cell, increasing myocardial contractility.

## Passive transport (*00:28:39*)

![[images/ch04_p026_passive_transport.png]]

*Simple diffusion, channel-mediated movement, facilitated diffusion, and active transport schematic.*

### Simple diffusion

- Movement of nonpolar or very small molecules from high to low solute concentration without requiring ATP.
- Does not require carrier proteins.
- May or may not involve channel proteins.
- Examples:
  - CO₂/O₂ transport: high to low concentration without carrier/channel proteins because these are lipophilic gaseous molecules.
  - Na⁺ transport: high to low concentration via a channel protein.

### Facilitated diffusion

- Requires a carrier protein.
- Example: glucose transport from high to low concentration by a carrier protein (GLUT).

## Fick's law and diffusion-rate graphs

- Rate of transport is determined by **Fick's law of simple diffusion**.

\[
J = \frac{D \cdot A \cdot \Delta C}{X}
\]

- **D:** diffusion constant.
- **A:** surface area of the membrane (of diffusion).
- **ΔC:** concentration gradient between two compartments; described as the main factor determining the rate of simple diffusion.
- **X:** thickness of the membrane.

![[images/ch04_p027_diffusion_rate_graph.png]]

*Rate versus substrate concentration for simple and facilitated diffusion.*

- **Simple diffusion:** as substance concentration increases, the rate of simple diffusion increases steadily.
- **Facilitated diffusion:** initially, the rate of transport increases with substrate concentration. Later, the rate stops increasing because carrier proteins are saturated by solutes. The maximum amount of solute transport at maximum saturation is the **transport maximum (Tmax)**.

## Non-ionic diffusion (*00:38:00*)

- Non-ionic diffusion occurs in the kidney and GIT, for the transportation of weak acids or bases.

![[images/ch04_p027_nonionic_diffusion.png]]

*Non-ionic diffusion and ammonium trapping in the nephron.*

### In the kidney's collecting-duct cells

- Glutamine generates ammonium ions and bicarbonate ions.
- Ammonium ions are broken into H⁺ ions and ammonium gas (as worded in the notes).
- Ammonium gas penetrates the cell membrane and enters the lumen of the nephron by simple diffusion.
- H⁺ is transported into the nephron lumen via the hydrogen ATPase pump.

### In the lumen of the nephron

- Both components (H⁺ ions and ammonium gas) combine again inside the lumen to form ammonium ions.
- The formed ammonium ions attempt to return to the kidney's collecting-duct cells, but this is not possible.
- The notes state: “Diffusion of ammonium ions occur only in non-ionic form.” This wording is retained as printed.

## Exocytosis (*00:44:30*)

- Vesicles are present inside cells and contain packed substances/protein molecules.
- The Golgi body facilitates packing of vesicles.
- Fusion of the vesicular membrane with the cell membrane produces **exocytosis**.
- It is a **Ca²⁺-dependent** process.

![[images/ch04_p028_exocytosis_vesicle.png]]

*Vesicular exocytosis.*

| Constitutive pathway | Non-constitutive pathway / regulated pathway |
|---|---|
| Vesicle is directly targeted toward the membrane → fuses with the membrane → secretion occurs. | Vesicles move toward other parts of the cytoplasm → are stored for some time → vesicle preprocessing occurs → secretion takes place. |

## Endocytosis (*00:47:01*)

![[images/ch04_p028_endocytosis_types.png]]

*Endocytosis pathways: phagocytosis, pinocytosis, clathrin-mediated uptake, and caveolae-dependent uptake.*

- **Phagocytosis (cell eating):** bacteria, dead cells, or other bits of microscopic material are engulfed by cells. Example: neutrophil.
- **Pinocytosis (cell drinking):** vesicles are much smaller, and the ingested substances are in solution.
- **Clathrin-mediated endocytosis:** mediated by clathrin proteins (described as a triskelion-shaped protein).
- **Caveolae-dependent uptake:** mediated by caveolin proteins, which form a hairpin loop in the membrane.

![[images/ch04_p029_phagocytosis.png]]

*Phagocytosis.*

### Caveolae-dependent uptake

- Occurs in cholesterol-rich and sphingolipid-rich regions of the cell membrane, described as the **raft region**.
- A depression forms, producing a caveola.

### Clathrin-mediated/receptor-mediated endocytosis

- Clathrin requires ligand attachment to a receptor to facilitate endocytosis.
- LDL binds to the LDL receptor on the cell membrane (receptor–ligand reaction) → clathrin protein is recruited.
- A depression forms in the cell membrane, containing the receptor–ligand complex with clathrin outside.
- The depression becomes a fully formed vesicle containing the receptor–ligand complex, with clathrin coating outside.
- Dynamin splits the neck region of the vesicle and cuts it from the cell membrane.
- The detached clathrin-coated vesicle contains the receptor–ligand complex.
- The pathway proceeds through an early endosome → late endosome → fusion with a lysosome.

![[images/ch04_p029_clathrin_endocytosis.png]]

*Clathrin-mediated/receptor-mediated endocytosis pathway.*

**Examples of clathrin-mediated endocytosis**

- LDL receptor-mediated uptake by the cell.
- Growth-factor uptake by the cell: nerve growth factor.
- Transferrin uptake by the cell.

### Caveolin-mediated endocytosis (*00:54:54*)

- Also called **receptor-independent endocytosis** or **raft-dependent endocytosis**.
- At the raft, a depression forms.
- Caveolin protein is introduced at the level of the depression on the cell membrane.
- A flask-shaped vesicle forms at the site of depression.
- In the vesicle, caveolin protein forms a hairpin-shaped loop.
- Dynamin protein breaks the vesicle from the cell membrane; endocytosis is mediated by caveolin protein.
- Example stated: endothelial cells obtain nutrition from blood via caveolin-mediated endocytosis.

![[images/ch04_p029_caveolin_endocytosis.png]]

*Caveolin-mediated endocytosis pathway.*

## SGLT and GLUT (*00:57:47*)

- **SGLT:** secondary active transporter.
- **GLUT:** facilitated diffusion.

| Transporter | Expression site listed |
|---|---|
| SGLT1 | Kidney (PST); intestinal luminal membrane |
| SGLT2 | Kidney (PCT) |
| SGLT3 | Skeletal muscle; small intestine (glucose sensor) |
| GLUT1 | RBC, BBB, placenta (“universal GLUT”) |
| GLUT2 | β-cells of pancreas, liver, and intestinal epithelium |
| GLUT3 | WBC, neuronal membrane |
| GLUT4 | Insulin-responsive GLUT; muscle and adipose tissue |
| GLUT5 | Fructose transporter; luminal membrane of intestine; sperm |

### SGLT1

- In the GIT, transports glucose/galactose and Na⁺ from the lumen.
- Present on the luminal surface.
- On the basolateral aspect, glucose enters the blood through **GLUT2**.
- In the kidney (proximal straight tubule), accounts for **10%** of glucose absorption.
- On the basolateral aspect in the kidney's proximal straight tubule, glucose enters blood through **GLUT1**.
- Coupling ratio for SGLT1: **2:1**, as two Na⁺ molecules are transported for one glucose molecule.

### SGLT2

- Present in the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), where it absorbs **90%** of glucose and Na⁺ from the kidney.
- Present on the luminal surface.
- On the basolateral aspect, glucose enters blood through **GLUT2**.
- Coupling ratio for SGLT2: **1:1**, as one Na⁺ is transported for one glucose molecule.

### Important GLUTs

- Insulin-responsive GLUTs: **GLUT4 and GLUT12**.
- Insulin-responsive GLUT in the blastocyst: **GLUT8**.
- Fructose transporters: **GLUT5 and GLUT11**.
- GLUTs in the CNS:
  - GLUT1: endothelial cells of the blood–brain barrier (BBB).
  - GLUT3: neurons.
  - GLUT5: astrocytes.
- Major GLUT in placenta and fetus: **GLUT1**.
