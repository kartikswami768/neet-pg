# 15. Nociceptors

Pain receptors detect damaging stimuli acting on the body.

## Fast pain and slow pain

- **Fast pain:** first pain.
- **Slow pain:** second pain.
- Pain receptors are described as polymodal because they can be activated by mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli.
- When nociceptors are activated by mechanical or thermal stimuli, the pain perceived is fast pain.
- Chemical stimuli always produce slow pain, although slow pain can also be produced by mechanical, chemical and thermal stimuli.

![[images/ch15_p106_fast_vs_slow_pain_graph.png]]

| Fast pain | Slow pain |
|---|---|
| Example in the source: striking the dorsum of the hand with a hammer causes immediate pain. | Pain persists in the damaged area for a long duration. |
| Sharp and high intensity, but of shorter duration. | Lower intensity than fast pain, persisting for longer. |
| Carried by Aδ fibres (fast conduction velocity), so pain is felt immediately. | Carried by C fibres (unmyelinated, very slow conduction), producing late perception that persists longer. |
| Aδ neuron neurotransmitter at the spinal-cord synapse is written as **“Glutamine”** in the source. | Neurotransmitter produced by C fibres: mostly substance P (sometimes written “Glutamine” in the source). Substance P is released slowly and EPSP generation takes time; the source contrasts this with “Glutamine,” which is released quickly and produces a fast EPSP. |
| Pain is localised and originates from a superficial part of the body (mostly superficial Aδ fibres). | Deep tissue, muscle and viscera → slow/dull pain; poorly localised. |

## Nociceptors and transient receptor potential (TRP) channels

- Nociceptors are located at free nerve endings.
- The end of the neuron lacks myelination and is therefore called a free nerve ending.
- Free nerve endings contain touch receptors, pain receptors and thermoreceptors.
- Multiple ion channels at free nerve endings are dedicated to pain and temperature detection; these are called **TRP channels** (transient receptor potential channels).
- TRP-channel stimulation → ions (mostly Ca²⁺ in the source) enter the nerve terminal → depolarisation → action-potential generation → transmission to the CNS.

![[images/ch15_p107_trp_nociceptor_channel_classification.png]]

### Mechanical nociceptors

- **TRPA1** (A-ankyrin); other types of TRP channels are also listed.
- With mechanical pain, tissue can release ATP → stimulation of P2X/P2Y receptors.
- **MRGPR** (Mas-related G-protein-coupled receptor) detects sharp-object-mediated mechanical pain.

### Thermal nociceptors

#### Cold receptors

- TRPA1: activated below 17°C.
- TRPM (M-menthol): activated at 8–28°C.

#### Warm receptors

- TRPV1 (polymodal channel): activated above 43°C; also activated by H⁺ ions and capsaicin.
- TRPV2: activated above 52°C.
- TRPV3: 33–39°C.
- TRPV4: 25–34°C; the source notes activation at normal skin temperature (~32°C).

### Chemical stimuli / tissue injury

- Bradykinin acts on B₁ and B₂ receptors.
- Serotonin acts on 5-HT receptors.
- Histamine acts on H₃ and H₄ receptors as listed in the source.
- H⁺ activates ASIC (acid-sensing ion channels).
- NGF (nerve growth factor) acts on tyrosine kinase A.
  - NGF changes the neuron's phenotype and increases pain sensitivity in some neurons.

### Nociceptor activators and sensitisers

| Pain-producing substances | Pain sensitisers |
|---|---|
| Directly activate the pain receptor/neuron. Examples listed: bradykinin (most potent), serotonin, K⁺ and histamine. | Do not produce pain on their own but increase pain-receptor sensitivity. Examples listed: prostaglandin, leukotriene and substance P. |

## Pain pathways

- Sensory fibres carrying pain from the periphery enter through the dorsal region of the spinal cord.
- At the spinal-cord level, glutamate (Aδ fibres) or substance P (C fibres) acts on the cell body of a second-order neuron.
- Axons of the second-order neuron cross to the other side of the spinal cord and ascend to the thalamus.
- A third-order neuron carries sensation from the thalamus to the somatosensory cortex.
- Depending on whether pain is carried by Aδ or C fibres, it follows the **neospinothalamic** or **paleospinothalamic** pathway.

![[images/ch15_p108_first_to_third_order_pain_pathway.png]]

### Neospinothalamic pathway (Aδ fibres)

- Aδ fibres enter the spinal cord and synapse with a second-order neuron that reaches the thalamus.
- The first-/second-order synapse is at **Lamina I** of the spinal cord.
- Almost all second-order neurons end in the thalamus.
- The thalamic nuclei listed are the **ventroposterolateral (VPL)** and **ventroposteromedial (VPM)** nuclei.
- Pain perception/localisation occurs at the thalamus; the cortex determines pain quality (sharp, dull, stabbing).

![[images/ch15_p108_neospinothalamic_pathway.png]]

### Paleospinothalamic pathway / old pathway (C fibres)

- C fibres (first-order neurons) enter the spinal cord and synapse with second-order neurons.
- Glutamate is listed as the neurotransmitter between first- and second-order neurons.
- Only 10–25% of second-order fibres reach the thalamus and cortex; most reach other brain regions.
- C-fibre-mediated pain is poorly localised because only 10–25% of fibres reach the thalamus, where localisation occurs.
- Other projections reach, for example:
  - Reticular nucleus of the reticular activating system.
  - Tectal region of the midbrain.
  - Periaqueductal grey matter region of the midbrain.
  - The source states that fibres from these regions extend to various hypothalamic nuclei.
- C-fibre-mediated pain reaches various brainstem regions and is associated in the source with chronic suffering produced by slow pain.
- Chronic slow pain can disturb sleep patterns because of reticular-nucleus activity.
- Reticular, tectal and periaqueductal grey-matter fibres reach various thalamic nuclei; some thalamic fibres are responsible for autonomic control. Chronic slow pain can therefore produce autonomic symptoms.

![[images/ch15_p109_paleospinothalamic_pathway.png]]

### Thalamic pain syndrome / Dejerine–Roussy syndrome

- Develops after a thalamic stroke (ischaemic or haemorrhagic) destroying the VPL/VPM nucleus.
- Damage to the thalamic nuclei causes miscommunication between afferent pathways and the cerebral cortex, changing what/how a person feels.
- Symptoms listed: burning, tingling and hypersensitivity, including allodynia and dysaesthesia (unpleasant, abnormal sense of touch) throughout the body.

## Analgesic system of the CNS

The source distinguishes a **spinal mechanism** from a **supraspinal mechanism**.

- **Spinal mechanism:** operates at the spinal-cord level; gate-control theory of pain is at the dorsal-root region.
- **Supraspinal mechanism:** nuclei above the spinal cord, including the periventricular area around the third ventricle and the periaqueductal grey-matter region of the midbrain.

### Gate-control theory of pain

- Proposed by Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall (1965); the source adds “given Nobel prize in medicine.”
- First-order C- or Aδ-fibre neurons from the periphery enter the dorsal horn.
- They synapse at Lamina II (substantia gelatinosa) with a second-order neuron ascending to the thalamus.
- The synapse between first- and second-order neurons forms the gate for pain sensation.
- Lamina II contains an inhibitory **G (gelatinosa) neuron**.
- The G neuron is activated by other spinal-cord fibres (Aβ or Aδ, as written in the source).
- Aβ fibres carry touch and pressure, enter the spinal cord and synapse with the second-order touch-pathway neuron. Branches from Aβ fibres activate the G neuron in Lamina II, inhibiting the pain pathway between first- and second-order neurons.
- If the Aβ fibre is not stimulated, the gate remains open; stimulation closes the gate and reduces pain sensation.
- Massage, acupuncture and application of balm around the painful area are listed as examples of closing the gate.
- **TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation):** electrodes on the skin produce electrical current that stimulates Aα and Aβ fibres → closes the gate.

![[images/ch15_p110_gate_control_theory.png]]

- Acupuncture is described as reducing pain by closing the spinal-cord gate and secreting endogenous opioids.

### Supraspinal mechanism of pain reduction

- Neurons from the periaqueductal grey-matter region and periventricular region surrounding the third ventricle project to brainstem nuclei, including the raphe nucleus, locus coeruleus and rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), and stimulate them.
- On stimulation, neurons from these nuclei project to the spinal cord.
- Neurotransmitters listed: raphe nucleus → serotonin; locus coeruleus → norepinephrine; RVLM → norepinephrine.
- They act on spinal-cord neurons/interneurons and produce **enkephalin**.
- Enkephalin (endogenous opioid) inhibits first-order neuron terminals and second-order neuron dendrites, reducing pain transmission at the spinal cord and reducing pain perception at the thalamus.
- A small amount of morphine given in/around the periaqueductal grey-matter or periventricular region is described as suppressing pain perception to a very low level. Morphine at the spinal-cord level reduces perception to a lower level. The source also describes activation of the PAG/PV pathway and inhibition of presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons.

![[images/ch15_p111_descending_analgesic_system.png]]

### Hyperalgesia and allodynia

- **Hyperalgesia:** exaggerated response to a noxious stimulus.
- **Allodynia:** pain in response to a normally innocuous stimulus, such as touch or a little cold.
- The source attributes both to increased sensitivity of nociceptive fibres.

### Pain-insensitive structures of the brain

- **Listed as pain-insensitive:** brain parenchyma, ependyma, choroid plexus, pia mater and arachnoid.
- **Pain-sensitive:** dura mater.

- a. Inhibitory neurotransmitter at the cerebral cortex
- b. Adrenergic receptor stimulation
