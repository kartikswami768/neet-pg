# 25. Functional Anatomy of the Respiratory System

## Weibel's model

- Total divisions: 23.
- The 23rd division corresponds to alveoli.
- There are approximately 300–500 million alveoli in both lungs.
- Total surface area: 50–100 m² (average: 70 m²).

| Region | Divisions / structures | Function and features |
|---|---|---|
| Trachea | Division 0 | Trachea. |
| Conducting zone | Divisions 1–10: bronchi; divisions 11–16: terminal bronchioles | Air passage; no gas exchange. |
| Exchange zone | Divisions 17–19: respiratory bronchioles; divisions 20–22: alveolar ducts; division 23: alveoli | Gas exchange starts with alveolar budding in respiratory bronchioles and continues through the exchange zone. |

## Dead space

Air enters the respiratory system but does not take part in gaseous exchange.

### Anatomical dead space

- Anatomical dead-space air is the air present in the conducting zone.
- Conducting-zone volume equals anatomical dead-space volume.
- Conducting-zone/anatomical dead-space volume = **2.2 × body weight (kg)**.
- Dilation or an increase in the length of the conducting zone increases conducting-zone volume.
- **Single-breath nitrogen washout method / Fowler's method:** measures anatomical dead space and closing volume.

### Alveolar dead space

- Air present in alveoli but not taking part in gas exchange.
- Examples: embolism and emphysema.
- In a normal person, alveolar dead space is zero.

### Physiological dead space

- Anatomical dead space + alveolar dead space.
- In a normal person, physiological dead space equals anatomical dead space.
- In embolism or emphysema, physiological dead space is greater than anatomical dead space.
- It is calculated by the Bohr mixing equation:

\[
\text{Physiological dead space} = TV \times \frac{\text{Alveolar CO}_2 - \text{Expired CO}_2}{\text{Alveolar CO}_2}
\]

## Histology of the respiratory tract

![[images/ch38_p282_respiratory_histology_diagram.png]]

| Structure | Epithelium and features |
|---|---|
| Trachea and bronchi | Pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium; contain cartilage, glands and smooth muscle. |
| Bronchioles | Cuboidal-ciliated epithelium; respiratory bronchioles contain smooth muscle; cartilage and glands are absent; Clara cells are present (the source says there are no cilia on the surface). |
| Alveoli | Simple squamous epithelium; Type I and Type II pneumocytes; cartilage, glands and smooth muscle are absent. |

### Clara cells / club cells

- Found in bronchioles only.
- Non-ciliated, non-mucous, secretory cells.
- Functions:
  - Major secretory cell of bronchiolar epithelium; secretes uteroglobin (CC10) protein, with anti-inflammatory properties.
  - Clara cells secrete proteins that cause stabilization of surfactant.
  - Principal site for xenobiotic metabolism within the lung.
  - Stem-cell pool for ciliated epithelium.
- In humans, many forms of lung cancer may originate from Clara cells, including adenocarcinoma.

## Stem cells

1. Basal and mucous cells of the proximal airway: major stem cells of the respiratory system.
2. Clara cells in the bronchioles: termed “variant Clara cell” in the source.
3. Type II pneumocyte in the alveoli: stem cell for Type I pneumocyte.

## Pulmonary neuro-endocrine cells (PNECs)

- Epithelial cell type lining large and small airways, throughout the airway.
- Chemosensitive cells that detect hypoxia.
- Secrete dopamine, serotonin and gastrin-releasing peptide.
- PNECs are considered the cell origin for small-cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and carcinoid.
- PNEC hyperplasia is reported in patients with asthma, COPD and bronchopulmonary dysplasia.

## Cells of alveoli

### Type I pneumocytes

- Responsible for gas exchange.
- Contain aquaporin-V channels for water transport.

### Type II pneumocytes

- Thick cytoplasm; have lamellar bodies storing surfactant.
- The source states that they secrete α₁-antitrypsin to protect alveoli.
- Secrete IL-8 and IL-1β.
- Responsible for xenobiotic metabolism.

### Type III pneumocytes

- Also called brush cells.

## Airway resistance

- Calculated by the Hagen–Poiseuille equation.
- Key formula shown in the source:

\[
R = \frac{8\eta L}{\pi r^4}
\]

- The source describes resistance as inversely proportional to airway radius/diameter.
- Small airways (less than 2 mm in diameter): 10–20%.
- Medium to large bronchi: 40%.
- Upper airway (pharynx and larynx): 40%.
- Maximum airway resistance: lobar and segmental bronchi (2–4 mm diameter; medium/large bronchi), corresponding to Weibel divisions 4–5.
- Airway resistance peaks at divisions 4–5 (lobar/segmental bronchi).

### Volume versus resistance

- Resistance decreases as lung volume increases.
- Maximum resistance: residual volume (RV).
- Minimum resistance: total lung capacity (TLC).

![[images/ch38_p284_resistance_vs_lung_volume.png]]

## Velocity of airflow

- Velocity is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.
- **Cross-sectional area:**
  - Minimum at division 3.
  - Maximum at division 23.
- **Velocity:**
  - Maximum at division 3.
  - Minimum at division 23.
- Turbulence of airflow is maximum at the trachea.
