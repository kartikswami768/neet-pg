# Carbon Dioxide Transport

## CO₂ transport

### Partial pressure and content

- Alveolar PCO₂: **40 mmHg**.
- Arterial blood: PCO₂ = **40 mmHg**; CO₂ content = **48 mL/dL**.
- Venous blood: PCO₂ = **46 mmHg**; CO₂ content = **52 mL/dL**.
- Difference in CO₂ content between venous and arterial blood: **4 mL/dL**, representing CO₂ obtained from tissues.
- The source states that approximately **5%** of CO₂ is transported in plasma and that the majority is transported in red blood cells (RBCs), via haemoglobin or bicarbonate.

### Forms of CO₂ transport

| Form | Plasma level in the source | RBC level in the source | Details given in the source |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Dissolved CO₂ (D-CO₂) | 5% | 5% | Dissolved form. |
| Carbamino compound (CO₂–NH₂) | 1% | 21% | In plasma, formed by protein (8 g%); in RBCs, haemoglobin forms CO₂–NH₂ complexes, stated as 33%. |
| Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) | 5% | 63% | RBC bicarbonate formation occurs via carbonic anhydrase. The source states that carbonic anhydrase accelerates HCO₃⁻ formation 5,000 times faster inside RBCs. |

The percentages and compartment labels above are transcribed from the source table; the relationships are not independently reconciled.

## CO₂ transport at tissues: Bohr effect

- When pH decreases or PCO₂ increases, blood oxygen affinity decreases and P50 increases, promoting release of O₂ to tissues.
- The **Bohr effect** is a rightward shift of the oxygen dissociation curve caused by changes in CO₂ concentration or pH.
- Tissue metabolism releases CO₂, which diffuses into plasma and RBCs.
- CO₂ in plasma is represented as:
  - Dissolved CO₂ (D-CO₂).
  - Carbamino compounds (CO₂–NH₂).
  - Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻).
- Within RBCs, CO₂ reacts with H₂O through carbonic anhydrase to form H₂CO₃, which dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻.
- RBCs contain oxyhaemoglobin (HbO₂). H⁺ binds HbO₂, shown in the source as:

  \(\mathrm{H^+ + HbO_2 \rightarrow HHb + O_2}\)

- O₂ then crosses the RBC membrane into plasma and is delivered to tissues.
- Release of O₂ in the presence of CO₂/H⁺ is identified as the Bohr effect.
- The separate effects of pH and CO₂ are described as qualitatively similar, but the effect of H⁺ (the pH Bohr effect) is stated to be much stronger than the CO₂ effect.

## Chloride shift (Hamburger effect)

- When HCO₃⁻ is high inside RBCs, it crosses the RBC membrane to enter plasma in exchange for Cl⁻.
- The transporter responsible is the **anion exchanger / Band 3 protein**.
- The source states that increased Cl⁻ inside RBCs increases RBC osmotic tension, water enters RBCs, and PCV increases in venous blood.
- PCV of venous blood is stated to be approximately **3% higher** than that of arterial blood.

## CO₂ transport at the lungs: Haldane effect

- RBCs in venous blood contain HHb (deoxyhaemoglobin).
- O₂ from alveoli enters RBCs and reacts with HHb to form HbO₂ (oxyhaemoglobin).
- The source describes HbO₂ as more acidic and unable to bind H⁺ ions, so H⁺ is released.
- **Reverse chloride shift:** Cl⁻ exits RBCs as HCO₃⁻ re-enters through antiporters.
- H⁺ reacts with HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃; H₂CO₃ dissociates into CO₂ and H₂O.
- CO₂ is released from RBCs into the alveoli for expiration.
- As HCO₃⁻ re-enters and Cl⁻ exits, osmotic tension decreases and excess water is released; the source states that PCV is thereby normalised.
- **Haldane effect:** Oxygenation of blood in the lungs displaces CO₂ from haemoglobin and increases CO₂ removal. The Haldane effect is described as the shift in the CO₂ dissociation curve caused by oxygenation of haemoglobin.

## CO₂ dissociation curve

![[images/ch45_p323_co2_dissociation_curve.png]]

- X-axis: PCO₂.
- Y-axis: volume/content of CO₂ (the graph labels blood CO₂ content as volume %).

| Variable | Venous blood | Arterial blood |
|---|---:|---:|
| PO₂ | 40 mmHg | 100 mmHg |
| PCO₂ | 46 mmHg | 40 mmHg |
| CO₂ content | 52 mL/dL | 48 mL/dL |

- The curve shifts downward at high PO₂ (arterial level), lowering CO₂ content.
- The difference in CO₂ content between artery and vein is **4 mL/dL**.
- Downward shifting of the CO₂ dissociation curve is identified as the Haldane effect.
- Without downward shifting of the curve, the source states that the artery–vein difference in CO₂ content would be **2 mL/dL**.
- The Haldane effect is stated to approximately double the amount of CO₂ released from haemoglobin in the lungs and double CO₂ pickup in tissues.
