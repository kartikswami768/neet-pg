# High Altitude Physiology

## Altitude categories

- **High altitude:** 1,500–3,500 m (5,000–11,500 ft).
- **Very high altitude:** 3,500–5,000 m (11,500–18,000 ft).
- **Extreme high altitude:** >5,000 m (>18,000 ft).

## Barometric pressure

- Barometric pressure and altitude have an inverse relationship.
- At high altitude, barometric pressure (Pᵦ) decreases.
- The source graph has barometric pressure on the X-axis and altitude on the Y-axis.
- At sea level, barometric pressure = **1 ATM**.
- The source states that barometric pressure decreases by 50% for every 5,000 m of altitude:
  - 5,000 m = ½ ATM.
  - 10,000 m = ¼ ATM.

## Partial pressure

### Mount Everest example

- Height = 8,849 m (approximated in the source as ~10,000 m).
- Barometric pressure (Pᵦ) ≈ 253 mmHg (¼ of 760 mmHg).
- The source states that the composition of air remains the same.
- Partial pressure = Pᵦ × percentage of the gas in atmospheric air.
- As Pᵦ decreases at high altitude, partial pressures of O₂, N₂, and CO₂ decrease.

**Oxygen partial-pressure values given in the source:**

- PO₂ at Mount Everest: `(253 × 21/100) = 53 mmHg`.
- Partial pressure of O₂ in inspiratory air: `(253 − 47) × 21/100 = 43 mmHg`.
- Alveolar PO₂ at Mount Everest: 40 mmHg.
- Alveolar PO₂ at sea level: 100–104 mmHg.

## Problems at high altitude

- **Hypoxic hypoxia:** decreased PO₂.
- **Hypothermia:** for every 150 m of ascent, temperature decreases by approximately 1°C; Mount Everest temperature is given as approximately −30°C.
- **Increased insensible water loss:**
  - Normal: approximately 700 mL daily (50% through skin, 50% through respiratory tract).
  - High altitude: approximately 4 L/day.
- **Solar radiation:** identified by the source as a major problem contributing to hypoxia.

## Acclimatization

- The source defines **adaptation** as long-term changes and **acclimatization** as short-term adjustments.

### Changes during acclimatization

- **Ventilation increases:** fresh O₂ is inhaled and CO₂ exhaled; tidal volume and respiratory rate increase.
- **Diffusion capacity increases:** the source links this to increased ventilation and perfusion.
- **RBCs and haemoglobin increase:** hypoxia stimulates erythropoietin release from the kidneys, producing more RBCs and haemoglobin.
- **Capillary density increases:** new capillaries form by angiogenesis. In response to hypoxia, cells produce more HIF-α, upregulating genes such as VEGF and increasing angiogenesis.
- **Mitochondria and cytochrome oxidase increase:** the source states that these improve oxygen utilisation and attributes this change to HIF-α.

### Change in ventilation

![[images/ch48_p339_ventilatory_acclimatization_curve.png]]

- Hypoxic hypoxia stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors and the respiratory centre, increasing tidal volume (TV) and respiratory rate (RR), and thus ventilation within 1–2 hours.
- CO₂ washout causes respiratory alkalosis.
- **Braking effect on respiration:** alkalosis inhibits peripheral chemoreceptors, decreasing ventilation.
- Over 2–3 days, the kidneys compensate for alkalosis by excreting bicarbonate and retaining H⁺ ions.
- Increased bicarbonate excretion decreases CSF bicarbonate, causing CSF acidosis.
- CSF acidosis stimulates central chemoreceptors, increasing ventilation to **1.65 times normal**, according to the source.
- Ventilation remains high until alkalosis is corrected.

### Oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve

- Alkalosis causes a temporary left shift, attributed in the source to low 2,3-DPG levels.
- Prolonged alkalosis increases 2,3-DPG, resulting in a permanent right shift.
- A right shift enhances oxygen unloading in tissues.

## Other changes due to hypoxia at high altitude

### Haematological

- Hypoxia stimulates erythropoietin (EPO) from the kidneys.
- EPO strongly stimulates the colony-forming unit of erythroblasts, increasing RBC and haemoglobin production over 3–5 days.

### Cardiovascular system

- Hypoxia stimulates the rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), causing sympathetic stimulation → increased heart rate (immediate change), increased cardiac output, and a moderate increase in blood pressure.

### Pulmonary and systemic vasculature

| Pulmonary vasculature | Systemic vasculature |
|---|---|
| Patchy vasoconstriction. | Vasodilation. |
| Smooth muscle contains O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels. | Smooth muscle contains ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels. |
| Hypoxia closes O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels → increased intracellular K⁺ (as printed) → depolarisation → opening of voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ influx → smooth-muscle contraction and vasoconstriction. | Hypoxia is shown as decreasing ATP production → stimulation/opening of ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels → K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarisation → smooth-muscle relaxation and vasodilation. |

## Diseases of high altitude

### Acute mountain sickness (AMS)

- Develops within 24 hours of ascent.
- Symptoms listed: severe headache, alteration of sleep, anorexia, and nausea.

### High-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE)

- Described as a severe form of AMS.
- Symptoms: symptoms of AMS plus ataxia and mental changes.

### High-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE)

- Develops 2–4 days after ascent.
- Symptoms listed: severe cough, chest pain, and pink/foamy sputum.

## Pathophysiology of AMS, HACE, and HAPE

| AMS/HACE | HAPE |
|---|---|
| Oedema → corpus callosum. | Hypoxia → patchy vasoconstriction → increased blood flow to open capillaries → endothelial stress failure → endothelial damage → increased capillary permeability → oedema. |
| Hypoxia → vasodilation of brain vasculature → increased cerebral blood flow (autoregulation failure) → increased capillary hydrostatic pressure → oedema. | The source states that oedema fluid contains increased RBCs, WBCs, and proteins. |

## Treatment of HACE and HAPE

- **Immediate descent:** at least 1,000 m with oxygen therapy or supplementation.
- For AMS and HACE, the source lists:
  - Corticosteroids.
  - Sumatriptan.
  - Gabapentin.
- For HAPE, the source lists:
  - Calcium-channel blockers (nifedipine 20 mg slow release).
  - Phosphodiesterase inhibitors (tadalafil, sildenafil).
  - Theophylline.
  - Aminophylline.
- **Prophylaxis:** acetazolamide 125 mg BD for acclimatization; start 1–2 days before climbing.
