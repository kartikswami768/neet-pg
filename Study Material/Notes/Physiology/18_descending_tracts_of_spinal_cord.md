# 18. Descending Tracts of Spinal Cord

## Layers of the cerebral cortex

1. **Molecular (plexiform) layer:** Superficial layer.
2. **External granular layer.**
3. **External pyramidal layer.**
4. **Internal granular layer.**
5. **Internal pyramidal layer.**
6. **Multiform (fusiform) layer:** Deep layer.

![[images/ch18_p128_cortical_area_map.png]]

### Cortical layers associated with ascending and descending tracts

| Cortical layer/group | Association and functions stated in the source |
|---|---|
| Layer 4 (internal granular layer) | Receives specific ascending somatosensory tracts from the VPL/VPM nuclei of the thalamus; described as the thickest layer. |
| Layers 1, 2, 3, 4 | Receive non-specific projections from basal ganglia, cerebellum, or other brain regions. Basal ganglia and cerebellum project to the thalamus via VA/VL nuclei. |
| Layers 2 and 3 | Make interconnections between the two cerebral hemispheres. |
| Layer 5 (internal pyramidal layer) | Most descending tracts of the brain originate from layer 5. Pyramidal neurons have long axons. The corticospinal tract originates from layer 5 and descends into the spinal cord. |
| Layer 6 (multiform/fusiform layer) | Contains fusiform cells whose axons descend to the thalamus; stated not to be part of the corticospinal tract. |

## Motor cortex

- **Primary motor cortex:** Located in front of the central sulcus; Brodmann area 4.
- **Supplementary motor area and premotor area:** Located in front of the primary motor cortex; Brodmann area 6.

![[images/ch18_p129_motor_cortex_areas.png]]

| Primary motor cortex | Supplementary motor cortex | Premotor cortex |
|---|---|---|

## Descending tracts

| Pyramidal tract | Extrapyramidal tract |
|---|---|
| Descending tracts originate from layer 5 of the cerebral cortex. | Neurons descend from subcortical areas: midbrain, pons, medulla, vestibular nucleus, and basal ganglia. |
| Contains approximately 1 million axons projecting to the spinal cord. | Does not pass through the medullary pyramid. |
| Forms the medullary pyramid at the medulla and descends to the spinal cord. | Influences α-motor neurons via interneurons. |
| Corticospinal tract is also called the pyramidal tract. | |
| Influences α-motor neurons via various interneurons. | |

## Upper and lower motor neurons

| Upper motor neuron (UMN) | Lower motor neuron (LMN) |
|---|---|
| Originates in the cerebral cortex or a subcortical area. | The α-motor neuron is also called the LMN. |
| Combination of pyramidal and extrapyramidal tracts. | Originates from the anterior part of the spinal cord. |
| Influences the α-motor neuron (LMN). | Controls contraction of skeletal muscle. |
| | Called the “final common pathway”. The same pathway is used by UMN input and by reflex pathways at the spinal-cord level. |

### UMN and LMN lesions

**UMN lesion**

- UMN includes both pyramidal and extrapyramidal tracts:
  - Pyramidal tract: stimulates the LMN.
  - Extrapyramidal tract: inhibits the LMN.
- Excitatory and inhibitory inputs are balanced, with slight inhibitory dominance (source wording).
- UMN damage → LMN overexcitation.
- Features listed:
  - Hypertonia (spasticity or rigidity).
  - Deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) increased.
  - No atrophy initially (disuse atrophy may occur in the long term).
  - No fasciculation/fibrillation.
  - Positive Babinski sign.
  - Regional muscle group affected.

**LMN lesion**

- The source attributes maintained muscle tone to asynchronous LMN discharge.
- Features listed:
  - Hypotonia.
  - Loss of deep tendon reflex.
  - Severe skeletal-muscle atrophy.
  - Fasciculation: involuntary contraction of a motor unit.
  - Fibrillation: involuntary contraction of a single muscle fibre.
  - Segmental/distal muscle group affected.

## Spasticity versus rigidity

| Spasticity | Rigidity |
|---|---|
| Caused by lesions in the pyramidal tract. | Seen in extrapyramidal lesions. |
| Weakness present. | Subtypes listed: cogwheel rigidity and lead-pipe rigidity. |
| Affects one muscle group, typically antigravity muscles. | Affects both flexor and extensor muscles with hypertonia throughout movement. |
| More resistance in one direction. | Hypertonia persists through the entire range of motion. |
| Tone is greater in the initial part of movement: “clasp-knife spasticity”. | Velocity-independent; does not vary with movement speed. |
| Velocity-dependent; more noticeable with fast movements. | **Cogwheel rigidity (Parkinson’s):** tremor superimposed on hypertonia, causing intermittent increases in tone during movement. **Lead-pipe rigidity (neuroleptic malignant syndrome):** uniform increase in tone. |

## Pyramidal tract

- The pyramidal tract originates from the primary motor cortex, premotor cortex, supplementary motor cortex, and somatosensory area.
- The source lists approximately 1 million fibres:
  - 30% from the primary motor cortex.
  - 30% from the premotor/supplementary cortex.
  - 40% from the somatosensory cortex.
- Fibres converge at the internal capsule and reach the spinomedullary junction.
- At the spinomedullary junction, **80% of fibres cross** to the opposite side and enter the spinal cord. They descend through the spinal cord and end at cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral regions. Via interneurons, they stimulate α-motor neurons of skeletal muscle. These form the **lateral corticospinal tract**, controlling distal skeletal muscles and fine movements.
- **20% of fibres remain uncrossed** and reach cervical and thoracic regions. They form the **anterior corticospinal tract** in the anterior spinal cord, cross at the spinal level via interneurons, and synapse with α-motor neurons to stimulate proximal skeletal muscle and maintain posture.

## Extrapyramidal tracts

| Tract | Origin and course | Function stated in the source |
|---|---|---|
| **Tectospinal tract** | Originates from the midbrain tectum; crosses and descends to the cervical region. | Maintains posture in response to visual stimulation (e.g. turning the neck away from light). |
| **Rubrospinal tract** | Originates from the red nucleus in the midbrain; crosses and descends to the cervical region. | Stimulates α-motor neurons of flexor muscles, causing contraction of neck flexors. |
| **Pontine reticulospinal tract** | Originates from the pons/part of the reticular nucleus; descends uncrossed to the sacral region; tonically active. | Stimulates γ-motor neurons of extensor muscles. |
| **Medullary reticulospinal tract** | Originates from the medulla/part of the reticular nucleus; crosses and descends to the lumbosacral region. | Inhibits γ-motor neurons of extensor muscles. |
| **Vestibulospinal tract** | Originates from the vestibular nuclei in the pons and medulla; uncrossed fibres descend to the lumbosacral region. | Stimulates α-motor neurons of extensor muscles; tonic inhibitory control from the cerebellum is listed. |

![[images/ch18_p132_extrapyramidal_tracts.png]]

### Functions listed for extrapyramidal tracts

- Main function: maintenance of posture.
- The rubrospinal tract, in addition to maintenance of posture, also controls fine movements of distal muscles.
- Stimulation of γ-extensor → muscle-spindle activation → stimulation of Ia fibre → α-motor-neuron stimulation.

![[images/ch18_p133_decerebration_pathway.png]]

## Decerebration and decortication

### Decerebration

- Transection of the brainstem between the superior and inferior colliculi (mid-collicular), described as a cut above the pons.
- **Lost tracts:** rubrospinal and tectospinal.
- **Intact tracts:** pontine reticulospinal, medullary reticulospinal, and vestibulospinal.
- **Effect:** decerebrate rigidity, also called gamma rigidity.

Sequence described in the source:

Pontine reticulospinal tract (tonically active) → stimulation of γ-motor neurons of extensor muscles → muscle-spindle activation → activation of α-motor neurons of extensor muscles → contraction of all extensor muscles → strong extension of all four limbs.

- Rigidity is lost if the dorsal-root neuron is cut.
- **Decerebellate rigidity:** cutting the anterior lobe of the cerebellum activates the vestibulospinal tract and increases extensor-muscle rigidity.

### Decortication

- Injury to the cerebral cortex (cut above the midbrain).
- Effects listed:
  - Pontine reticulospinal tract causes lower-limb extension.
  - Rubrospinal tract causes upper-limb flexion.

![[images/ch18_p134_decerebrate_decorticate_postures.png]]

## Some important brain areas

- **Prosopagnosia:** Inability to recognize faces. The source attributes it to damage to the medioventral surfaces of the temporal lobes (inferior surface) and the medial undersides of both occipital lobes.
- **Arithmetic calculation:** The inferior portion of the left frontal lobe controls number processing and calculations; a lesion leads to acalculia.
- **Brain area for navigation:**
  - Right hippocampus: location of place.
  - Right caudate nucleus: facilitates movement to that place. The source adds that this area is more developed in men due to larger brain size.

## MCQs

**1.** Which of the following is false in decerebrate rigidity? (NEET 2019)

- a. Upper limb flexed; lower limb extended
- b. Both upper and lower limbs extended
- c. Lesion between the superior and inferior colliculi produces this rigidity
- d. Cutting the dorsal root abolishes the rigidity

**Answer:** (a)

**2.** Features of a pyramidal-tract lesion are all except (AIIMS 1998):

- a. Clasp-knife rigidity
- b. Involuntary movements
- c. Positive Babinski sign
- d. Exaggerated reflexes and increased tone

**Answer:** (b)
