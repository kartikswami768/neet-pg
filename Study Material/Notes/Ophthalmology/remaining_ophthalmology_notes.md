# Conjunctiva, Community Ophthalmology & Remaining Major Ophthalmology Topics

## Scope

These notes cover the major ophthalmology material outside the dedicated sections on basic anatomy, cornea/sclera, neuro-ophthalmology, squint, lens, glaucoma, optics/refraction, retina, and eyelids/orbit. The emphasis is on examination-relevant clinical findings, classifications, diagnostic tests, treatment, complications, public-health definitions, programmes, and high-yield distinctions.

---

## 1. Conjunctiva

### 1.1 Anatomy and basic structure

#### Parts of conjunctiva

| Part | Location |
|---|---|
| **Bulbar conjunctiva** | Lines the sclera |
| **Palpebral conjunctiva** | Lines the inner surface of the eyelids |
| **Forniceal conjunctiva** | Lies between bulbar and palpebral conjunctiva |

#### Histology and tear-film role

- **Goblet cells** are present in the conjunctival epithelium.
- Goblet cells secrete **mucin**, forming the **innermost layer of the tear film**.
- Conjunctival lymphatics are an important distinguishing feature:
  - **Medial conjunctiva → submandibular lymph nodes**
  - **Lateral conjunctiva → preauricular lymph nodes**

#### Clinical surface reactions

- **Papillae** = hypertrophied vascular cores; classically associated with **bacterial** and **allergic** conjunctival reactions.
- **Follicles** = lymphoid aggregates, appearing as whitish/yellowish-grey lesions; classically associated with **viral** and **chlamydial** disease.
- **Chemosis** = oedema of the bulbar conjunctiva.
- **Hyperemia/congestion** = conjunctival redness.

### 1.2 Conjunctivitis: clinical pattern and classification

Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva and is also described as **eye flu**.

#### Typical symptoms

- Redness
- Watering
- Stickiness
- Irritation
- Foreign-body sensation
- Discharge
- Pain may occur in severe disease.

Typical conjunctivitis generally does **not** cause loss of vision. **Loss of vision suggests corneal involvement.**

#### Discharge-based approach

| Discharge / reaction | More suggestive of |
|---|---|
| **Purulent / mucopurulent** | Bacterial; chlamydial may also be mucopurulent |
| **Watery** | Viral; allergic |
| **Papillary reaction** | Bacterial; allergic |
| **Follicular reaction** | Viral; chlamydial |

Morning crusting/sticking of the lids is characteristic of the discharge pattern seen in infectious conjunctivitis; coloured halos are also described in the clinical presentation.

### 1.3 Bacterial conjunctivitis

#### Acute bacterial conjunctivitis

| Type | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Acute mucopurulent** | Most commonly **Staphylococcus aureus** |
| **Acute purulent / hyperacute / blennorrhoea** | **Neisseria gonorrhoeae**; overhanging lids, copious discharge, preauricular lymphadenopathy |
| **Acute membranous** | True membrane; **bleeds on removal** |
| **Acute pseudomembranous** | Pseudomembrane; **does not bleed on removal** |
| **Angular / diplobacillary conjunctivitis** | **Moraxella axenfeld**; affects lateral canthi; tetracycline ointment + zinc/boric-acid drops are described |

#### Membrane distinction

**True membrane** is firmly adherent and removal produces bleeding. A **pseudomembrane** can be removed without bleeding.

### 1.4 Chlamydial conjunctivitis

#### Chlamydial serovars

| Serovar | Disease |
|---|---|
| **A, B, Ba, C** | Trachoma |
| **D–K** | Adult inclusion conjunctivitis / ophthalmia neonatorum |
| **L1, L2, L3** | Lymphogranuloma venereum; no ocular disease described |

#### Trachoma

Trachoma is a chronic chlamydial conjunctival disease with an inflammatory stage followed by a **cicatrising/scarring stage**.

- Also called **Egyptian ophthalmia**.
- Spread is described by **FFF**:
  - Finger
  - Fly
  - Fomite
- It may be clinically subclinical for long periods.
- Characteristic inflammatory findings include **sago-grain follicles** and papillary reaction.

> **Diagram omitted:** Trachoma: follicles and scarring landmarks.
#### Characteristic signs

| Sign | Description |
|---|---|
| **Herbert follicles** | Follicles at the upper limbus |
| **Herbert pits** | Depressions at the limbus left after Herbert follicles disappear |
| **Arlt line** | Line of cicatrisation/scarring in the **sulcus subtarsalis** |
| **Pannus** | Corneal vascularisation associated with trachoma |

#### WHO trachoma staging

1. **Stage I – Follicles:** ≥5 follicles at the upper palpebral conjunctiva.
2. **Stage II – Inflammatory:** itching and watering.
3. **Stage III – Scarring:** Arlt line and Herbert pits.
4. **Stage IV – Trichiasis:** misdirection/in-turning of eyelashes.
5. **Stage V – Corneal opacity:** due to rubbing of lashes against the cornea.

The grading system is also referred to as **FISTO**.

#### Trachoma and blindness

- Scarring produces lid distortion and trichiasis.
- Trichiasis causes repeated corneal irritation.
- The major preventable blinding endpoint is **corneal opacity**.

#### SAFE strategy

| Letter | Component |
|---|---|
| **S** | Surgery for trichiasis/entropion |
| **A** | Antibiotics — **azithromycin**; topical tetracycline 1% is also described |
| **F** | Facial cleanliness/hygiene |
| **E** | Environmental cleanliness/change |

- **Azithromycin 1 g single dose** is the drug of choice.
- A public-health threshold described is **>10% trachoma follicle prevalence in children aged 1–9 years**, at which azithromycin is used as the control-of-trachoma intervention.
- India: trachoma elimination is recorded as **8 October 2024**.

##### Sequelae of trachomatous scarring

- **Trichiasis** — in-turning/misdirection of eyelashes.
- **Entropion** — in-turning of the lid itself.
- **Tylosis** — thickened lid margin.
- **Symblepharon** — adhesion between palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva.
- **Dry eye** — due to conjunctival scarring.
- Corneal opacity is the major sight-threatening complication.

### 1.5 Ophthalmia neonatorum

Any discharge or watering from the eye during the first **4 weeks of life** is treated as pathological/infective.

#### Cause according to onset

| Onset after birth | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Within first 6 h** | Chemical conjunctivitis, classically silver nitrate exposure |
| **24–48 h** | **Neisseria gonorrhoeae**; most severe |
| **2–5 days** | Other bacteria; Gram-positive organisms are emphasised |
| **5–7 days** | **HSV-2** |
| **>1 week** | **Chlamydia trachomatis (D–K)**; most common |

Other organisms described include **Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Pseudomonas, and Klebsiella**.

#### Prevention

- Historical **Crede method** used **silver nitrate** and is described as obsolete.
- **Erythromycin eye ointment** in the neonate's eye is given as prevention; the timing described is within the first **2 hours**.

#### Treatment

- Antibiotic eye treatment
- Repeated cleaning of the eyes

### 1.6 Viral conjunctivitis

#### Adenoviral disease

- **Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (EKC)**
- **Pharyngoconjunctival fever (PCF)**

#### Acute haemorrhagic / Apollo conjunctivitis

Mnemonic **PACE**:

- **P**icorna virus
- **A**denovirus type 11
- **C**oxsackie A24
- **E**nterovirus type 70

**HSV conjunctivitis** and **molluscum contagiosum conjunctivitis** are also recognised viral conjunctival infections.

#### 1.7 Allergic conjunctivitis

##### Types

- Seasonal allergic conjunctivitis
- **Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) / spring catarrh**
- **Atopic keratoconjunctivitis** — described as VKC-like disease in adults
- **Giant papillary conjunctivitis** — mechanical irritation due to contact lenses, protruding sutures, or an ill-fitting prosthetic eye
- **Phlyctenular conjunctivitis**

##### Vernal keratoconjunctivitis

- Most common in **boys aged 5–15 years**; another description gives the typical age around **10 years**.
- Hypersensitivity: **Type I > Type II**.
- Often associated with exogenous allergens such as **dust and pollen**.
- Seasonal/summer predominance is described; “spring catarrh” is a traditional but imperfect name.
- Symptoms: intense itching, irritation, redness, watering.

##### Signs

| Sign | Key finding |
|---|---|
| **Giant papillae** | Cobblestone appearance on upper tarsal conjunctiva |
| **Horner–Trantas dots** | Eosinophilic deposits at the limbus |
| **Maxwell–Lyon sign** | Thick, ropey/stringy discharge |
| **Pseudogerontoxon / Cupid's bow** | White/gelatinous limbal appearance, especially superiorly; may resemble arcus |
| **Shield ulcer** | Sterile, shield-shaped corneal ulcer; not an infectious ulcer |

> **Diagram omitted:** Vernal keratoconjunctivitis: cobblestone papillae and limbal Horner–Trantas dots.
##### Treatment

- Antihistamines
- Mast-cell stabilisers for prevention/control
- **Olopatadine** is the drug of choice because of combined antihistamine + mast-cell stabilising action.
- Topical corticosteroids are used for acute exacerbations/severe disease.
- Low-dose steroids described: **loteprednol** and **fluorometholone**.
- Immunomodulators are used in refractory disease.

##### Phlyctenular keratoconjunctivitis

- Associated with hypersensitivity to endogenous antigens, particularly **Staphylococcus aureus** and **tubercular antigen**.
- **Type IV hypersensitivity**.
- Itching and watering.
- Corneal involvement may produce **fascicular ulcer**, which may progress into a ring ulcer.
- Treatment: **steroids**.

#### 1.8 Subconjunctival haemorrhage and other acute surface findings

##### Subconjunctival haemorrhage

Subconjunctival haemorrhage is associated with:

- Trauma
- Hypertension
- Bleeding diathesis
- Pertussis/violent coughing

Haemorrhagic conjunctivitis may also accompany bacterial or viral conjunctival infection.

##### Chemosis

**Chemosis = oedema of the bulbar conjunctiva.**

#### 1.9 Conjunctival and limbal degenerations / masses

##### Pinguecula

- **Elastotic degeneration**.
- Located **away from the limbus**.
- Yellowish deposit.
- Usually stationary and asymptomatic.
- Treatment: observation; removal is essentially cosmetic.

##### Pterygium

- **Elastotic degeneration** of conjunctival tissue.
- Triangular/wing-shaped fibrovascular growth of conjunctiva extending **across the limbus onto the cornea**.
- Predisposing factors described:
  - Ultraviolet exposure, especially **UV-B**
  - Dust
  - Heat
  - Limbal stem-cell deficiency
- Nasal predominance is described.
- Can obstruct the visual axis and can cause visual loss.
- Can cause **with-the-rule astigmatism** from corneal flattening/tensional pull.

> **Diagram omitted:** Pterygium: triangular conjunctival growth crossing the limbus.
##### Pterygium versus pseudopterygium

**Glass rod test:**
- Glass rod does **not** pass beneath a true pterygium.
- In **pseudopterygium**, the glass rod can pass underneath.

##### Treatment of pterygium

- Excision alone has a high recurrence rate.
- Recurrence prevention:
  - **Mitomycin-C**
  - **Conjunctival autografting**; described as the treatment of choice.
- Mitomycin-C has the disadvantage of possible **scleral necrosis**.
- **PERFECT surgery**: Pterygium Extended Resection followed by Extended Conjunctiva Transplantation.
- The extended graft is harvested from the same eye, described from the superior-temporal region.

##### Bitot's spots and xerophthalmia

Bitot's spots are associated with **vitamin A deficiency**.

- Usually involve the **temporal conjunctiva**.
- Characteristic **foamy** appearance.

> **Diagram omitted:** Bitot's spot and xerophthalmia.
##### Limbal dermoid

- Benign congenital lesion.
- Located at the limbus.
- Contains tissues representing **all three germ layers**; hair and other adnexal structures may occur.
- Excision when visually significant.

###### Dermolipoma / lipodermoid

- Located away from the limbus, classically in the **upper outer quadrant**.
- Fat-like subconjunctival mass.
- Usually a cosmetic concern.

#### 2. Dry Eye and Ocular Surface Disease

##### 2.1 Tear film

The tear film is described in three functional layers:

1. **Mucin layer** — innermost; produced mainly by conjunctival **goblet cells**.
2. **Aqueous layer** — produced by the **lacrimal and accessory lacrimal glands**.
3. **Lipid layer** — produced by the **Meibomian glands**; reduces evaporation of tears.

##### 2.2 Tests for dry eye

###### Schirmer test

- Uses **Whatman Paper 41**.
- Tear strip is placed in the lower lid for **5 minutes**.
- Reading described as:
  - **≥15 mm** — normal
  - **<5 mm** — severe dry eye

> **Diagram omitted:** Schirmer test.
###### Phenol red thread test

- Faster test.
- Thread is coated with phenol and placed in the eye for **15 seconds**.
- Colour changes because of change in pH.
- **<9 mm** indicates severe dry eye.

###### Tear-film break-up time (TBUT)

- After the last blink, the patient stares and the time until the first dry spot appears is measured.
- **<10 seconds** indicates dry eye.

###### Ocular-surface staining

- **Rose Bengal** stains devitalised/necrotic tissue.
- **Lissamine green** similarly stains ocular surface disease and is described as safer/less toxic than Rose Bengal.
- Lissamine green stains both **cornea and conjunctiva**.
- Tear osmolarity is described as an important biomarker for detecting dry eye.

##### 2.3 Treatment

- Lubricating eye drops, including **carboxymethylcellulose**.
- **Hyaluronic-acid derivatives**.
- When posterior blepharitis/meibomianitis contributes to an evaporative component, thermal pulsation can soften retained meibomian secretions and improve lipid delivery to the tear film.

###### Meibomian gland dysfunction and ocular surface disease

- Posterior blepharitis/meibomianitis is associated with **acne rosacea**.
- Trapped meibomian oil reduces delivery of lipid to the tear film and can contribute to dry eye.

# 3. Lacrimal Apparatus and Lacrimal Drainage Disorders

## 3.1 Tear-production versus tear-drainage pathway

**Lacrimal gland → ocular surface/tear menisci → upper and lower puncta → canaliculi → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → inferior meatus of nose.**

- The **lacrimal gland** produces tears and lies superolaterally.
- The **lacrimal sac** collects tears and lies inferomedially.
- Upper and lower puncta are aligned with the **upper punctum medial to the lower punctum**.
- Lacrimal canaliculus measurements:
  - Vertical part: **2 mm**
  - Horizontal part: **8 mm**
  - Total: **10 mm**

> **Diagram omitted:** Lacrimal drainage anatomy.
## 3.2 Watering and epiphora

Two broad forms are described:

1. **Lacrimation** = hypersecretion.
2. **Overflow epiphora** = tears overflow because drainage is inadequate.

A **high tear meniscus** on slit-lamp examination suggests overflow with a drainage problem.

## 3.3 Examination and investigations

### Regurgitation test

- Press over the medial canthus/lacrimal sac.
- Regurgitation suggests **lacrimal drainage obstruction**.

### Syringing

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Hard stop** | Lower lacrimal passage is patent to the level reached by the probe; a lower-passage obstruction is not suggested |
| **Soft stop** | Suggests a problem in the upper lacrimal passage |

With a hard stop, fluid can be injected:
- If the system is patent, fluid reaches the mouth and may be tasted.
- Epiphora with fluid reaching the mouth can still reflect punctal stenosis/partial obstruction or lacrimal pump failure.

### Dacryoscintigraphy

- Simple, non-invasive, physiological assessment of the nasolacrimal system.
- Uses **radioactive labelling of tears** and follows the tracer with a **gamma counter**.
- Described as the most reliable investigation for **lacrimal pump failure**.

> **Diagram omitted:** Dacryoscintigraphy and lacrimal drainage obstruction.
### Jones dye test

- Fluorescein is instilled in the eye and cotton is placed in the nose.
- Recovery of fluorescein at the nasal cotton indicates a positive/normal passage.
- If the primary dye test is negative, fluid is injected through the upper passage.
- Filling of the sac with fluorescein followed by recovery of fluorescein after injection supports a normal upper lacrimal passage.
- Failure to recover dye suggests upper-passage or lacrimal-pump dysfunction.
- Jones tests 1 and 2 assess lacrimal drainage, while **dacryoscintigraphy** is the most reliable test for lacrimal pump failure.

## 3.4 Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction / congenital dacryocystitis

- Early watering/discharge in a neonate must be distinguished from ophthalmia neonatorum.
- Watering during the **first month of life** is treated as pathological in the source framework and is not automatically attributed to uncomplicated nasolacrimal obstruction.

### Management sequence

| Age/stage | Management |
|---|---|
| **<9 months** | Crigler/hydrostatic massage; best initial approach |
| **After 9 months** | Probing |
| **Around 18 months** | Probing is described as particularly appropriate/best for probing success |
| **If persistent, especially after 4 years** | Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) |

### DCR

- Creates a direct communication between the **lacrimal sac and nasal cavity**.
- The source describes the opening in the **medial meatus**.
- Probing is directed downward, more laterally, and backward, in the direction of the **ala of the nose**.

## 3.5 Dacryocystitis

**Dacryocystitis = inflammation of the lacrimal sac.**

### Acute dacryocystitis

- Painful, red, swollen area over the lacrimal sac.
- May ooze pus.
- Most common organism described: **Staphylococcus aureus**.
- Complication: **lacrimal fistula**.
- Treatment of a lacrimal fistula: **DCR**.

### Chronic dacryocystitis / persistent nasolacrimal blockage

- Repeated syringing may be used.
- **Dacryocystorhinostomy** is the definitive drainage procedure described.
- A lacrimal sac tumour or markedly atrophic sac is treated by **dacryocystectomy**.

# 4. Uvea and Uveitis

## 4.1 Anatomical classification of uveitis

| Type | Main structures | Other terminology |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior uveitis** | Iris + pars plicata | Iritis / iridocyclitis |
| **Intermediate uveitis** | Pars plana | Pars planitis |
| **Posterior uveitis** | Choroid + retina | Choroiditis, chorioretinitis, retinochoroiditis |
| **Panuveitis** | All uveal segments | — |

## 4.2 Anterior uveitis

### Symptoms

- Pain
- Redness
- Lacrimation
- Photophobia
- Blurring/loss of vision

> **Diagram omitted:** Anterior uveitis: keratic precipitates, iris nodules and posterior synechiae.
### Signs

#### Keratic precipitates (KPs)

Aggregates of inflammatory cells and proteins deposited on the corneal endothelium.

- **Mutton-fat KPs** — larger deposits, classically inferiorly in **Arlt's triangle**, associated with granulomatous uveitis.
- **Fine KPs** — described with non-granulomatous uveitis, especially HLA-B27-related disease.
- **Stellate KPs** — characteristic of **Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis** and also described with HSV uveitis.

#### Aqueous cells and flare

- **Aqueous cells** are a marker of inflammatory activity.
- **Aqueous flare** is described as the earliest sign of anterior uveitis and represents the **Tyndall effect/Brownian movement** of protein in the anterior chamber.

#### Iris nodules

| Nodule | Location |
|---|---|
| **Koeppe** | Pupillary margin |
| **Busacca** | Iris stromal surface |
| **Berlin** | Angle/peripheral iris |

#### Posterior synechiae

- Adhesion between the **pupillary margin and the lens**.
- Produces an **irregular/festooned pupil** on dilatation.
- Severe uveitis may produce a membrane over the pupil (**occlusio pupillae**).
- Synechiae can produce **secondary angle-closure glaucoma**.

### Treatment

- **Topical corticosteroids**, with tapering.
- **Atropine** as mydriatic and cycloplegic.

### Complications

1. Band-shaped keratopathy
2. Complicated cataract
3. Glaucoma
   - Secondary angle closure from posterior synechiae
   - Secondary open-angle glaucoma from trabeculitis
   - Latanoprost is described as contraindicated in active uveitic secondary open-angle glaucoma
   - Alpha agonists/dipivefrine are listed as preferred pressure-lowering agents in the source
4. Phthisis bulbi

---

## 4.3 Systemic associations of anterior uveitis

### Ankylosing spondylitis

- **HLA-B27 positive**.
- Sacroiliitis and “bamboo spine”/dagger sign.
- Young adult males, early morning stiffness.
- Characteristic eye disease: **acute recurrent non-granulomatous anterior uveitis**.

### Juvenile rheumatoid arthritis / juvenile chronic arthritis

- Age **<15 years**.
- More common in females.
- **Pauciarticular disease (<5 joints)** carries the highest risk of uveitis.
- Uveitis: **painless, chronic, bilateral, non-granulomatous anterior uveitis**.
- Complications: glaucoma, complicated cataract, band-shaped keratopathy.
- Cataract surgery with IOL is described as contraindicated because of severe postoperative inflammation in active disease.

### Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis

| Feature | Finding |
|---|---|
| Laterality | Usually unilateral in the described classic presentation |
| Iris | Affected eye becomes lighter; heterochromia |
| KPs | Small, stellate, pan-corneal |
| Synechiae | **Absent** — highlighted as characteristic |
| Amsler sign | Bleeding in the anterior chamber from pressure change |
| Cataract | Posterior subcapsular/complicated cataract |
| Association | **Rubella** |
| Steroids | Often ineffective because the uveitis is mild |

### Posner–Schlossman syndrome (glaucomatocyclitic crisis)

- Recurrent attacks of **markedly elevated IOP** with mild anterior uveitis.
- **Open angle** on gonioscopy.
- Prostaglandin analogues and **pilocarpine** are described as contraindicated because they can worsen inflammation.
- **Beta-blocker** is listed as the drug of choice for IOP control.

---

## 4.4 Intermediate uveitis

- Inflammation centred on the **pars plana**.
- Cells in the vitreous can appear as white **snowballs**.
- Inflammatory deposits on the pars plana produce **snowbanking**.
- The most common cause of visual loss described is **cystoid macular oedema**.
- Treatment: **periocular steroids**, including parabulbar/sub-Tenon steroid therapy.
- Further escalation in refractory disease includes systemic therapy/immunomodulation and pars plana vitrectomy in the described treatment pathway.

---

## 4.5 Posterior and panuveitis

### Toxoplasma chorioretinitis

- Described as a common cause of posterior uveitis.
- Typical active lesion: **“headlight in fog”** appearance — solitary posterior-pole lesion with hazy vitritis.
- Healed/scarred lesion: **punched-out scar**.
- Clindamycin is described under steroid cover; steroids are used because parasite destruction can release antigen and trigger severe inflammation.

> **Diagram omitted:** Toxoplasma retinochoroiditis: active lesion and healed scar.
### Sarcoidosis

- Can produce panuveitis with conjunctival, anterior-uveal and vitreous involvement.
- Classic fundus appearance: **candle-wax drippings**, reflecting periphlebitis along retinal veins.

### Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease

Characteristic manifestations include:

- **Panuveitis**
- **Bullous/exudative retinal detachment**
- **Sugiura sign**
- **Sunset-glow fundus**
- Poliosis (white eyelashes)
- Madarosis (loss of eyelashes)
- Vitiligo
- Encephalitis/meningitis
- Tinnitus and vertigo

Treatment: **steroids**.

### Sympathetic ophthalmia

- Classically follows **penetrating ocular trauma**.
- Bilateral **granulomatous panuveitis**.
- Mechanism described: exposure of sequestered uveal pigment/antigen leading to immune-mediated involvement of the fellow eye.
- First symptom: **photophobia**.
- First sign: **retrolental flare**.
- Ciliary-body involvement can cause **paralysis of accommodation**.
- **Dalen–Fuchs nodules** are nodules at the retinal pigment epithelium and are also seen in VKH and sarcoidosis.
- Treatment: massive corticosteroid therapy, including topical/subconjunctival/systemic treatment.
- If the injured eye is unsalvageable, the source describes **enucleation within 2 weeks / 14 days** of injury.

> **Diagram omitted:** Panuveitis patterns: VKH and sympathetic ophthalmia.
### Behçet disease

- Recurrent **hypopyon** may occur; this is also termed a transient hypopyon syndrome in the visual review.
- Association with **HLA-B5/HLA-B51**.
- Oral ulcers and genital ulcers.
- Positive skin testing is described.
- Eye disease includes **non-granulomatous anterior and posterior uveitis**.
- Obliterative vasculitis related to circulating immune complexes is described.

### Syphilis-related ocular findings

- **Iris roseola**: dilated capillaries forming iris nodules.
- Can produce anterior, posterior, granulomatous or non-granulomatous uveitis.
- A **salt-and-pepper fundus** is described with posterior uveitis and is also listed among the associations of rubella, syphilis, retinitis pigmentosa sine pigmento, and myotonic dystrophy.

# 5. Ocular Trauma and Ocular Emergencies

## 5.1 Corneal and superficial foreign body

- The most common foreign body is described as **iron**.
- Removal: **26-gauge needle** on the slit lamp after topical **proparacaine** anaesthesia.

## 5.2 Blunt ocular trauma: high-yield findings

| Finding | Characteristic feature |
|---|---|
| **Iridodialysis** | Iris root tears from its attachment → **D-shaped pupil** |
| **Hyphema** | Blood in the anterior chamber; complete hyphema is described as an **8-ball hyphema** |
| **Vossius ring** | Iris pigment deposited on the anterior lens capsule |
| **Rosette cataract** | Flower-shaped traumatic cataract |
| **Commotio retinae / Berlin oedema** | Pale retinal appearance after blunt trauma |
| **Retinal dialysis** | Retinal detachment/tear at the retinal base |
| **Zonular dialysis** | Zonules break → lens subluxation |
| **Vitreous haemorrhage** | Described as a common vitreous complication in young adults |
| **Traumatic optic neuropathy** | Can lead to primary optic atrophy |
| **Angle-recession glaucoma** | Secondary open-angle glaucoma after angle damage |

> **Diagram omitted:** Blunt ocular trauma: hyphema, ciliary-body tear and angle recession.
## 5.3 Chemical ocular injury

### Acid versus alkali

| Feature | Acid | Alkali |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | **Coagulation** / “freezing” of enzymes | **Saponification** with deeper penetration |
| Relative danger | Less dangerous | **More dangerous** |

### First response

- **Immediate copious irrigation** with any available solution.
- Minimum duration described: **15–30 minutes**.
- Remove contaminated material and continue irrigation until the surface is adequately cleansed.

### Treatment and prognosis

- **Steroids** are the drug of choice for treatment.
- Prognosis depends importantly on:
  - **Corneal clarity**
  - **Limbal ischaemia**
- A **white/ischaemic limbus** indicates poor prognosis.

## 5.4 Intraocular foreign bodies and metallic toxicity

### Siderosis bulbi

- Due to **iron**.
- Toxic degeneration of ocular tissues.
- Associations/findings described:
  - Heterochromia
  - Siderotic cataract
  - Dust-coloured cornea

### Chalcosis bulbi

- Due to **copper**.
- Copper deposition in Descemet's membrane produces a **Kayser–Fleischer ring**.
- **Sunflower cataract** is associated.

### Management

- **Removal of the intraocular foreign body** is essential.
- **MRI is contraindicated** when a metallic intraocular foreign body is suspected.

## 5.5 Orbital blow-out fracture as a traumatic emergency

- A large object striking the orbital rim, such as a tennis ball or fist, can increase intraorbital pressure and fracture the **orbital floor**.
- Orbital contents may herniate into the maxillary sinus.
- Imaging finding: **tear-drop sign**.
- Infraorbital nerve involvement may cause **loss of cheek sensation**.

## 6. Pediatric and Congenital Ophthalmology Beyond the Dedicated Sections

### 6.1 Ocular development and coloboma

The embryological sequence is:

- The optic structures develop from the **forebrain**.
- **PAX6** is identified as an important developmental gene.
- Development begins around the **22nd day of gestation**.
- Optic vesicle contacts the surface ectoderm to form the early **lens placode**.
- The lens placode invaginates to form the **lens pit**, then the lens vesicle and finally the lens.
- The **choroid fissure** closes around the **6th–7th week of gestation**.
- Failure of closure produces **coloboma**.

#### Tissue derivation mnemonics

**Surface ectoderm — SLEEK**

- Skin appendages
- Lens
- Epithelium of conjunctiva
- Epithelium of cornea

**Neuroectoderm — STORME**

- Secondary vitreous
- Tertiary vitreous
- Optic nerve
- Retina
- Muscles involved in pupillary function — sphincter and dilator pupillae
- Epithelium of iris and ciliary body

**Neural crest**

- Sclera except temporal part
- Choroid
- Corneal stroma and corneal endothelium
- Trabecular meshwork
- Ciliary muscles

**Mesoderm — PSME**

- Primary vitreous
- Temporal sclera
- Extraocular muscles
- Endothelial lining of blood vessels

### 6.2 Retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma is the **most common primary intraocular tumour of childhood**.

#### Presentation

- Typical age of presentation: approximately **18 months**.
- Bilateral disease presents earlier.
- Most cases present by **5–6 years**.
- **Most common presentation: leukocoria** (white-pupillary reflex / amaurotic cat's eye).
- **Second most common presentation: strabismus**.
- Other presentations: glaucoma, rubeosis iridis and proptosis.

> **Diagram omitted:** Retinoblastoma: leukocoria and characteristic histopathology.
#### Spread and morphology

- Most common route of spread: **optic nerve**.
- Other routes include subarachnoid spread, haematogenous spread and anterior spread.
- Growth may be **mixed**, with both endophytic vitreous growth and exophytic scleral/subretinal growth.
- Diffuse infiltrating growth has a worse prognosis.

#### Histopathology

| Structure | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes** | True lumen; better differentiation |
| **Homer–Wright rosettes** | No true lumen |
| **Fleurettes** | Flower-like differentiation |
| Sheets of immature cells without rosettes | Poor differentiation |

#### Genetics and syndromic association

- **13q14** abnormality is described as the classic retinoblastoma gene region.
- Mutation outside the 14th band is associated with dysmorphic features and a **13q syndrome**.
- **Trilateral retinoblastoma** = bilateral retinoblastoma with a **pinealoma**.

#### International grouping: Group A–E

- **Group A:** small tumour (<3 mm) and away from important structures such as the macula and optic disc.
- **Group B:** other tumours larger than 3 mm or closer to optic disc/fovea but confined to the retina.
- **Group C:** well-defined tumour with limited subretinal or vitreous seeding.
- **Group D:** large/poorly defined tumour with widespread vitreous or subretinal seeding; retina may be detached.
- **Group E:** very large tumour, anterior extension, bleeding, glaucoma or other features associated with poor prognosis.

#### Diagnosis and management

- Clinical examination plus **ultrasound** and, where described, CT to detect **intralesional calcification**.
- **Biopsy is contraindicated** because of tumour seeding along the tract.
- Management depends on size and location:
  - Large tumours: **enucleation**
  - Smaller tumours: cryotherapy, thermotherapy, radiation, chemotherapy
- Chemotherapy combination described: **vincristine + etoposide + carboplatin (VEC regimen)**.
- Most common secondary non-ocular malignancy described: **osteosarcoma**.

# 7. Community Ophthalmology

## 7.1 Definitions of visual loss and blindness

### Severity-based definitions

| Category | Vision in better eye |
|---|---|
| **Low vision** | <6/18 |
| **Economic blindness** | <6/60 |
| **Social blindness** | <3/60 |
| **Manifest blindness** | <1/60 |
| **Absolute blindness** | No perception of light |

**Social blindness (<3/60)** corresponds to the WHO level used in older definitions.

### Programme definition of blindness

The National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment describes blindness as:

- **Visual acuity <3/60 in the better eye**, **or**
- **Visual field <10°**.

The WHO wording differs in whether **best-corrected visual acuity** or **best-available/presented visual acuity** is used for the better eye.

---

## 7.2 Magnitude and epidemiology

- National blindness prevalence is given as **0.36%**; an older figure of **1%** is also reported.
- The **National Blindness & Visual Impairment Survey (2015–2019)** is cited in the programme section.
- A programme goal is given as reduction of avoidable blindness prevalence to **<0.25% by 2025**.

### Age-patterned causes highlighted

| Age group / population | Blindness / visual impairment emphasis |
|---|---|
| **≥50 years** | Cataract is listed as the commonest cause of blindness; corneal opacity is another major cause. |
| **0–49 years** | Corneal opacity is listed as a major cause of blindness; amblyopia and refractive error are highlighted for visual impairment; cataract is also listed. |

The review also lists:

- **Cataract** as the commonest cause of blindness in India and, in the cited framework, worldwide.
- In developed countries, **glaucoma followed by age-related macular degeneration (ARMD)** is listed.
- **Refractive errors** are highlighted as the second commonest cause of blindness in one community-ophthalmology framework and as the **most common cause of ocular morbidity**.
- **Glaucoma** is listed as the commonest cause of **preventable blindness in India** in the same framework.
- **Vitamin A deficiency** is highlighted as a major cause of childhood blindness.

---

## 7.3 National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment (NPCBVI)

- First adopted in India in **1976**.
- In **2017**, the programme became **National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment (NPCBVI)** with visual impairment explicitly incorporated.
- The programme definition uses the **<3/60 / <10° visual-field framework**.
- Major emphasis: reduction of avoidable blindness through cataract services, refractive-error management, school eye health, management of childhood causes, and other priority eye diseases.

### Vision 2020 — The Right to Sight

- Launched by WHO in **1999** with non-governmental-organisation partnerships including **Lions Club, Sight Savers and Orbis International**.

Target diseases highlighted:

- **Trachoma**
- **Onchocerciasis** — the India-focused framework described replacing this with **glaucoma + diabetic retinopathy**
- **Refractive errors**
- **Cataract**
- **Childhood eye diseases**

### 7.7 Vitamin A deficiency and xerophthalmia

Vitamin A deficiency is a major preventable cause of childhood blindness.

#### WHO-style xerophthalmia stages

| Stage | Finding |
|---|---|
| **XN** | **Night blindness** — first symptom |
| **X1A** | **Conjunctival xerosis** — first sign |
| **X1B** | **Bitot spot** — foamy conjunctival lesion, usually temporal |
| **X2** | Corneal xerosis |
| **X3A** | Keratomalacia involving **<1/3** of corneal surface |
| **X3B** | Keratomalacia involving **>1/3** of corneal surface |
| **XS** | Corneal scar |
| **XF** | Xerophthalmic fundus; RPE/fundus changes, including **Uyemura spots / white-spotted fundus** |

> **Diagram omitted:** Xerophthalmia spectrum: Bitot spot and corneal involvement.
#### High-yield sequence

**Night blindness → conjunctival xerosis → Bitot spot → corneal xerosis → keratomalacia → scar/fundus changes.**

The vitamin-A treatment section describes injectable vitamin A, with **1 lakh IU on days 0, 1 and 14** in children above 1 year and a reduced/half dose in the younger age group.

---

### 7.8 Cataract and refractive-error control as public-health priorities

#### Cataract control

- Cataract is repeatedly identified as a leading cause of avoidable blindness.
- Within the eye-care hierarchy, operative services begin at the **secondary/service-centre level**.
- NPCBVI includes cataract control as a core blindness-prevention activity.

#### Refractive-error control

- Refractive error is a major cause of childhood and population visual impairment.
- School screening is a principal route to identification.
- Primary/vision centres provide **refraction** and school eye-care activities.

---

### 7.9 Diabetic retinopathy screening as a community service

The screening framework is:

- **Type 1 diabetes:** first retinal screening after approximately **5 years**.
- **Type 2 diabetes:** retinal screening **immediately at diagnosis**.

The clinical classification and treatment of diabetic retinopathy belong to retinal disease management; here the key community-ophthalmology point is the **timing of screening**.

### 9. Integrated Exam Pearls

- **Loss of vision in a patient labelled as conjunctivitis → think corneal involvement.**
- **Follicles → viral/chlamydial; papillae → bacterial/allergic.**
- **True membrane bleeds on removal; pseudomembrane does not.**
- **Trachoma: Herbert pits + Arlt line + pannus + trichiasis.**
- **SAFE = Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, Environmental hygiene.**
- **VKC: cobblestone papillae + Horner–Trantas dots + ropy discharge + shield ulcer.**
- **Pterygium crosses the limbus; pinguecula does not.**
- **Pterygium can cause with-the-rule astigmatism and visual-axis obstruction.**
- **Glass rod cannot pass under a true pterygium; it can pass under pseudopterygium.**
- **First symptom of xerophthalmia = night blindness; first sign = conjunctival xerosis.**
- **Schirmer ≥15 mm is normal in the described test; <5 mm is severe dry eye.**
- **TBUT <10 s indicates dry eye.**
- **Upper punctum lies medial to lower punctum.**
- **Dacryoscintigraphy is the key physiological test highlighted for lacrimal pump failure.**
- **Anterior uveitis: cells indicate inflammatory activity; flare is described as an early sign.**
- **Koeppe = pupillary margin; Busacca = iris surface; posterior synechiae = festooned pupil.**
- **Mutton-fat KPs → granulomatous uveitis. Stellate KPs → Fuchs.**
- **Intermediate uveitis → snowballs/snowbanking; CME is the major cause of visual loss.**
- **Toxoplasma → headlight in fog; healed lesion → punched-out scar.**
- **VKH → panuveitis + exudative RD + skin/neurologic findings.**
- **Sympathetic ophthalmia → bilateral granulomatous panuveitis after penetrating trauma.**
- **Iridodialysis → D-shaped pupil; hyphema → blood in AC; rosette cataract → blunt trauma.**
- **Alkali injury is more penetrating/dangerous than acid; irrigate first.**
- **Metallic intraocular foreign body → MRI contraindicated.**
- **Retinoblastoma → leukocoria first, strabismus second; optic nerve is the major route of spread; biopsy is contraindicated.**
- **NPCBVI community blindness definition: <3/60 in the better eye or visual field <10°.**
- **NPCBVI: initiated 1976; expanded to include visual impairment in 2017.**
- **Vision-centre level provides refraction and school eye care; surgery begins at the secondary/service level.**
- **Type 1 diabetes: DR screening after 5 years in the cited framework; Type 2: immediately at diagnosis.**

#### 10. Compact Revision Flowcharts

##### Conjunctival red eye

**Red eye → discharge/reaction**

→ **Watery** → viral / allergic

→ **Mucopurulent** → bacterial / chlamydial

→ **Follicles** → viral / chlamydial

→ **Papillae** → bacterial / allergic

→ **Pain or reduced vision** → assess carefully for corneal/other intraocular involvement

##### Trachoma progression

**Chlamydial infection → inflammation/follicles → scarring → Arlt line + Herbert pits → trichiasis/entropion → repeated corneal injury → corneal opacity → blindness**

##### Xerophthalmia progression

**Vitamin A deficiency → night blindness (XN) → conjunctival xerosis (X1A) → Bitot spot (X1B) → corneal xerosis (X2) → keratomalacia (X3A/X3B) → scar (XS) / xerophthalmic fundus (XF)**

##### Epiphora approach

**Watering → decide hypersecretion vs overflow**

→ high meniscus / suspected drainage problem

→ regurgitation test

→ syringing (hard stop vs soft stop)

→ Jones dye test

→ **dacryoscintigraphy when lacrimal pump failure is suspected**

##### Acute anterior uveitis

**Pain + photophobia + redness + blurred vision → slit-lamp examination → KPs + cells/flare + synechiae/nodules → topical steroid + cycloplegic → monitor for cataract, glaucoma and band keratopathy**

#### 12. Final Rapid Review

##### Conjunctiva

**Bulbar + palpebral + fornix → goblet-cell mucin → medial submandibular / lateral preauricular drainage → papillae vs follicles → bacterial/chlamydial/viral/allergic patterns → trachoma → VKC → pterygium/pinguecula → xerophthalmia.**

##### Lacrimal

**Gland → puncta → canaliculi → sac → NLD → inferior meatus → epiphora work-up with regurgitation, syringing, Jones dye and dacryoscintigraphy → massage/probing/DCR for obstruction.**

##### Uveitis

**Anterior = iris/pars plicata; intermediate = pars plana; posterior = choroid/retina; panuveitis = all.**

##### Trauma

**Iridodialysis → D-shaped pupil; hyphema → 8-ball; Vossius ring → iris pigment on lens; rosette cataract → blunt trauma; chemical burn → irrigate immediately; metallic IOFB → no MRI.**

##### Community ophthalmology

**Blindness <3/60 or field <10° → NPCBVI → cataract/refractive error/childhood disease/trachoma control → primary refraction/school screening → secondary surgery → tertiary training/complex care → Vision 2020 + RBSK + vitamin-A blindness prevention.**
