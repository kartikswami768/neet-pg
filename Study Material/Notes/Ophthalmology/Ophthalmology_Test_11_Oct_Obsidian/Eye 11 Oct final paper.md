## Question 18

Mrs. Rajni, a 50-year-old woman with a 10-day history of nasal discharge, headache that increases on bending down, and pain over the medial canthus, now presents to the outpatient department with high-grade fever and chills, headache, and bilateral proptosis. On further inquiry, it is brought to light that the proptosis began in the right eye, and since yesterday, the left eye has also been affected. On examination, her pulse rate is 56 beats per minute, BP is 150/96 mm of Hg, and the temperature is 102F. There is periorbital edema, proptosis, and ophthalmoplegia. The pupillary response is sluggish. Fundoscopy reveals papilloedema. Which of the following is the most probable diagnosis?

- **A.** Retinoblastoma
- **B.** Orbital Pseudotumor
- **C.** Orbital Cellulitis
- **D.** Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis
### Discussion
**Correct answer: D. Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis (CST)**

- **Preceding sinusitis:** Common source of septic CST.
- **Proptosis progressing from one eye to the other:** Characteristic of spread through intercavernous venous connections.
- **Bilateral ophthalmoplegia:** Involvement of CN III, IV, and VI within the cavernous sinuses.
- **Papilledema + headache + bradycardia (56/min) + hypertension:** Suggests raised intracranial pressure.
- **High-grade fever and chills:** Suggest septic thrombosis.

==**Exam pearl:** Orbital cellulitis can cause unilateral proptosis and ophthalmoplegia, but rapidly developing bilateral orbital signs after sinusitis strongly suggest CST.==

**Clinical significance:** This is a life-threatening emergency requiring urgent hospital admission, IV antibiotics, and specialist assessment.

## Question 30

A 65-year-old male comes to the emergency department because of a sudden, painless loss of vision in his right eye that occurred a half an hour ago. Five hours ago, he experienced a similar but transient loss of vision in the same eye, which lasted for five minutes. He has hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and peripheral vascular disease. He had an anterior wall myocardial infarction six years ago. His medications include Glyburide, Captopril, Atenolol, Simvastatin, and Aspirin. His temperature is 36.7°C (98.0°F), respirations are 16/min, pulse is 88/min, and blood pressure is 146/88 mmHg. Examination of the right eye reveals visual acuity of 20/60 and subtle retinal whitening. A right carotid bruit is heard. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in the management of this patient?

- **A.** Topical pilocarpine
- **B.** Ocular massage and high flow oxygen
- **C.** Systemic corticosteroids
- **D.** Topical beta blocker
### Discussion
The patient has:
- Sudden, painless monocular visual loss.
- A preceding episode of transient visual loss (amaurosis fugax).
- Retinal whitening on examination.
- A carotid bruit, suggesting significant carotid atherosclerotic disease.

These findings strongly suggest **acute central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)**.

**Correct answer: B. Ocular massage and high-flow oxygen** (likely intended exam answer).

- **A. Topical pilocarpine:** Used in selected glaucoma settings, not as standard treatment for CRAO.
- **B. Ocular massage and high-flow oxygen:** Traditional measures, but they have not demonstrated reliable visual benefit and may be harmful.
- **C. Systemic corticosteroids:** Not routine treatment for embolic CRAO; indicated urgently if giant cell arteritis is suspected.
- **D. Topical beta-blocker:** May lower IOP but is not definitive treatment for CRAO.

**Clinical management:** Immediate referral to a stroke centre for emergency assessment and consideration of time-sensitive reperfusion treatment in eligible patients. Do not delay stroke evaluation to perform ocular massage or administer oxygen as a substitute for definitive assessment.

**Exam takeaway:** Choose **B** if forced to select from the given options; clinically, CRAO is an ophthalmic and systemic vascular emergency.

Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- **A.** Acute angle-closure glaucoma
- **B.** Acute anterior uveitis
- **C.** Amaurosis fugax
- **D.** Central retinal vein occlusion
### Discussion
The patient is an 80-year-old man with sudden, painless loss of vision in the left eye. Fundus examination reveals optic disc swelling, retinal hemorrhages, dilated tortuous veins, and cotton-wool spots.

**Correct answer: D. Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO)**

- **Dilated, tortuous retinal veins:** Characteristic of CRVO.
- **Retinal hemorrhages:** Due to impaired venous drainage and increased retinal venous pressure.
- **Optic disc edema and cotton-wool spots:** Reflect retinal congestion and ischemia.

| Option | Why incorrect |
|---|---|
| A. Acute angle-closure glaucoma | Severe pain, red eye, halos, mid-dilated pupil |

**Exam pearl:** CRVO produces a *blood-and-thunder fundus* — widespread retinal hemorrhages, dilated tortuous veins, disc edema and cotton-wool spots.

## Question 38

Which patient with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis is most likely to suffer a secondary uveitic glaucoma?

- **A.** Still's disease
- **B.** Pauciarticular, ANA-positive, RF-negative
- **C.** Polyarticular, ANA-positive, RF-positive
- **D.** Pauciarticular, ANA-positive, RF-positivef

### Discussion
**Correct answer: B. Pauciarticular, ANA-positive, RF-negative.**

Children with oligoarticular (pauciarticular) JIA, particularly those who are ANA-positive and RF-negative, have a high risk of chronic anterior uveitis. | Young age at arthritis onset | Increased risk                                               |
| RF positivity                | Not the classic high-risk profile for JIA-associated uveitis |

**Exam pearl:** Oligoarticular JIA + ANA positivity → chronic, often asymptomatic anterior uveitis → complications include cataract, band-shaped keratopathy and secondary glaucoma.

## Question 40

A patient develops traumatic hyphema that resolves within one week. However, the affected eye remains hypotonous for several months despite retaining good visual acuity. While on vacation, the patient suddenly develops severe ocular pain and blurred vision in the previously injured eye. Examination in the emergency department reveals an intraocular pressure of 62 mmHg measured by Schiotz tonometry. Treatment with topical molol and oral acetazolamide is initiated. What is the most likely cause of this sudden elevation in intraocular pressure?

- **A.** Angle recession glaucoma
- **B.** Ghost cell glaucoma
- **C.** Recurrent hyphema
- **D.** Spontaneous closure of a cyclodialysis cleft
### Discussion
Answer is D → Cyclodialysis of Cleft
**Signs of Blunt Trauma:**
1. Sub Conjunctival Hemorrhages
2. Iridodialysis → D shaped Pupil
3. Ciliary Body → Cyclodialysis → Detachment of CB → CB shutdown → ↓ IOP
4. Lens: Rosette Cataract + Vossious Ring + Subluxation
5. Vitreous Hemorrhage
6. Retina
	1. Macular Hole
	2. Macular Edema (Berlin's Edema) → Cherry Red Spot
	3. Retinal Hole → Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment
7. Raccoon Sign/Panda Sign
8. Blowout Fracture

**Cyclodialysis** is the separation of the ciliary body from the scleral spur, creating an abnormal communication between the anterior chamber and the suprachoroidal space.

- **Cause:** Blunt ocular trauma; occasionally surgery.
- **Mechanism:** Aqueous humor escapes into the suprachoroidal space, increasing uveoscleral outflow and reducing IOP.

Normal pathway:
Anterior chamber → Trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal

In cyclodialysis:
Anterior chamber → Cyclodialysis cleft → Suprachoroidal space

| Feature | Explanation |
|---|---|
| IOP | Low (ocular hypotony) |
| Complications | Choroidal detachment, hypotony maculopathy, reduced vision |
| Diagnosis | Gonioscopy; ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) if difficult to visualize |
| Treatment | Observation for small clefts; cycloplegics may help; persistent clefts may require laser or surgical closure |

**Why does IOP suddenly rise after closure?**

1. Cleft forms → aqueous escapes into the suprachoroidal space → IOP falls.
2. Cleft persists → prolonged ocular hypotony.
3. Cleft closes spontaneously → abnormal outflow pathway disappears.
4. IOP spikes → aqueous outflow may temporarily become inadequate, producing severe ocular hypertension.

**Exam pearl:** Traumatic hyphema followed by prolonged hypotony and then sudden severe ocular hypertension suggests spontaneous closure of a cyclodialysis cleft.
