# Neuro-ophthalmology

## 1. Neuro-ophthalmic framework

Neuro-ophthalmology is primarily a localization discipline. A patient with visual loss, an abnormal pupil, diplopia, abnormal eye movements, optic-disc swelling, or a characteristic visual-field defect can often be localized by combining:

- visual acuity and contrast/brightness perception
- color vision
- pupillary responses, especially the relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)
- visual-field pattern
- optic-disc appearance
- ocular alignment and movements
- associated cranial-nerve, brainstem, cerebellar, cortical, or systemic findings

The central rule for visual-pathway localization is that **retinal fibers represent the visual field in reverse**: temporal visual field information travels in nasal retinal fibers, while nasal visual field information travels in temporal retinal fibers. At the optic chiasm, nasal retinal fibers cross and temporal retinal fibers remain uncrossed. Therefore, lesions posterior to the chiasm produce defects in the **contralateral visual hemifield**.

## 2. Optic nerve: anatomy and clinical examination

### 2.1 Anatomy

The optic nerve is formed by the axons of retinal ganglion cells. Fibers run from the retina through the optic nerve, chiasm, and optic tract to the lateral geniculate body without an intervening synapse before the lateral geniculate relay.

Approximate optic-nerve dimensions:

| Segment | Approximate length |
|---|---:|
| Intraocular/optic-disc segment | ~1 mm |
| Intraorbital segment | 25-30 mm |
| Intracanalicular segment | ~9 mm |
| Intracranial segment | ~16 mm |
| Total optic nerve | 3.5-5 cm |

The **intraorbital segment is the longest part** and provides the nerve with slack for eye movement. The optic nerve and ophthalmic artery pass through the optic foramen.

The optic disc is the blind spot because it contains no rods or cones. Normal optic-nerve myelination begins posterior to the globe; myelinated retinal nerve fibers extending onto the optic disc are an anatomical variation and do not themselves cause visual loss.

### 2.2 Optic-nerve disorder: core clinical pattern

A typical optic neuropathy may produce:

- reduced visual acuity
- visual-field loss
- dyschromatopsia, especially acquired red-green impairment with optic-nerve disease
- reduced brightness perception
- a relative afferent pupillary defect when the lesion is asymmetric

The RAPD is an important early sign of asymmetric optic-nerve dysfunction and is elicited with the **swinging flashlight test**.

### 2.3 Visual-field patterns associated with optic-nerve disease

Common patterns include:

- **Central scotoma** - classic of optic neuritis and many toxic/nutritional optic neuropathies.
- **Centrocecal scotoma** - extends between the fixation point and the blind spot; classically associated with toxic/nutritional and tobacco-related optic neuropathy.
- **Nerve-fiber bundle/Bjerrum scotoma** - classically associated with glaucoma.
- **Sector defect** - segmental optic-nerve or retinal vascular involvement.
- **Altitudinal defect** - strongly associated with anterior ischemic optic neuropathy.

## 3. Visual pathway

> **Diagram omitted:** Visual pathway.
### 3.1 Retina -> optic nerve -> chiasm -> tract -> LGN -> optic radiations -> visual cortex

The major visual pathway is:

**Retinal photoreceptors -> bipolar cells -> retinal ganglion cells -> optic nerve -> optic chiasm -> optic tract -> lateral geniculate nucleus/body (LGN/LGB) -> optic radiations -> primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.**

At the optic chiasm:

- nasal retinal fibers cross to the opposite side
- temporal retinal fibers remain ipsilateral

Therefore:

- each optic tract contains **ipsilateral temporal retinal fibers + contralateral nasal retinal fibers**
- the left optic tract and left visual cortex process the **right visual field**
- the right optic tract and right visual cortex process the **left visual field**

**Field-of-view rule:** loss of a fiber bundle produces the corresponding represented visual-field loss; after the chiasm, the affected visual field is contralateral to the lesion.

### 3.2 Optic chiasm and neighboring anatomy

The optic chiasm lies in relation to the pituitary region, explaining why pituitary lesions classically produce bitemporal hemianopia. Important causes of bitemporal hemianopia include:

- third-ventricular glioma or obstructive CSF disturbance causing ventricular enlargement and chiasmal compression
- pituitary macroadenoma (>10 mm / 1 cm)
- craniopharyngioma
- anterior communicating artery aneurysm

### 3.3 Chiasmal junctional syndrome

At the **optic nerve-optic chiasm junction**, the classic anterior-knee/Wilbrand-knee pattern can involve fibers from the opposite nasal retina looping into the affected optic nerve before continuing posteriorly.

A lesion affecting one optic nerve plus selected crossed fibers can produce:

- complete ipsilateral anopia
- a contralateral superotemporal/supratemporal quadrantanopic defect

This is the classic **junctional scotoma**, classically associated with a compressive lesion such as meningioma.

The inferior nasal fibers are the fibers emphasized in the classic Wilbrand-knee explanation.

> **Diagram omitted:** Anterior pathway visual-field localization.
### 3.4 Lateral optic chiasm

A lateral chiasmal lesion is a rare/theoretical lesion and would affect the temporal retinal fibers. Because each side contributes temporal fibers from the ipsilateral eye, the resulting defect is **binasal hemianopia** and is therefore effectively bilateral.

#### 3.5 Optic tract

An optic-tract lesion produces a **contralateral homonymous hemianopia**. The defect is generally incongruous compared with the more posterior pathway.

Optic-tract lesions can also affect pupillomotor fibers because these fibers leave the optic tract before the lateral geniculate relay. The resulting abnormal hemianopic pupillary response is classically termed **Wernicke's hemianopic pupil**.

With established optic-tract disease, optic atrophy can develop bilaterally because each optic tract contains fibers from both eyes.

#### 3.6 Lateral geniculate body

The lateral geniculate body is the principal first relay of the visual pathway.

Six laminae are organized into:

- **Magnocellular layers:** 1 and 2 - larger neurons, important for gross motion and movement-related processing.
- **Parvocellular layers:** 3-6 - important for fine form and color-related processing.
- **Koniocellular cells:** occupy interlamellar regions and contribute to blue/color pathways.

Eye of origin in the LGN:

- **Contralateral eye:** layers 1, 4, 6
- **Ipsilateral eye:** layers 2, 3, 5

An incomplete or sectoral lesion of the LGN can produce characteristic, highly localized field defects. An incomplete or sectoral lesion of the lateral geniculate body can produce a **keyhole visual-field defect**; posterior choroidal circulation is relevant to this localization.

> **Diagram omitted:** LGN organization and visual-field localization.
#### 3.7 Differentiating optic-tract, LGN and optic-radiation lesions

| Feature | Optic tract | LGN | Optic radiations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pupillary finding | **Wernicke hemianopic pupil** may occur | Normal | Normal |
| Optic atrophy | May become bilateral | May become bilateral | No optic atrophy |
| Field congruity | Incongruous | Incongruous | Increasingly congruous, especially posteriorly |

The absence of optic atrophy does not exclude a retrochiasmal lesion; it is particularly expected with a pure optic-radiation lesion.

### 3.7 Optic radiations

From the LGN, fibers reach the visual cortex through the optic radiations.

#### Meyer loop - inferior optic radiations

- passes anteriorly through the temporal lobe
- carries information representing the **contralateral superior visual field**
- lesion -> **contralateral superior homonymous quadrantanopia**
- mnemonic: **Pie in the sky**
- useful memory aid: **TIPS = Temporal, Inferior, Pie in the Sky**

#### Baum loop - superior optic radiations

- passes through the parietal lobe
- carries information representing the **contralateral inferior visual field**
- lesion -> **contralateral inferior homonymous quadrantanopia**
- mnemonic: **Pie on the floor**

The posterior visual-field defect becomes increasingly **congruous** as lesions move posteriorly toward the occipital cortex.

> **Diagram omitted:** Retro-chiasmal visual-field localization.
### 3.8 Visual cortex

The primary visual cortex is supplied predominantly by the **posterior cerebral artery (PCA)**. The occipital pole/tip representing the macular field has additional blood supply from the **middle cerebral artery (MCA)**.

Therefore:

- PCA occipital infarction -> **contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing**
- injury involving the occipital pole/macular representation -> **contralateral homonymous macular defect**
- isolated MCA involvement of the occipital pole can produce central/macacular visual loss

The term **cookie-cutter effect** is used for the striking central visual defect produced when the macular cortical representation at the occipital tip is involved.

Macular sparing is a classic feature of an occipital cortical lesion because the macular representation has dual vascular supply. Pupillary light responses can remain normal with a purely cortical/radiation lesion because the pupillomotor pathway has already diverged from the visual pathway in the optic tract before the lateral geniculate relay.

> **Diagram omitted:** Keyhole and cortical visual-field localization.
Posterior visual-system lesions can also produce higher cortical manifestations:

- temporal-lobe lesions: formed visual hallucinations and, depending on location, olfactory/gustatory hallucinations
- parietal lesions: contralateral inferior quadrantanopia; dominant parietal involvement may produce acalculia and agraphia
- deeper parietal lesions can disturb optokinetic responses; an asymmetric defect in the optokinetic response can help localize posterior hemispheric dysfunction

Additional field patterns:

- **Bi-superior hemianopia** is an altitudinal field pattern that respects the horizontal meridian; important associations include glaucoma, optic-disc drusen, and anterior ischemic optic neuropathy.
- **Keyhole defect:** incomplete LGN/retro-chiasmal lesion producing a localized defect within a developing homonymous hemianopia; do not confuse with the phrase "keyhole vision" used in retinitis pigmentosa.
- **Sectoral quadrantanopia** can occur with focal geniculocortical vascular lesions; anterior choroidal artery involvement is a recognized association.

## 5. Pupillary system

### 5.1 Pupillary light reflex

> **Diagram omitted:** Pupillary light-reflex pathway.
The afferent limb of the pupillary light reflex is shared with the visual pathway up to the optic tract. Pupillomotor fibers leave the optic tract before the lateral geniculate relay.

**Light -> retina -> optic nerve -> optic tract -> pretectal nucleus -> bilateral Edinger-Westphal nuclei via internuncial neurons -> bilateral oculomotor nerves -> ciliary ganglia -> short ciliary nerves -> sphincter pupillae -> miosis.**

Because each pretectal nucleus projects bilaterally to both Edinger-Westphal nuclei, illumination of one eye produces:

- a **direct** light response in the stimulated eye
- a **consensual** response in the opposite eye

### 5.2 Afferent pupillary defects

#### Absolute afferent pupillary defect (AAPD)

A severe/complete afferent lesion causes:

- isocoria at rest
- light in the normal eye -> bilateral constriction
- light in the severely affected eye -> neither eye constricts

#### Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD, Marcus Gunn pupil)

RAPD indicates an **asymmetry of afferent input**, most often from asymmetric optic-nerve or severe retinal disease.

On the swinging flashlight test:

1. light is directed to the normal eye -> both pupils constrict
2. light is swung to the diseased eye -> the afferent stimulus is weaker
3. both pupils dilate relative to their preceding constricted state, giving the **paradoxical dilation** characteristic of RAPD

The RAPD is a major sign of optic-nerve dysfunction and is especially useful in unilateral/asymmetric disease.

### 5.3 Efferent pupillary defects

Efferent defects affect the parasympathetic/sympathetic output to the pupil and therefore produce **anisocoria**. The exam-oriented notes use a pupil-size difference of **>0.4 mm** as a practical threshold for anisocoria.

A useful bedside rule:

- anisocoria **greater in the dark** -> the abnormal pupil is the constricted pupil -> think sympathetic pathway/Horner syndrome
- anisocoria **greater in bright light** -> the abnormal pupil is the dilated pupil -> think parasympathetic/CN III pathway defect

### 5.4 Argyll Robertson pupil

Classic features:

- small, often irregular pupils
- poor or absent light reaction
- accommodation/near response preserved
- **light-near dissociation**
- classically bilateral
- classically associated with **neurosyphilis**

Memory: **accommodation present, pupillary light reflex absent**.

### 5.5 Adie (Holmes-Adie) tonic pupil

Typical pattern:

- usually unilateral dilated pupil
- poor/sluggish light reaction
- tonic near response
- segmental iris sphincter palsy may be visible
- lesion at the ciliary ganglion or short ciliary nerves
- associated with reduced/absent deep tendon reflexes in Holmes-Adie syndrome

Dilute pilocarpine (classically 0.125%) may produce constriction because of denervation hypersensitivity.

### 5.6 Pharmacological mydriasis

Typical pattern:

- dilated pupil
- accommodation is impaired
- no meaningful constriction even with a strong cholinergic challenge such as 1% pilocarpine in the classic exam algorithm
- atropine is a classic cause

#### 5.7 Hutchinson pupil

A unilateral fixed, dilated pupil caused by **compressive CN III involvement in uncal/transtentorial herniation** is a neurosurgical emergency.

#### 5.8 Corneal and vestibulo-ocular reflexes

**Corneal reflex:**

- afferent: CN V, especially ophthalmic division
- efferent: CN VII to orbicularis oculi
- function: blinking after corneal stimulation

**Vestibulo-ocular reflex / oculocephalic (Doll's-eye) reflex:**

- afferent: CN VIII vestibular system
- efferent: CN III and VI
- purpose: maintain gaze during head movement and assess brainstem function in appropriate patients

## 6. Sympathetic pathway and Horner syndrome

> **Diagram omitted:** Oculosympathetic pathway.
The pupillary dilator is supplied by the sympathetic pathway:

**Hypothalamus -> brainstem -> ciliospinal center of Budge (cervical/upper thoracic spinal cord, classically C8-T2) -> superior cervical ganglion -> internal carotid plexus -> cavernous sinus -> long ciliary nerves -> dilator pupillae.**

### 6.1 Three neuronal levels

**First-order neuron (central):** hypothalamus -> spinal cord.

Possible lesions include:

- brainstem lesions
- Wallenberg syndrome
- syringomyelia
- other central autonomic lesions

**Second-order neuron (preganglionic):** spinal cord -> superior cervical ganglion, passing near the lung apex.

Important causes include:

- Pancoast tumor
- neck lesions
- carotid or aortic pathology involving the course of the fibers

**Third-order neuron (postganglionic):** superior cervical ganglion -> internal carotid artery -> cavernous sinus -> orbit.

Important causes include:

- internal carotid artery dissection
- cluster headache-associated sympathetic dysfunction
- otitis/middle-ear disease
- cavernous-sinus lesions

### 6.2 Horner syndrome

Horner syndrome is an **oculosympathetic palsy** caused by interruption of the sympathetic supply to the eye and facial structures.

Ipsilateral features:

- **miosis** from unopposed sphincter pupillae activity
- **mild ptosis** from loss of sympathetic innervation to Müller's muscle
- **anhidrosis**, depending on the level of the lesion
- loss of the ciliospinal reflex
- apparent enophthalmos may be described, but true enophthalmos is not a defining feature
- **heterochromia iridis** is especially associated with congenital/early-onset Horner syndrome
- inferior-lid elevation can accompany the ptosis, producing a narrowed palpebral fissure

Classic examination/localization tests include:

- **4% cocaine test:** normal pupil dilates; a Horner pupil shows poor/no dilation because norepinephrine reuptake is blocked but presynaptic release is impaired
- **1% apraclonidine test:** affected Horner pupil may dilate through denervation hypersensitivity; this is the classic positive response used to demonstrate Horner syndrome
- **amphetamine/adrenaline testing:** historically used for localization to preganglionic versus postganglionic lesions

# 7. Optic neuropathies

## 7.1 Optic neuritis

Optic neuritis is an inflammatory/demyelinating optic neuropathy. **Multiple sclerosis** is a classic association.

Typical features:

- subacute unilateral visual loss
- central scotoma
- red-green dyschromatopsia
- pain, especially with eye movement
- RAPD when unilateral/asymmetric
- **Uhthoff phenomenon:** transient worsening with increased body temperature, classically after exercise
- **Pulfrich phenomenon/sign:** abnormal depth perception from asymmetric conduction delay between the two optic nerves

### Fundus patterns

- **Papillitis:** optic-disc swelling is visible.
- **Retrobulbar optic neuritis:** the optic disc may initially appear normal despite visual dysfunction.
- **Neuroretinitis:** optic-disc edema associated with a macular star of hard exudates.

The classic exam phrase is:

- papillitis: **patient sees poorly, examiner sees disc swelling**
- retrobulbar neuritis: **patient sees poorly, examiner may see a normal disc**

### Treatment principle

The Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial established that high-dose corticosteroid treatment, particularly intravenous methylprednisolone followed by a short oral course in the original regimen, **accelerates visual recovery**; oral prednisone alone at the low dose used in the original trial was not beneficial and increased recurrence risk. Disease-specific management is required when optic neuritis is part of multiple sclerosis or another inflammatory disorder; interferon-based disease-modifying therapy is directed at multiple sclerosis rather than used as the acute optic-neuritis treatment itself.

## 7.2 Papilledema

Papilledema is **optic-disc edema caused by raised intracranial pressure**. It is generally bilateral and, early in the course, does not primarily damage optic-nerve fibers enough to reduce visual acuity.

Mechanism:

- increased intracranial/retrolaminar pressure alters the translaminar pressure gradient
- axoplasmic transport becomes stagnant
- intracellular fluid accumulates in optic-nerve axons
- the optic disc becomes swollen

> **Diagram omitted:** Papillitis, papilledema and anterior ischemic optic neuropathy fundus appearances.
Early features:

- blurring of the optic-disc margin; nasal margin blurring is an early clue
- venous dilation/congestion
- visual acuity often preserved initially
- normal or near-normal color vision early
- enlargement of the blind spot on perimetry
- transient visual obscurations/amaurosis fugax-like episodes may occur

As papilledema becomes severe or chronic:

- visual-field constriction may develop
- optic-nerve damage may become permanent
- chronic papilledema can end in secondary optic atrophy

**Paton's lines** are retinochoroidal folds around the optic disc caused by the mechanical effects of edema.

### 7.3 Papilledema versus optic neuritis/papillitis

| Feature | Papilledema | Optic neuritis / papillitis |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Raised ICP | Inflammation/demyelination |
| Laterality | Usually bilateral | Often unilateral/asymmetric |
| Visual acuity early | Usually preserved | Usually reduced |
| Color vision | Usually preserved early | Often reduced |
| RAPD | Usually absent if symmetric | Often present if unilateral/asymmetric |
| Visual field | Enlarged blind spot early; constriction later | Central scotoma common |
| Pain with eye movement | Absent | Typical |
| Disc swelling | Marked in established papilledema | May be mild in papillitis |

## 7.4 Anterior ischemic optic neuropathy

AION is an ischemic infarction of the optic nerve head and is divided into:

- **arteritic AION (AAION)**
- **non-arteritic AION (NAION)**

### AAION

The major cause emphasized is **giant cell arteritis (GCA)**.

Typical pattern:

- sudden, severe visual loss
- optic-disc edema that may be pale/chalky
- altitudinal field defect is common
- RAPD when unilateral/asymmetric
- can be accompanied by transient visual loss (amaurosis)

AAION is a visual emergency because the fellow eye can become involved.

When GCA is suspected, immediate corticosteroid treatment is required; laboratory and vascular evaluation is performed urgently.

### NAION

Typical pattern:

- sudden, usually painless visual loss
- often noticed on awakening or in the early morning
- altitudinal field defect is strongly associated
- RAPD if unilateral/asymmetric
- reduced color saturation and brightness perception
- blurred/swollen optic disc
- vascular mechanism involves the short posterior ciliary circulation

## 7.5 Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON)

Characteristic features:

- mitochondrial inheritance, classically maternal
- genetically determined optic neuropathy
- sequential visual involvement of the two eyes is typical
- pseudoedematous optic-disc appearance with retinal vascular changes may occur early
- pupils may be relatively normal when both eyes are symmetrically affected, with little or no RAPD
- progressive central visual loss can lead to optic atrophy

### 7.6 Toxic and nutritional optic neuropathy

Typical exam pattern:

- bilateral central/centrocecal scotomas
- impaired color vision
- optic-disc changes may be subtle initially

The mnemonic **M-DICE** summarizes classic toxic associations:

- **M** - methanol
- **D** - digoxin
- **I** - isoniazid
- **C** - chloroquine
- **E** - ethambutol

Ethambutol classically causes color-vision disturbance before more obvious visual loss.

Tobacco-related toxic optic neuropathy can produce a **centrocecal scotoma**, and cyanide toxicity is an important toxic association.

### 7.7 Traumatic optic neuropathy

Blunt or penetrating orbital/head trauma can produce optic-nerve dysfunction and eventually primary optic atrophy. An RAPD, color deficit, reduced acuity, and field loss depend on the severity and side of involvement.

## 7.8 Optic atrophy

> **Diagram omitted:** Types of optic atrophy.
### Primary optic atrophy

The optic disc is:

- chalky white
- sharply defined
- classically with a relatively preserved/shallow cup

Associations include:

- optic neuritis
- compressive tumors
- trauma
- hereditary optic neuropathies such as LHON
- toxic/nutritional optic neuropathies
- neurosyphilis

### Secondary optic atrophy

Occurs after antecedent disc swelling.

Disc appearance:

- dirty gray-white pallor
- blurred/ill-defined margins

Associations include:

- papilledema
- papillitis
- AION

### Consecutive optic atrophy

Results from disease of the retina and its blood supply.

Examples include:

- retinal vascular occlusion
- healed retinal vasculitis
- extensive panretinal photocoagulation
- retinitis pigmentosa

A classic retinitis pigmentosa triad is:

1. bone-spicule pigmentation
2. attenuated retinal arterioles
3. pale waxy optic disc

Glaucomatous optic atrophy is a separate pattern characterized by progressive neuroretinal rim loss and enlargement/deepening of the cup.

## 8. Color vision in neuro-ophthalmology

Color testing is useful because acquired color loss can localize pathology.

Primary color terminology:

- red = **protan**
- green = **deutan**
- blue = **tritan**

### Acquired patterns

- blue-yellow disturbance: commonly associated with retinal disease and may occur in glaucoma
- red-green disturbance: characteristic of optic-nerve dysfunction
- generalized age-related blue discrimination loss can occur with aging

### Congenital color defects

Usually X-linked recessive for red-green defects, with males affected more frequently and females acting as carriers.

**Dichromatism:** one cone system is absent.

- protanopia
- deuteranopia
- tritanopia

**Anomalous trichromatism:** all three cone systems are present, but one has abnormal sensitivity.

- protanomaly
- deuteranomaly
- tritanomaly

**Achromatopsia / monochromatism:** severe or complete loss of normal color discrimination.

### Tests

- **Ishihara:** common pseudo-isochromatic test for red-green deficiency; the standard set contains 38 plates in the full version and does not reliably detect tritan defects.
- **Hardy-Rand-Rittler:** pseudo-isochromatic screening, useful in children; uses simple shape symbols and can detect a broader range of congenital color defects than red-green-only screening.
- **Farnsworth-Munsell 100 Hue:** highly sensitive, detailed quantitative color assessment.
- **Farnsworth-Munsell 15 Hue:** less sensitive than the 100 Hue test and useful for simpler assessment.
- Holmgren wool test.
- Nagel anomaloscope.
- Lantern testing.

## 10. Cranial-nerve palsies and diplopia

### 10.1 Third-nerve (oculomotor) palsy

A complete CN III palsy can produce:

- **ptosis** from levator palpebrae superioris paralysis
- **down-and-out** eye position
- impaired adduction, elevation, and depression
- **mydriasis** when parasympathetic fibers are involved
- loss of accommodation
- crossed diplopia

Mechanistically, the down-and-out position reflects unopposed lateral rectus and superior oblique actions.

### 10.2 Fourth-nerve (trochlear) palsy

The affected muscle is the superior oblique.

Typical findings:

- impaired intorsion
- impaired depression in adduction
- ipsilateral hypertropia
- excyclotorsion
- vertical diplopia, particularly on downgaze
- head tilt away from the affected side as a compensation
- hypertropia increases with head tilt toward the affected side and with gaze in the direction that requires the superior oblique to depress the eye

The **Bielschowsky head-tilt test** is used to support the diagnosis.

### 10.3 Sixth-nerve (abducens) palsy

The affected muscle is the lateral rectus.

Findings:

- impaired abduction
- esotropia
- horizontal, **uncrossed** diplopia
- face turn toward the affected side to maintain binocular single vision

### 10.4 Pattern of diplopia

A useful clinical rule is that diplopia is worst in the field of action of the weak muscle.

- CN III palsy -> complex diplopia with ptosis and possible anisocoria
- CN IV palsy -> vertical/torsional diplopia, especially when looking down
- CN VI palsy -> horizontal uncrossed diplopia

## 11. Horizontal gaze circuitry

### 11.1 Frontal eye field -> PPRF -> abducens nucleus -> MLF -> contralateral medial rectus

For a horizontal gaze command:

**Frontal eye field -> contralateral PPRF -> ipsilateral abducens nucleus ->**

- ipsilateral lateral rectus directly
- interneurons crossing and ascending in the MLF to the contralateral oculomotor nucleus -> contralateral medial rectus

The **paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF)** is the principal horizontal gaze center in the pons.

> **Diagram omitted:** PPRF, MLF and internuclear ophthalmoplegia.
### 11.2 PPRF lesion

A lesion of the PPRF produces an **ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy** because the horizontal gaze command cannot activate the ipsilateral abducens system.

### 11.3 Vertical gaze centers

The **rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (riMLF)** in the midbrain is an important vertical gaze center. The **interstitial nucleus of Cajal** contributes to vertical and torsional gaze holding. Lesions in these midbrain gaze-control structures cause characteristic vertical or torsional ocular-motor deficits.

### 11.4 Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)

INO results from a lesion of the **medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF)**.

Classic findings:

- impaired **adduction of the eye ipsilateral to the MLF lesion**
- nystagmus of the abducting eye on the opposite side
- diplopia may occur
- convergence can be relatively preserved because the medial-rectus motor apparatus itself is not destroyed

**Multiple sclerosis** is a classic cause, especially of bilateral INO.

### 11.5 Duane retraction syndrome

Duane retraction syndrome is a congenital ocular-motor disorder with anomalous innervation of the lateral rectus. The characteristic finding is **globe retraction with narrowing of the palpebral fissure on adduction**.

The clinical classification is:

The condition is more common in females and the left eye is more frequently involved.

- **Type 1:** abduction predominantly limited.
- **Type 2:** adduction predominantly limited.
- **Type 3:** both abduction and adduction limited.

The underlying pattern is a developmental absence/hypoplasia of the abducens nerve or nucleus with aberrant innervation of the lateral rectus by branches of CN III.

## 12. Brainstem syndromes with neuro-ophthalmic findings

Certain brainstem syndromes combine a characteristic ocular-motor cranial-nerve deficit with long-tract neurological signs.

### Weber syndrome

A midbrain lesion classically produces:

- **ipsilateral CN III palsy**
- **contralateral hemiparesis** from corticospinal-tract involvement

### Benedikt syndrome

A tegmental midbrain lesion classically produces:

- **ipsilateral CN III palsy**
- **contralateral tremor, choreoathetosis, or ataxia** from red-nucleus/cerebellar connections

### Millard-Gubler syndrome

A ventral pontine lesion classically produces:

- **ipsilateral CN VII palsy**
- **contralateral hemiparesis**

CN VI involvement can accompany the pontine lesion depending on its extent.

## 13. Nystagmus and abnormal eye movements

### 13.1 Optokinetic nystagmus (OKN)

OKN is elicited when the eyes track a continuously moving visual field rather than a single discrete target.

It can be used:

- to assess visually driven ocular-motor function
- in young children who cannot cooperate with conventional acuity testing
- to uncover or assess some forms of malingering

A **defective optokinetic response toward the side of a deep parietal lesion**, with a relatively preserved response in the opposite direction, can help localize posterior hemispheric dysfunction.

### 13.2 Vestibulo-ocular reflex / Doll's-eye response

The vestibulo-ocular reflex maintains gaze despite head movement. Head movement to one side produces compensatory ocular movement in the opposite direction when the reflex is intact.

The pathway is used clinically to assess brainstem function and involves:

- CN VIII afferent input
- CN III and VI efferent ocular motor output

### 15. Myasthenia gravis as a neuro-ophthalmic disorder

Typical ocular myasthenic features include:

- bilateral ptosis
- fluctuation/worsening later in the day
- diplopia
- fatigability of ocular movements
- **Cogan lid twitch**

The classic historical **Tensilon (edrophonium) test** demonstrates transient improvement in ptosis or ophthalmoplegia. In contemporary practice, diagnosis usually relies on clinical assessment plus appropriate antibody/electrophysiologic testing rather than routine edrophonium use.

#### 16. Thyroid eye disease and restrictive ophthalmopathy

Thyroid eye disease can cause both restrictive ocular-motor disease and **compressive optic neuropathy**.

Important features include:

- may occur in hyperthyroid, hypothyroid, or euthyroid patients
- autoimmune inflammation affects orbital fibroblasts and extraocular muscles
- increased muscle volume and orbital connective-tissue changes increase orbital pressure and may produce proptosis

##### 16.1 Order of extraocular-muscle involvement

**Inferior rectus -> medial rectus -> superior rectus -> lateral rectus**.

Memory aid: **I M S L**.

The inferior rectus is typically involved first.

##### 16.2 Motility pattern

Inferior-rectus fibrosis produces:

- restricted elevation
- mechanical/restrictive limitation rather than pure paresis
- forced-duction test is positive when mechanical restriction is present

The muscle belly is primarily involved; relative tendon sparing helps distinguish thyroid myopathy from some other restrictive processes.

##### 16.3 Compressive optic neuropathy in thyroid eye disease

Enlarged muscles at the orbital apex can compress the optic nerve, producing optic neuropathy. This is **compressive optic neuropathy, not optic neuritis**.

For threatened vision, treatment includes:

- corticosteroids
- radiation/immunosuppression in appropriate cases
- orbital decompression when vision is threatened or severe orbital crowding/exposure is present

#### 17. Cavernous sinus, superior orbital fissure and orbital apex syndromes

> **Diagram omitted:** Cavernous sinus, orbital apex and related orbital syndromes.
Relevant anatomy:

- optic nerve and ophthalmic artery -> optic canal/foramen
- cranial nerves III, IV, V1, and VI are related to the superior orbital fissure/cavernous sinus region
- V1 and VI have particularly important relationships to the annulus of Zinn in the orbit

##### 17.1 Cavernous sinus thrombosis

Cavernous sinus thrombosis can arise as a complication of orbital or facial infection and is an ophthalmic/neurosurgical emergency.

Important clinical localization:

- **CN VI palsy is an early/classic ocular motor sign** because the sixth nerve courses through the cavernous sinus alongside the internal carotid artery
- III and IV palsies may follow
- V1/V2 sensory loss may occur
- corneal sensation may be reduced if V1 is involved
- severe orbital signs can include proptosis and chemosis

Orbital cellulitis requires urgent treatment because of the risk of cavernous sinus thrombosis.

##### 17.2 Tolosa-Hunt syndrome

Granulomatous inflammation involving the cavernous sinus/superior orbital fissure/orbital apex can produce:

- painful ophthalmoplegia
- involvement of multiple ocular motor nerves
- V1 sensory involvement in the relevant anatomic compartment

##### 17.3 Superior orbital fissure syndrome

A superior orbital fissure lesion can involve **CN III, IV, VI and V1**, producing ophthalmoplegia with forehead/corneal sensory loss. The optic nerve is not primarily involved; therefore, **vision may be preserved unless the lesion extends to the orbital apex**.

##### 17.4 Orbital apex syndrome

The orbital apex contains the optic nerve plus ocular motor and sensory pathways. A lesion here can produce a combination of:

- visual loss from CN II involvement
- complete external ophthalmoplegia from III/IV/VI involvement
- V1 sensory loss/corneal anesthesia

This distinguishes orbital-apex disease from isolated cavernous-sinus disease when the optic nerve is also affected.

##### 17.5 Cavernous sinus versus superior orbital fissure versus orbital apex versus orbital cellulitis

| Feature | Cavernous sinus thrombosis | Orbital apex syndrome | Orbital cellulitis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key cranial nerves | III, IV, V1, V2, VI | II, III, IV, V1, VI | II can become involved with severe posterior spread; III, IV, VI may be affected |
| Optic-nerve involvement | Usually absent early | Characteristic | May occur in severe/posterior disease |
| Corneal sensation | Reduced if V1 involved | Reduced if V1 involved | Usually preserved early |
| Ocular motility | Sequential ophthalmoplegia; VI is a classic early clue | Complete external ophthalmoplegia with visual loss | Restriction from orbital inflammation |
| Laterality | May begin unilateral and become bilateral | Usually unilateral | Usually unilateral |
| Proptosis/chemosis | Marked | Less prominent than the ophthalmoplegia/visual loss combination | Marked |

##### 17.6 Carotid-cavernous fistula

A carotid-cavernous fistula is a vascular communication between the carotid circulation and cavernous sinus that can produce a distinctly neuro-ophthalmic presentation. Important features include:

- **pulsatile, often bilateral proptosis**
- ophthalmoplegia, with **CN VI classically affected earliest**
- trauma is a common association, particularly with direct high-flow fistulas
- vascular imaging, particularly **digital-subtraction angiography**, for definitive characterization

#### 18. Foster Kennedy and pseudo-Foster Kennedy syndromes

##### Foster Kennedy syndrome

Classically associated with a frontal/olfactory-groove mass and characterized by the classic pattern of:

- ipsilateral optic atrophy
- contralateral papilledema
- ipsilateral anosmia

##### Pseudo-Foster Kennedy syndrome

A similar asymmetric disc appearance can occur without a mass, particularly when one optic nerve has old optic atrophy and the opposite eye develops a new optic-disc swelling/ischemic event. **Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy** is an important cause.

# 19. Clinical localization patterns that should be recognized immediately

## 19.1 Monocular visual loss + RAPD

Think **optic nerve or severe retinal afferent disease**.

Central scotoma + dyschromatopsia + pain with eye movement -> optic neuritis is a classic pattern.

Altitudinal defect + sudden painless loss + swollen disc -> ischemic optic neuropathy is a classic pattern.

Centrocecal defect + dyschromatopsia -> toxic/nutritional optic neuropathy is a classic pattern.

## 19.2 Bitemporal hemianopia

Think **optic chiasm** until proven otherwise.

Pituitary macroadenoma is a major exam association.

## 19.3 Monocular loss + contralateral superotemporal quadrantanopia

Think **junctional scotoma** at the optic-nerve/chiasm junction.

## 19.4 Homonymous hemianopia

Think a **retro-chiasmal lesion**.

- incongruous -> optic tract/LGN
- superior quadrantanopia -> temporal/Meyer loop
- inferior quadrantanopia -> parietal/Baum loop
- highly congruous hemianopia with macular sparing -> occipital cortex

## 19.5 Visual loss + normal pupils in bilateral symmetric disease

A normal or minimally asymmetric pupillary response does **not** exclude severe bilateral optic neuropathy when both afferent pathways are affected equally. LHON is an important exam example.

### 19.6 Swollen optic discs

Distinguish:

- papilledema from raised ICP
- papillitis/optic neuritis
- neuroretinitis with macular star
- ischemic optic neuropathy
- pseudopapilledema/optic-disc drusen
- optic-disc drusen causing pseudopapilledema
- myelinated nerve fibers

#### 19.7 Anisocoria

- greater in dark -> small abnormal pupil -> Horner pathway
- greater in light -> large abnormal pupil -> CN III/parasympathetic/pharmacologic causes

#### 19.8 Ptosis + miosis

Think **Horner syndrome**.

#### 19.9 Ptosis + down-and-out eye + mydriasis

Think **CN III palsy with parasympathetic involvement**, and consider compressive causes urgently.

#### 19.10 Abduction failure

Think **CN VI palsy** unless another restrictive or mechanical process explains it.

#### 19.11 Adduction failure + abducting-eye nystagmus

Think **internuclear ophthalmoplegia (MLF lesion)**.

#### 19.12 Bilateral fluctuating ptosis + diplopia

Think **ocular myasthenia gravis**.

## 20. High-yield comparison tables

### 20.1 Optic neuritis versus papilledema versus NAION versus AAION

| Feature | Optic neuritis | Papilledema | NAION | AAION |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical visual loss | Subacute | Usually preserved early | Sudden | Sudden, often severe |
| Pain | Often movement-related | Absent | Usually absent | May be associated with headache/systemic GCA symptoms |
| Laterality | Often unilateral | Usually bilateral | Usually unilateral | May rapidly become bilateral |
| Color vision | Reduced | Usually normal early | Reduced | Markedly reduced |
| Field | Central scotoma | Enlarged blind spot early | Altitudinal common | Altitudinal common |
| RAPD | Common if unilateral | Usually absent if symmetric | Common if unilateral | Common if unilateral |
| Disc | May be normal in retrobulbar neuritis; swollen in papillitis | Swollen bilaterally | Swollen | Often swollen, classically pallid |
| Key association | Multiple sclerosis | Raised ICP | Vascular/anatomic risk factors | Giant cell arteritis |

### 20.2 Afferent versus efferent pupillary lesions

| Feature               | Afferent lesion                                  | Efferent lesion                                                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Nerve | Principal muscle deficit | Typical deviation | Diplopia | Key associated finding |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Optic nerve -> monocular; chiasm -> bitemporal; tract/LGN/radiations/cortex -> contralateral homonymous; Meyer -> superior quadrant; Baum -> inferior quadrant; occipital -> congruous hemianopia with macular sparing when PCA territory is involved.**

### 21. Visual-field testing and neuro-ophthalmic examination

#### 21.1 Formal visual-field examination

Formal perimetry converts the patient's subjective visual field into a reproducible map.

- **Kinetic perimetry** uses moving targets and is useful when a patient cannot perform a conventional static threshold test or when the peripheral field is of particular interest.
- **Static automated perimetry** presents stationary stimuli of varying intensity at predefined points; the **Humphrey Field Analyzer** is a standard example.
- A normal visual field is not perfectly circular; it is broadly oval. The exam figures give approximately **100° temporally, 60° nasally, 50° superiorly, and 70° inferiorly**, with the physiologic blind spot roughly **10°–20° from fixation**. The temporal field is therefore widest and the superior field narrowest, with an inferonasal indentation/notch.
- Mapping a defect as **central, centrocecal, arcuate/nerve-fiber, sectoral, altitudinal, quadrantic, heteronymous, or homonymous** is often more localizing than the absolute amount of visual loss.

#### 21.2 Practical examination sequence

A practical neuro-ophthalmic examination sequence is:

1. **Visual acuity** - quantify central visual loss. **Snellen 6/6** is the conventional normal acuity notation; **LogMAR 0** corresponds to normal acuity, with increasing LogMAR values indicating poorer acuity. In a standard acuity chart, the full letter subtends about 5 minutes of arc and each stroke/interval about 1 minute of arc.
2. **Color vision and brightness comparison** - particularly sensitive to optic-nerve dysfunction.
3. **Pupils** - inspect for anisocoria, direct/consensual responses, and perform the swinging flashlight test.
4. **Visual fields** - identify whether loss is monocular, heteronymous, homonymous, quadrantic, central, altitudinal, or otherwise patterned.
5. **Ocular alignment and motility** - test all relevant gaze positions and look for incomitance and gaze-dependent diplopia.
6. **Cover/uncover and prism testing** when ocular deviation is present; fusional vergence can be assessed with a **RAF ruler**.
7. **Color and sensory fusion tests** such as Ishihara, Worth four-dot, Maddox, and stereopsis testing when indicated.
8. **Fundus examination** - assess disc swelling, pallor, cup/rim, retinal vascular changes, macular star, and other clues to localization.
9. **Photostress recovery**, when macular versus optic-nerve disease is uncertain, can provide a localization clue: visual loss with a relatively normal recovery time favors optic-nerve disease rather than macular dysfunction.
10. **Formal ocular-motor tests** such as Hess/Lees when a paretic muscle is suspected.
11. **Brainstem reflexes** such as corneal and vestibulo-ocular responses when central disease is suspected.


#### 22. Essential exam mnemonics

- **LR6 SO4, rest 3** -> extraocular motor innervation.
- **TIPS** -> Temporal lobe, Inferior optic radiation, Pie in the Sky.
- **Pie on the floor** -> parietal/Baum loop lesion.
- **HIMAPLE** -> heterochromia, inferior lid elevation, miosis, anhidrosis, ptosis, loss of ciliospinal reflex, apparent enophthalmos in Horner syndrome.
- **M-DICE** -> methanol, digoxin, isoniazid, chloroquine, ethambutol.
- **SoLID** -> superior oblique: lateral rotation/abduction component, intorsion, depression.
- **ARP** -> Accommodation Reflex Present, Pupillary reflex absent.
- **DOOR** -> deviation opposite the corneal-reflex displacement.
- **DOOM** -> deviation opposite the compensatory eye movement on cover testing.
- **DOOB** -> deviation opposite the prism base direction.

#### 23. Rapid localization summary

##### Visual loss

**Monocular** -> optic nerve/retina.

**Bitemporal** -> optic chiasm.

**Contralateral homonymous** -> optic tract/LGN/optic radiations/visual cortex.

**Superior quadrantanopia** -> temporal/Meyer loop.

**Inferior quadrantanopia** -> parietal/Baum loop.

**Congruous homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing** -> occipital cortex.

##### Pupil

**RAPD** -> asymmetric afferent pathway disease.

**Small pupil + ptosis + anhidrosis** -> Horner syndrome.

**Large pupil + ptosis + ophthalmoplegia** -> CN III pathway.

**Light-near dissociation** -> Argyll Robertson or related pretectal/postganglionic disorders based on the remainder of the examination.

**Tonic near response + segmental iris palsy** -> Adie pupil.

##### Eye movements

**Abduction failure** -> VI palsy.

**Adduction failure + abducting nystagmus** -> INO/MLF lesion.

**Down-and-out eye + ptosis** -> III palsy.

**Vertical diplopia worse on downgaze** -> IV palsy.

**Fluctuating ptosis + diplopia** -> myasthenia gravis.

##### Optic disc

**Bilateral disc edema + raised ICP context** -> papilledema.

**Disc edema + central visual loss/pain** -> papillitis/optic neuritis.

**Pallid swollen disc + sudden severe visual loss** -> AAION until excluded.

**Swollen disc + early-morning sudden painless visual loss + altitudinal field defect** -> NAION pattern.

**Chalky-white sharply demarcated disc** -> primary optic atrophy.

**Dirty gray-white blurred disc after prior edema** -> secondary optic atrophy.

**Pale waxy disc + attenuated vessels + bone spicules** -> consecutive optic atrophy in retinitis pigmentosa.
