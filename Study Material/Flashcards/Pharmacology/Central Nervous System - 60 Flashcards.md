# Central Nervous System — 60 Flashcards

These cards are derived from the cleaned CNS pharmacology notes and focused on NEET-PG/INI-CET active recall.

#flashcards/Pharmacology/Central-Nervous-System
### CNS-001 — Sedative-hypnotics · Dose-response · progression · receptor: GABA-A / CNS · difficulty: 1
As the dose of a CNS depressant increases, what is the approximate sequence of effects?::Sedation → hypnosis → muscle relaxation → anaesthesia → respiratory depression/coma.

### CNS-002 — Sedative-hypnotics · Barbiturates vs benzodiazepines · comparison · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
What is the classic exam distinction between barbiturates and benzodiazepines at the GABA-A receptor?::Barbiturates prolong the duration of Cl⁻ channel opening, whereas benzodiazepines increase the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening.

### CNS-003 — Sedative-hypnotics · Barbiturates · classification · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
Which barbiturates should you associate with ultrashort, short and long duration of action?::Ultrashort: thiopentone/thiopental and methohexital. Short: butabarbital, amobarbital, pentobarbital and secobarbital. Long: phenobarbital and primidone.

### CNS-004 — Sedative-hypnotics · Barbiturates · high-yield · receptor: CYP / porphyrins · difficulty: 2
Why are barbiturates important in pharmacology with respect to drug metabolism and acute intermittent porphyria?::They induce hepatic CYP enzymes and can induce hepatic heme synthesis; therefore they can precipitate acute hepatic porphyrias such as acute intermittent porphyria.

### CNS-005 — Sedative-hypnotics · Barbiturates · drug-use · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
Which ultrashort-acting barbiturate is classically used for general anaesthesia, and which is associated with ECT?::Thiopentone/thiopental is a general anaesthetic; methohexital is classically used for ECT because its relatively weak anticonvulsant effect permits seizure activity.

### CNS-006 — Sedative-hypnotics · Phenobarbital/primidone · metabolism · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 2
What is the important relationship between primidone and phenobarbital?::Primidone is metabolized to phenobarbital and PEMA; phenobarbital contributes substantially to primidone's antiseizure effect.

### CNS-007 — Sedative-hypnotics · Benzodiazepines · clinical use · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
Which benzodiazepines are high-yield choices for convulsive status epilepticus, and what changes when there is no IV access?::IV lorazepam is a key first-line choice when IV access is available; IM/intranasal/buccal midazolam is useful when IV access is unavailable.

### CNS-008 — Sedative-hypnotics · Benzodiazepines · metabolism · receptor: CYP / glucuronidation · difficulty: 1
Which benzodiazepine mnemonic identifies the agents preferred when significant liver disease makes oxidative metabolism undesirable?::LOT = lorazepam, oxazepam and temazepam; these undergo mainly phase-II glucuronidation and depend less on oxidative CYP metabolism.

### CNS-009 — Sedative-hypnotics · Benzodiazepines · antidote · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
What is the mechanism and major caution of flumazenil?::Flumazenil is a competitive antagonist at the benzodiazepine site on GABA-A receptors and can reverse benzodiazepine effects; it can precipitate seizures or withdrawal in susceptible patients, so it is used selectively.

### CNS-010 — Sedative-hypnotics · Z-drugs · comparison · receptor: GABA-A α1 · difficulty: 1
Which Z-drug is associated with the longest, intermediate and shortest duration, respectively?::Zopiclone is longest, zolpidem is intermediate, and zaleplon is shortest; zaleplon is particularly useful for sleep-onset insomnia.

### CNS-011 — Sleep regulation · Melatonin/orexin · classification · receptor: MT1/MT2 / orexin · difficulty: 1
Which drugs act on the melatonin system, and which are dual orexin receptor antagonists?::Ramelteon and tasimelteon are melatonin-receptor agonists; agomelatine is an MT1/MT2 agonist plus 5-HT2C antagonist. Suvorexant and lemborexant are dual orexin receptor antagonists.

### CNS-012 — Toxicology · Toxic alcohols · pathway · receptor: ADH · difficulty: 2
What is the key metabolic pathway and toxicity of methanol?::Methanol → formaldehyde → formate/formic acid; formate causes optic toxicity with visual loss/blindness and contributes to severe metabolic acidosis and CNS toxicity.

### CNS-013 — Toxicology · Toxic alcohols · pathway · receptor: ADH · difficulty: 2
What is the key metabolic pathway and toxicity of ethylene glycol?::Ethylene glycol → glycolaldehyde → glycolic acid and other metabolites → severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis; oxalate formation produces calcium oxalate crystals and renal injury.

### CNS-014 — Toxicology · Toxic alcohols · treatment · receptor: ADH · difficulty: 1
What are the key pharmacologic treatments for toxic alcohol poisoning?::Fomepizole is a preferred alcohol-dehydrogenase inhibitor when available; ethanol is an alternative. Correct severe acidosis with sodium bicarbonate when indicated, and use hemodialysis when accepted clinical/laboratory severity criteria are met. Folinic acid is an adjunct in methanol poisoning; pyridoxine/thiamine may be used in selected ethylene-glycol cases.

### CNS-015 — Substance-related disorders · Alcohol withdrawal · clinical use · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
Which benzodiazepines are high-yield choices for delirium tremens, and which are preferred when significant liver disease is present?::Diazepam is a classic treatment for severe alcohol withdrawal/DT; lorazepam or oxazepam are preferred when significant liver disease makes oxidative metabolism less desirable.

### CNS-016 — Substance-related disorders · Alcohol-use disorder · comparison · receptor: opioid / aldehyde dehydrogenase · difficulty: 2
Which medications are core pharmacologic options for alcohol-use disorder relapse prevention, and what is the role of disulfiram?::Naltrexone and acamprosate are major relapse-prevention options; disulfiram is a deterrent that causes acetaldehyde accumulation after alcohol ingestion by inhibiting aldehyde dehydrogenase.

### CNS-017 — Opioids · Receptors · comparison · receptor: μ / κ / δ · difficulty: 1
What are the major opioid receptor associations and important effects?::μ: analgesia, euphoria, sedation, respiratory depression, miosis and constipation. κ: analgesia with dysphoria/psychotomimetic effects. δ: analgesic and modulatory effects. Morphine is a full μ agonist.

### CNS-018 — Opioids · μ-receptor effects · mechanism · receptor: μ · difficulty: 1
What high-yield effects should you remember for μ-receptor activation?::Sedation, analgesia, constipation, respiratory depression, euphoria, miosis and nausea/vomiting are classic effects; opioids can also increase biliary sphincter tone and suppress cough.

### CNS-019 — Opioids · Morphine metabolism · pharmacokinetics · receptor: μ · difficulty: 2
Which morphine metabolites are clinically important, and why?::Morphine-6-glucuronide is an active analgesic metabolite and can accumulate in renal impairment; morphine-3-glucuronide is not analgesic and may contribute to neuroexcitatory effects. Both are renally excreted.

### CNS-020 — Opioids · Acute poisoning · toxicology · receptor: μ · difficulty: 1
What are the classic features and immediate treatment of acute opioid poisoning?::Respiratory depression with pinpoint pupils is classic. Immediate priorities are airway/ventilatory support and naloxone; repeated doses or an infusion may be needed with long-acting opioids.

### CNS-021 — Opioids · Opioid-use disorder · maintenance · receptor: μ · difficulty: 1
Which two medications form the core evidence-based opioid agonist treatment for opioid-use disorder?::Methadone and buprenorphine are the core opioid agonist treatment options. Clonidine or lofexidine can reduce autonomic withdrawal symptoms, while naltrexone is used for relapse prevention after detoxification in appropriate patients.

### CNS-022 — Opioids · Loperamide · drug-use · receptor: peripheral μ · difficulty: 1
Why does loperamide treat diarrhoea without producing the typical central opioid effects at therapeutic doses?::It is a peripheral μ-opioid receptor agonist with minimal CNS penetration at recommended doses; it reduces intestinal motility and secretion. Abuse/high doses can still cause serious cardiac toxicity.

### CNS-023 — Opioids · Opioid-induced constipation · drug-use · receptor: μ / PAMORA · difficulty: 1
Which drug class is used specifically for opioid-induced constipation when laxative therapy is inadequate?::Peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonists (PAMORAs), such as naldemedine, naloxegol and methylnaltrexone.

### CNS-024 — Antiseizure drugs · Convulsive status epilepticus · treatment · receptor: GABA-A · difficulty: 1
What is the high-yield pharmacologic sequence for convulsive status epilepticus?::Give a benzodiazepine first (for example IV lorazepam or IM/intranasal/buccal midazolam), followed by an appropriate second-line antiseizure drug such as levetiracetam, fosphenytoin or valproate when seizures persist.

### CNS-025 — Antiseizure drugs · Absence seizures · drug-use · receptor: T-type Ca²⁺ · difficulty: 1
What is the classic drug of choice for typical absence seizures, and what EEG pattern is expected?::Ethosuximide is the classic drug of choice; the characteristic EEG is a 3-Hz spike-and-wave pattern. Valproate or lamotrigine may be used when broader seizure coverage is needed.

### CNS-026 — Antiseizure drugs · Focal and generalized seizures · comparison · receptor: Na⁺ channels · difficulty: 2
What are useful high-yield drug associations for focal seizures and generalized tonic-clonic seizures?::Focal seizures: carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam and phenytoin are important options. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures: lamotrigine, levetiracetam or valproate may be used, with valproate carrying major reproductive-safety limitations.

### CNS-027 — Antiseizure drugs · Infantile spasms · drug-use · receptor: GABA / neurosteroid · difficulty: 2
What are the important drug associations for infantile spasms, especially with tuberous sclerosis?::Hormonal therapy such as ACTH/high-dose corticosteroid therapy is established treatment; vigabatrin is particularly important when infantile spasms occur with tuberous sclerosis complex.

### CNS-028 — Antiseizure drugs · Lennox-Gastaut syndrome · drug-use · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 2
Which antiseizure drugs are high-yield for Lennox-Gastaut syndrome?::Valproate is a classic broad-spectrum option; lamotrigine, topiramate, clobazam and cannabidiol are important additional therapies. Felbamate may be used in selected refractory cases.

### CNS-029 — Antiseizure drugs · Dravet syndrome · drug-use · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 2
What drug associations are important in Dravet syndrome?::Valproate is commonly used; stiripentol is an important syndrome-specific option, and cannabidiol may be used. Treatment is specialist and syndrome-specific, with some sodium-channel blockers potentially worsening seizures.

### CNS-030 — Antiseizure drugs · Pregnancy · safety · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 2
Which antiseizure drug has the major teratogenicity warning, and what general principles apply to antiseizure therapy in pregnancy?::Valproate has a major fetal risk burden and should be avoided when an effective alternative is feasible. Do not stop antiseizure medication abruptly; use the lowest effective regimen and appropriate folate/reproductive counselling.

### CNS-031 — Antiseizure drugs · Phenytoin · pharmacokinetics · receptor: Na⁺ channels · difficulty: 2
Why can small dose changes in phenytoin produce disproportionately large changes in serum concentration?::Phenytoin shows capacity-limited nonlinear (Michaelis–Menten) pharmacokinetics as metabolic pathways approach saturation; it is metabolized mainly by CYP2C9, with CYP2C19 also contributing.

### CNS-032 — Antiseizure drugs · Phenytoin · adverse-effect · receptor: Na⁺ channels · difficulty: 2
What high-yield adverse effects are associated with phenytoin?::Gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, osteomalacia/folate-related effects, megaloblastic anaemia, rash/SJS-TEN risk, ataxia/nystagmus with toxicity, and cardiac toxicity with IV/toxic exposure are key associations.

### CNS-033 — Antiseizure drugs · Carbamazepine · adverse-effect · receptor: Na⁺ channels · difficulty: 2
What are the major high-yield adverse effects and interaction features of carbamazepine?::Diplopia/ataxia, hyponatraemia, hepatotoxicity, aplastic anaemia and serious cutaneous reactions are important. It is a strong hepatic enzyme inducer, can reduce oral-contraceptive concentrations, and undergoes autoinduction.

### CNS-034 — Antiseizure drugs · Oxcarbazepine · comparison · receptor: Na⁺ channels · difficulty: 1
What adverse effect is especially associated with oxcarbazepine, and how does it compare with carbamazepine?::Oxcarbazepine is particularly associated with hyponatraemia. Carbamazepine has a stronger association with serious cutaneous reactions and important HLA-related SJS/TEN risk in susceptible ancestry groups.

### CNS-035 — Antiseizure drugs · Valproate · mechanism · receptor: Na⁺ / T-type Ca²⁺ / GABA · difficulty: 2
Why is valproate considered a broad-spectrum antiseizure drug?::It has multiple mechanisms, including sodium-channel modulation, suppression of T-type calcium currents and enhancement of GABAergic inhibition; this broad mechanism profile gives activity across several seizure syndromes.

### CNS-036 — Antiseizure drugs · Valproate · adverse-effect · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 2
What are the most important adverse effects of valproate, and how is significant hyperammonaemia managed?::Important effects include weight gain, tremor, alopecia, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, thrombocytopenia, reproductive/teratogenic effects and hyperammonaemic encephalopathy. L-carnitine is used in selected severe valproate toxicity/hyperammonaemia cases.

### CNS-037 — Antiseizure drugs · Topiramate · adverse-effect · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 2
What mechanisms and adverse effects are high-yield for topiramate?::It blocks sodium channels, enhances GABAergic inhibition, reduces glutamatergic signalling and inhibits carbonic anhydrase. Important adverse effects include weight loss, nephrolithiasis, metabolic acidosis and acute myopia/secondary angle-closure glaucoma.

### CNS-038 — Antiseizure drugs · Gabapentin/pregabalin · mechanism · receptor: α2δ Ca²⁺ channels · difficulty: 1
What is the key mechanism and major clinical use of gabapentin and pregabalin?::They bind the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels and reduce excitatory neurotransmission. They are widely used for neuropathic pain; pregabalin/gabapentin are high-yield options for post-herpetic neuralgia and diabetic neuropathy.

### CNS-039 — Antiseizure drugs · Ethosuximide · mechanism · receptor: T-type Ca²⁺ · difficulty: 1
How does ethosuximide work, and why is it useful in absence seizures?::It suppresses thalamic T-type calcium currents, which are central to the oscillatory activity underlying typical absence seizures. It does not provide broad protection against focal or generalized tonic-clonic seizures.

### CNS-040 — Antiseizure drugs · Lacosamide · mechanism · receptor: Na⁺ channels · difficulty: 1
What is the distinctive sodium-channel action of lacosamide?::Lacosamide enhances the slow inactivation of voltage-gated sodium channels.

### CNS-041 — Parkinson disease · Pathophysiology · mechanism · receptor: dopamine · difficulty: 1
What neuroanatomical degeneration causes Parkinson disease, and which toxin is a classic model of parkinsonism?::Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta reduces nigrostriatal dopamine. MPTP is converted to MPP⁺ and selectively damages nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons, producing parkinsonism.

### CNS-042 — Parkinson disease · Levodopa · mechanism · receptor: dopamine · difficulty: 1
Why is levodopa used instead of dopamine for Parkinson disease, and why is it combined with carbidopa/benserazide?::Levodopa crosses the blood-brain barrier whereas dopamine does not. Carbidopa or benserazide inhibits peripheral DOPA decarboxylase, increasing CNS delivery of levodopa and reducing peripheral adverse effects.

### CNS-043 — Parkinson disease · Levodopa · adverse-effect · receptor: dopamine · difficulty: 2
What are the major long-term central complications of levodopa therapy and how can OFF episodes be treated?::Dyskinesias, hallucinations/psychosis and motor fluctuations such as the on-off phenomenon are important. MAO-B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors and other adjuncts can reduce OFF time; apomorphine is a rapid rescue option for OFF episodes.

### CNS-044 — Parkinson disease · MAO-B/COMT inhibitors · comparison · receptor: dopamine · difficulty: 2
How do MAO-B inhibitors and COMT inhibitors help patients receiving levodopa?::MAO-B inhibitors such as selegiline, rasagiline and safinamide reduce dopamine breakdown. COMT inhibitors such as entacapone, opicapone and tolcapone prolong levodopa/dopamine effects and can reduce OFF time; tolcapone is limited by hepatotoxicity.

### CNS-045 — Parkinson disease · Amantadine · drug-use · receptor: NMDA · difficulty: 1
What is the important Parkinson indication and adverse effect of amantadine?::Amantadine is particularly useful for levodopa-induced dyskinesia; livedo reticularis is a classic adverse effect. It also has NMDA-antagonist activity.

### CNS-046 — Movement disorders · Dopamine agonists · comparison · receptor: D2/D3 · difficulty: 1
Which dopamine agonists and formulations are high-yield in Parkinson disease, and what is apomorphine used for?::Pramipexole and ropinirole are oral; rotigotine is transdermal; apomorphine is injectable. Apomorphine is used as a rapid rescue treatment for OFF episodes. Dopamine agonists can cause somnolence, hallucinations, hypotension and impulse-control disorders.

### CNS-047 — Movement disorders · Wilson disease · drug-use · receptor: copper · difficulty: 1
What are the main pharmacologic treatments for Wilson disease?::Zinc reduces intestinal copper absorption; copper chelators such as penicillamine or trientine promote copper removal. Treatment is guided by disease severity and specialist monitoring.

### CNS-048 — Multiple sclerosis / rare CNS disorders · MS and newer CNS drugs · association · receptor: immune / multiple · difficulty: 2
What are the key exam associations for natalizumab, ocrelizumab, trofinetide, omaveloxolone and lonafarnib?::Natalizumab carries a major PML risk from JC polyomavirus. Ocrelizumab is an anti-CD20 therapy used for relapsing MS and primary progressive MS in appropriate adults. Trofinetide is used for Rett syndrome; omaveloxolone for Friedreich ataxia; lonafarnib for Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome in eligible patients.

### CNS-049 — Antipsychotics · Typical vs atypical · comparison · receptor: D2 / 5-HT2A · difficulty: 1
What is the core receptor difference between typical and atypical antipsychotics?::Typical antipsychotics mainly antagonize D2 receptors. Many atypical antipsychotics combine D2 modulation/blockade with prominent 5-HT2A antagonism, although individual receptor profiles vary.

### CNS-050 — Antipsychotics · Drug associations · drug-use · receptor: D2 · difficulty: 1
Which antipsychotic is classically associated with treatment of intractable hiccups, and which with Parkinson disease psychosis?::Chlorpromazine is a classic treatment for intractable hiccups. Pimavanserin is a 5-HT2A inverse agonist used for Parkinson disease psychosis without primary D2 blockade.

### CNS-051 — Antipsychotics · Xanomeline · newer drug · receptor: muscarinic · difficulty: 2
What is the important modern pharmacology point about xanomeline in schizophrenia treatment?::The approved product is xanomeline plus trospium (Cobenfy), a muscarinic-based antipsychotic combination. Xanomeline provides central muscarinic agonism, while trospium reduces peripheral muscarinic adverse effects.

### CNS-052 — Antipsychotics · Clozapine · drug-use · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 1
When is clozapine particularly important, and what serious adverse effects must be remembered?::It is the standard evidence-based drug for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and has an anti-suicidal role in selected patients. Major risks include severe neutropenia/agranulocytosis, seizures, myocarditis, metabolic effects and severe constipation/ileus.

### CNS-053 — Antipsychotics · Extrapyramidal symptoms · comparison · receptor: D2 · difficulty: 2
What are the major extrapyramidal syndromes, their timing and their classic treatments?::Acute dystonia occurs early and is treated with anticholinergics such as benztropine/diphenhydramine. Parkinsonism occurs over weeks; reduce/switch the drug, with amantadine or short-term anticholinergics in selected patients. Akathisia often responds to propranolol. Tardive dyskinesia is treated with VMAT2 inhibitors such as valbenazine or deutetrabenazine.

### CNS-054 — Antipsychotics · Neuroleptic malignant syndrome · emergency · receptor: D2 · difficulty: 2
What are the defining features and treatment of neuroleptic malignant syndrome?::Rigidity, hyperthermia, altered mental status, rhabdomyolysis and autonomic instability are characteristic. Stop the offending dopamine antagonist, provide aggressive supportive care and consider dantrolene and/or bromocriptine in severe cases. Dantrolene is the specific antidotal drug for malignant hyperthermia.

### CNS-055 — Mood stabilizers · Bipolar disorder · treatment · receptor: multiple · difficulty: 1
What is the broad pharmacologic framework for bipolar disorder in the notes?::Acute mania can be treated with lithium, valproate and/or antipsychotics depending on the clinical situation. Lithium is a major maintenance treatment. Antidepressant monotherapy is generally avoided in bipolar I disorder because of the risk of mood switching/destabilization.

### CNS-056 — Lithium · Pharmacokinetics and monitoring · monitoring · receptor: ion · difficulty: 2
What are the key pharmacokinetic facts and monitoring convention for lithium?::Lithium is not metabolized and is almost entirely renally eliminated. A serum sample is typically obtained about 12 hours after the last dose for consistent monitoring; renal function, thyroid function, electrolytes and interacting drugs also require attention.

### CNS-057 — Lithium · Toxicity · toxicology · receptor: ion · difficulty: 2
What are the major risk factors for lithium toxicity, and when is dialysis considered?::Risk rises with dehydration/sodium depletion, renal dysfunction and interacting drugs such as thiazides and NSAIDs; ACE inhibitors/ARBs can also increase levels. Stop lithium and correct volume depletion/support the patient. Hemodialysis is considered for severe toxicity based on the level plus renal function, neurologic/cardiovascular toxicity and predicted clearance rather than a single universal cutoff.

### CNS-058 — Lithium · Adverse effects · adverse-effect · receptor: ADH / thyroid · difficulty: 2
What are the classic chronic adverse effects of lithium?::Fine tremor, GI upset, weight gain, hypothyroidism/goitre and nephrogenic diabetes insipidus with polyuria/polydipsia are high-yield. Lithium enters collecting-duct principal cells and interferes with the vasopressin-regulated pathway; first-trimester exposure is also associated with an increased risk of cardiac malformations.

### CNS-059 — Antidepressants · SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs · comparison · receptor: 5-HT / NE · difficulty: 1
What are the core mechanisms and high-yield adverse effects of SSRIs, and how do TCAs differ?::SSRIs block serotonin reuptake and are first-line drugs for many depressive and anxiety disorders; nausea, sexual dysfunction, hyponatraemia and serotonin syndrome are important adverse effects. TCAs also block norepinephrine/serotonin reuptake but add antimuscarinic, H1, α1 and cardiac sodium-channel effects, making overdose much more dangerous.

### CNS-060 — Antidepressants · MAO inhibitors and atypicals · comparison · receptor: MAO / multiple · difficulty: 2
Which high-yield associations should you remember for MAO inhibitors and selected newer antidepressants?::Irreversible MAO inhibitors such as phenelzine and tranylcypromine have major interaction/washout requirements. Bupropion is an NDRI used for depression and smoking cessation but lowers seizure threshold; trazodone is associated with priapism; mirtazapine is sedating and appetite-increasing; buspirone is a 5-HT1A partial agonist; esketamine is an NMDA antagonist used for specific treatment-resistant/depressive indications, while ketamine itself is primarily an anaesthetic used off-label for depression in specialist settings.
